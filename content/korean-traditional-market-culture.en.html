Korean traditional market where affection and bargaining come and go — Why has the market survived in the mart and online era?

Traditional market, bargaining (enuri), bonus, emotion, Gwangjang market, Tongin market Yeopjeon lunch box — Explainer of Korean market culture for foreigners. Not ‘recommended restaurants’ but ‘why, what, and how things change.’

In the middle of Seoul, between a flashy department store and a 24-hour convenience store, a market that is over 100 years old is still crowded. A place where a merchant says, “Take some of this too,” and puts another handful of green onions into the bag, a stranger sitting next to you offers you a glass of makgeolli, and a grandmother shows you how to bake bindaetteok. Foreigners usually translate this as “traditional market,” but for Koreans, traditional market is not just a shopping place. It is a space of relationships where bargains and bonuses come and go and ‘affection’ flows, and at the same time, it is a place of change between the crisis of disappearance and revival due to being pushed out by hypermarkets and online shopping. This article is not about “which market and which restaurant is delicious” (→ location guide), but explains the meaning, emotion, and changes behind the scenery from the perspective of a foreigner. ⚠️ 3 things foreigners often misunderstand

1. "Traditional market = you have to bargain everywhere" ❌ — Not anymore. In the Korean market, fixed prices have spread rapidly, and in particular, trying to cut food prices is considered rude these days. Bargaining is limited to some items and markets, such as Namdaemun miscellaneous goods and cameras (③ below).

2. "Dumb (giving more for free) = loss-making business" ❌ — Dumb is a sign of benevolence and trust of Korean market merchants. When you buy lettuce, they add an extra handful of green onions. It is not a loss, but “come again next time” is the language of relationships. If you reject them, they actually get upset.

3. "Traditional market = Everyone is dying out due to being crowded out by marts" ❌ — Only half true. According to statistics, customers are decreasing (⑤ below), but the market where people are flocking again is also increasing due to youth malls, SNS, and tourist attractions. ‘Decline’ and ‘resurrection’ are occurring simultaneously (⑥ below).

① What is a traditional market — a space that was the heart of the neighborhood

Traditional market (traditional market) is a permanent or regular market that was the economic and social center of a neighborhood before department stores and large supermarkets were established in Korea.

  • Gwangjang Market is Korea’s first permanent market, opened in 1905, and is a symbolic space with a history of nearly 120 years.
  • After liberation, markets such as Namdaemun and Dongdaemun reached their heyday, and were the heartbeat of the community where merchants bargained, shouted, and talked.
  • The market was not just a place to sell goods. It was a life stage where people interacted with each other, where everything from fish and silk to local news was exchanged.

In other words, visiting a traditional market is not just about shopping, it is about looking into how Koreans eat, bargain, and share emotions.

② Food/Street Food — The moment when the market shines the brightest

The first attraction of a traditional market for foreigners is definitely food. In particular, Gwangjang Market is considered a place where you can taste the most traditional Korean food in one place, with over 5,000 stores and tens of thousands of visitors a day.

  • Bindaetteok (mung bean pancake): Gwangjang Market's representative menu made by grinding mung beans and adding pork, bean sprouts, and kimchi.
  • Drug Gimbap: Bite-sized mini kimbap nicknamed “drug (so delicious you’ll get addicted to it).” A simple version with only carrots, pickled radish, and spinach.
  • Yukhoe, kalguksu, tteokbokki, croquettes — every market has its own signature dish (Mangwon Market is known for dak-gangjeong, croquettes, and kalguksu).

Street food is right in front of you — point, and you've ordered. Sitting shoulder to shoulder with strangers at a long table, being offered a glass of makgeolli by the person next to you, is itself part of Korean market culture.

③ Bargaining (heungjeong / 흥정), Discounts, and Deom (덤) — A Fading Custom, Yet 'Warmth' Survives ★

Bargaining is the first thing that comes to mind with a traditional market. But the reality differs from what foreigners imagine.

  • Bargaining is on the decline. A custom that was once widespread has gradually given way to fixed pricing (jeongchalje). In markets where fixed pricing has taken hold, haggling down the price the way people used to is no longer easy.
  • It varies by item and by market. Manufactured goods, fish, and meat tend to carry a fixed price tag, but items sold by eye rather than by weight, like vegetables, still leave room for bargaining and goodwill. Namdaemun Market has the strongest bargaining culture of all, in clothing, general goods, and cameras — market merchants (sijang sangin / 시장 상인) there sometimes deliberately quote a high price expecting to be talked down.
  • Don't haggle over food. Vegetables and general goods are one thing, but trying to bargain down food prices is considered rude today. Street food and restaurant menus are paid at the listed price.
  • Deom (덤), the free little extra, has survived. Even as fixed pricing spreads, the merchant's discretionary "a little extra" still lives on in the markets — an extra handful of green onions with your lettuce, one more ladle of broth with your tteokbokki. Instead of haggling down the price, the balance has shifted toward trading jeong through deom.

In short: "Always haggle" is an outdated image. Today it depends on the item — food is fixed-price, and deom is a matter of goodwill. For foreigners, gratefully accepting a deom fits the market better than pushing hard for a discount.

④ Jeong (정) and Merchant Warmth — A Relationship, Not a Transaction ★

What sets a traditional market decisively apart from a supermarket is 'jeong'.

  • Buy lettuce and get an extra handful of green onions or a spoonful of seasoning thrown in for free — this deom is not simple service but a sign of goodwill and trust. The moment a customer accepts it, they step out of a transaction and into a web of relationships.
  • That's why market regulars (dangol / 단골) keep coming back to the same stall (gapan / 가판), saying "the ajumma here always looks after me." They go there for the person, not the price.
  • For foreigners, a deom can feel awkward at first. But turning one down is a bit like refusing a hug. A small bow, a smile, and a hearty "gamsahamnida (thank you)" let you fully take in the warmth of a culture that believes food is meant to be shared.

This 'jeong' isn't a sentiment unique to Korean markets — it's a way of relating that runs through Korean society as a whole (→ for deeper context, see #1135 on jeong culture).

⑤ Hypermarkets and Online vs. Traditional Markets — Where Did the Customers Go? ★

The biggest challenge facing traditional markets is a shift in the retail structure itself.

  • The customer decline is real. Reports say traditional market customers dropped by roughly 31% over the past five years. Online shopping overtook hypermarkets as the top retail channel in 2015, and by 2020 it had surpassed hypermarkets in sales as well.
  • The real competitor has changed. It used to be "hypermarket vs. traditional market"; now it's "online vs. offline." That's why customers don't switch to traditional markets even when hypermarkets close for their mandatory holiday — that day, they go not to a mart but to online platforms like Coupang.
  • Consumption has split across channels. People now use online for daily necessities, hypermarkets for bulk buying, and traditional markets for fresh food. The niche where markets have survived is mainly fresh food, dining, and experience.

A caveat: saying "traditional markets are dying" overstates it. More precisely, they're "losing ground as everyday-necessity markets while being reshaped into spaces for food, experience, and tourism."

⑥ Change — Crisis and Revival at Once ★

The current state of traditional markets isn't heading in a single direction — two currents overlap.

On the crisis side

  • Having ceded everyday spending to online shopping and dawn delivery, markets' standing as general-necessity retailers keeps weakening.
  • Government support for youth malls also had limited early results — in many cases, freshly renovated storefronts and support for young entrepreneurs alone weren't enough to draw customers.

On the revival side

  • Shift toward experience: Tongin Market introduced its yeopjeon lunch box in 2012. Visitors exchange money for old-style brass coins (yeopjeon) and go around the market filling a tray with side dishes one by one — a DIY lunch that has become a popular experience for foreigners.
  • Turning retro and cultural: Gyeongdong Market converted a 1960s-era theater into "Gyeongdong 1960 Starbucks" (a Starbucks Community Store) and paired it with a youth mall and night market, transforming itself into a hotspot for the MZ generation. It's now common to see twenty-somethings lining up in a herbal-medicine market.
  • Social media and tourism: More markets, like Mangwon Market, are drawing foreign and young visitors as their local atmosphere spreads through Instagram and travel content. Official channels like VisitKorea also actively promote traditional markets as tourism content.

A balanced view: not every market is being revived. Markets with an "irreplaceable appeal" — food, experience, retro charm — survive, while plain necessity markets keep struggling.

⑦ A Practical Guide for Foreigners — How to Enjoy the Market

Hours and prices vary by stall and season — check before you visit.

  • Bring cash. Gwangjang and Namdaemun run almost entirely on cash (Myeongdong and Hongdae are more card-friendly). Carrying around 50,000 won in small bills covers every stall smoothly. Namdaemun's camera stores sometimes quote a cheaper price for cash (to dodge card fees and tax).
  • Bargain selectively. You can give it a light try on general goods and clothing, but never haggle over food prices. Gratefully accepting a deom is more welcome than pushing hard for a discount.
  • Order with your fingers. The merchants' English is limited, but they are friendly. The food is in front of you, so pointing is all you need. Many stalls have multilingual menus.
  • Combined seating is standard. You eat shoulder to shoulder with strangers at a long table. A glass of makgeolli offered by the person next to you is also part of the market culture.
  • Aim for experiences. Rather than simple shopping, experiential types such as Tongin Yeopjeon Lunchbox (DIY) and Gyeongdong 1960 Starbucks (Retro) have higher satisfaction among foreigners.
  • 1 to 2 hours is enough. If you want to try several stalls, 2 hours is plenty.

❓ Foreigners Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • Is it necessary to bargain in a traditional market? — No. The bargaining system has spread widely, and haggling over food prices is considered rude. Haggling is limited to some items such as Namdaemun miscellaneous goods and cameras. Usually, you just pay the asking price and gratefully receive the bonus**.
  • It feels burdensome to give a ‘bonus’. Can I refuse? — It is better to smile and say thank you than to refuse. The bonus is not a loss-making business, but merchant's generosity, so if you refuse, you will be upset.
  • Do you accept cards or only cash? — It varies by market and store. Plaza and Namdaemun are cash-centric, while Myeongdong and Hongdae accept credit cards. It is safer to have some petty cash ready.
  • How ​​do I order if I don't speak English? — You can see the food in front of you, so you can point with your finger. There are many stalls with multilingual menus, and merchants are generally friendly to foreigners.
  • Marts and convenience stores are convenient, so why go to the market? — When it comes to daily necessities, supermarkets and online stores are convenient. However, the market gives other values ​​such as food, sentiment, and experience — it is a place where you go to buy ‘experiences’, not ‘transactions’.
