Korea's Traditional Markets — Where Jeong and Haggling Meet, and How They Survive the Mart & Online Era

> jeontong sijang (전통시장, traditional market) · heungjeong/enuri (흥정/에누리, haggling/price discount) · deom (덤, free extra) · jeong (정) · Gwangjang Market (광장시장) · Tongin Market (통인시장) yeopjeon dosirak (엽전 도시락, brass-coin lunchbox) — a Korean market culture explainer for foreigners. Not "which restaurant is good," but "why, what, and how it's changing."

In the heart of Seoul, between glittering department stores and 24-hour convenience stores, a market over a century old is still bustling. It's a place where a vendor tosses an extra handful of green onions into your bag saying "take this too," where a stranger at the next table offers you a cup of makgeolli (막걸리, unfiltered rice wine), and where a grandmother teaches you how to fry bindae-tteok (빈대떡, mung-bean pancake). Foreigners usually translate this as just a "traditional market," but for Koreans, the jeontong sijang (전통시장, traditional market) is not a mere shopping spot. It is a relational space where haggling and freebies flow and where 'jeong (정)' runs deep, and at the same time a frontline of change, standing between the threat of disappearing and a revival as big-box marts and online shopping push it aside. This article is not "which market, which stall tastes best" (→ that's a place guide), but rather the meaning, the emotion, and the ★change behind that scene, explained at a foreigner's eye level. This is installment #47 of the Korean culture explainer series.

⚠️ 3 Things Foreigners Often Misunderstand

1. "Traditional market = you must haggle everywhere" ❌ — Not anymore. Korean markets have seen fixed pricing (jeongchalje, 정찰제) spread fast, and in particular, trying to bargain down food prices is now considered rude. Haggling is limited to certain items and markets, like sundries and cameras in Namdaemun (남대문) (see ③ below).

2. "deom (덤, the free extra you get) = a losing deal for the seller" ❌ — The deom (덤, free extra) is a Korean market vendor's sign of warmth (insim, 인심, generosity) and trust. Buy some lettuce and they'll add a handful of green onions on top. It's not a loss but a language of relationship meaning "come again next time." Refusing it actually disappoints them.

3. "Traditional markets = all dying out, crushed by marts" ❌ — Only half true. Statistically customers are declining (see ⑤), but thanks to youth malls (cheongnyeon-mol, 청년몰), social media, and tourism, markets that are drawing crowds again are also rising. 'Decline' and 'revival' are happening at the same time (see ⑥).

① What Is a Traditional Market — The Space That Was the Heart of the Neighborhood

The jeontong sijang (전통시장, traditional market) was, before department stores and big-box marts arrived in Korea, the permanent or periodic market that served as the economic and social center of the neighborhood.

  • Gwangjang Market (광장시장) is Korea's first permanent market, opened in 1905, a symbolic space with nearly 120 years of history.
  • After Liberation (1945), markets like Namdaemun (남대문) and Dongdaemun (동대문) hit their heyday, and they were the very pulse of the community — vendors haggling, shouting, sharing stories.
  • The market wasn't just a place that sold goods. From fish and silk to neighborhood news, it was a stage of daily life where person rubbed shoulders with person.

In other words, to look at a traditional market is not simple shopping; it is to peer into how Koreans have eaten, haggled, and shared jeong (정) with one another.

② Food & Street Eats — The Market at Its Brightest

For foreigners, the first charm of a traditional market is unquestionably the food. Gwangjang Market (광장시장) in particular, with over 5,000 stalls and tens of thousands of daily visitors, is cited as the place to taste the most authentic, down-to-earth Korean food all in one spot.

  • bindae-tteok (빈대떡, mung-bean pancake): Ground mung beans fried with pork, bean sprouts, and kimchi — Gwangjang Market's signature dish.
  • mayak-gimbap (마약김밥, "narcotic" mini rice rolls): Bite-sized mini gimbap nicknamed "mayak (마약, narcotic)" because it's addictively good. A simple version with just carrot, pickled radish, and spinach.
  • yukhoe (육회, beef tartare), kal-guksu (칼국수, knife-cut noodles), tteokbokki (떡볶이, spicy rice cakes), croquettes — each market has a different signature menu (Mangwon Market (망원시장) is famous for dak-gangjeong (닭강정), croquettes, and kal-guksu (칼국수)).

Street food is right in front of you, so pointing with a finger is enough to order. The scene itself — sitting shoulder to shoulder with strangers at a long table, being offered makgeolli (막걸리) — is Korean market culture.

③ Haggling, Enuri & Deom — A Fading Scene, Yet a Surviving 'Warmth' ★

Say "traditional market" and you think of heungjeong/enuri (흥정/에누리, haggling/price discount). But the reality differs from a foreigner's imagination.

  • Haggling is on the decline. The once-widespread haggling culture has gradually shifted to fixed pricing (jeongchalje, 정찰제) over time. In markets where fixed pricing has taken hold, it's hard to bargain down prices the way it used to be.
  • It varies by item and market. Manufactured goods, fish, and meat tend to carry price tags (fixed pricing), but items sold by eye rather than by weight, like vegetables, still leave room for haggling and insim (인심, generosity). Namdaemun (남대문) has the thickest haggling culture in clothing, sundries, and cameras, where a vendor may deliberately quote a high price assuming you'll bargain it down.
  • Don't haggle over food prices. Vegetables and sundries aside, bargaining over food prices is considered rude today. For street food and restaurant menus, you pay the set price as is.
  • deom (덤, free extra) survived. Even as fixed pricing spread, the 'deom' that vendors add at their own discretion still remains in the markets. A handful of green onions with your lettuce, an extra ladle of broth with your tteokbokki (떡볶이) — instead of cutting the price through haggling, the center of gravity has shifted toward exchanging jeong (정) through deom (덤).

> In short: "Always haggle" is the old image. Now it's different by item, food is fixed-price, and deom is generosity (insim, 인심). For foreigners, an attitude of gratefully accepting the deom fits the market better than pushy haggling.

④ Jeong (정) & Vendor Generosity — Not a Transaction but a Relationship ★

What decisively sets a traditional market apart from a mart is 'jeong (정)'.

  • The deom (덤, free extra) — a handful of green onions or an extra ladle of seasoning added for free when you buy lettuce — is not mere service but a token of goodwill and trust. The moment you receive it, you, the customer, step not into a transaction but into a web of relationship.
  • That's why market regulars seek out the same shop "because the ajumma (아줌마, middle-aged woman) here always gives me extra." They go for the person, not the price.
  • For foreigners, the deom (덤) is often bewildering. But refusing a deom is a bit like refusing a hug. If you bow your head slightly, smile, and say a big "gamsahamnida (감사합니다, thank you)," you get to enjoy the warmth of that culture — one that believes food is meant to be shared.

This 'jeong' is not a special emotion unique to Korean markets but a way of relating that runs through Korean society as a whole (→ for deeper context, see #1135 Jeong Culture).

⑤ Big-Box Marts & Online vs Traditional Markets — Where Did the Customers Go ★

The biggest challenge for traditional markets is the change in the distribution structure itself.

  • The decline in customers is real. According to reports, traditional market customers fell by about 31% over the past five years. Online shopping overtook big-box marts to become the No. 1 retail channel in 2015, and from 2020 it surpassed marts in sales as well.
  • The real competitor changed. It used to be a 'big-box mart vs traditional market' showdown, but now it's an 'online vs offline' contest. So even when a mart closes for a mandatory holiday, customers don't come to the traditional market — that day, the shopper goes not to the mart but to online services like Coupang (쿠팡).
  • Consumption channels fragmented. People split their spending: daily necessities online, bulk buying at big-box marts, and fresh food at traditional markets. The area where markets survived is mainly fresh produce, food, and experience.

> Hedging: The flat claim that "traditional markets are dying" is overstated. More precisely, it's 'losing ground as a daily-necessities market, but being reshaped into a space for food, experience, and tourism.'

⑥ Change — Crisis and Revival at the Same Time ★

The present of the traditional market isn't a single direction but two currents overlapping.

On the Crisis Side

  • As everyday consumption is handed over to online and dawn delivery, its standing as a general daily-necessities market keeps weakening.
  • Government support for youth malls (cheongnyeon-mol, 청년몰) was also limited in effect at first — in many cases, neatly decorated stalls and startup subsidies for young entrepreneurs alone failed to draw customers.

On the Revival Side

  • Experiential transformation: Tongin Market (통인시장) introduced the yeopjeon dosirak (엽전 도시락, brass-coin lunchbox) in 2012. You exchange your money for old brass coins (yeopjeon, 엽전) and fill a tray compartment by compartment as you tour the market — a DIY lunch that became a popular experience among foreigners.
  • Retro & cultural hotspot: Gyeongdong Market (경동시장) transformed into a hotspot crowded with the MZ (Millennial and Gen Z) generation by combining 'Gyeongdong 1960 Starbucks (경동1960 스타벅스)' (a Starbucks Community Store), set inside a remodeled 1960s theater, with a youth mall and a night market. A scene of twenty-somethings lining up at an herbal-medicine market emerged.
  • Social media & tourism: Markets like Mangwon Market (망원시장), with their local vibe, increasingly draw foreign and young visitors riding Instagram and travel content. Official channels like VisitKorea (비짓코리아) also actively feature traditional markets as tourism content.

> Balance: Not every market revives. Markets with an 'irreplaceable charm' — food, experience, retro — survive, while plain daily-necessities markets keep struggling.

⑦ Markets in Practice for Foreigners — How to Enjoy Them

> Hours and prices vary by shop and season — checking before your visit is recommended.

  • Carry cash. Gwangjang (광장) and Namdaemun (남대문) run almost entirely on cash (the Myeongdong (명동) and Hongdae (홍대) areas are more card-friendly). Prepare about ₩50,000 (~$36) in small bills for smooth dealings at every stall. Namdaemun's camera arcade may quote a cheaper price for cash (dodging card fees and tax).
  • Haggle selectively. You can lightly try with sundries and clothing, but don't bargain over food prices. An attitude of gratefully accepting the deom (덤) is more welcome than pushy haggling.
  • Order by pointing. Vendors' English is limited but they're kind. Since the food is right in front of you, pointing gets the message across. Many stalls also keep multilingual menus.
  • Sharing tables is normal. You'll eat shoulder to shoulder with strangers at long tables. A cup of makgeolli (막걸리) offered by the person next to you is part of market culture too.
  • Aim for the experiences. More than plain shopping, experiential options like the Tongin (통인) yeopjeon dosirak (엽전 도시락) (DIY) and Gyeongdong 1960 Starbucks (경동1960 스타벅스) (retro) score high in foreigner satisfaction.
  • 1–2 hours is enough. To sample several stalls, budgeting 2 hours is plenty.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • Do I have to haggle at traditional markets? — No. Fixed pricing (jeongchalje, 정찰제) has spread widely, and haggling over food prices is considered rude. Haggling is limited to certain items like sundries and cameras in Namdaemun (남대문). Usually, you pay the quoted price and gratefully accept the deom (덤).
  • They're giving me a 'deom' and I feel awkward. Can I refuse? — A smiling thank-you is better than refusing. The deom (덤) isn't a losing deal but the vendor's generosity (insim, 인심), so refusing it actually disappoints them.
  • Do they take cards, or cash only? — It varies by market and shop. Gwangjang (광장) and Namdaemun (남대문) are cash-centered, while the Myeongdong (명동) and Hongdae (홍대) areas take cards well. Preparing small-denomination cash is the safe move.
  • How do I order if English doesn't work? — Since the food is right in front of you, pointing with a finger works. Many stalls keep multilingual menus, and vendors are generally kind to foreigners.
  • Marts and convenience stores are easy — do I really need to go to a market? — For daily necessities, marts and online are easier. But the market offers a different value: food, jeong (정), and experience — it's a place you go to buy an 'experience,' not a 'transaction.'
