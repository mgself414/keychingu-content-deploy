Korean Education & Exam Culture — Why a Nation Stops for One Test (and What It Costs)

> Suneung (수능) (CSAT), Private Education, Hagwon (학원), SKY, Gwageo (과거), Gireogi Appa (기러기 아빠) — An explainer on Korea's educational fervor for foreigners. It's not just about 'a country that studies hard,' but 'why, how, and at what cost.'

Every year on the third Thursday of November, something almost unbelievable happens in Korea. From 13:05 (1:05 PM) for about 35 minutes, all aircraft takeoffs and landings nationwide halt. Planes on the runways at Incheon International Airport (인천국제공항) wait, and aircraft already in the sky circle at an altitude of over 3km. On the same morning, the stock market opens an hour later than usual, and banks also open an hour late. This is to reduce rush-hour traffic and ensure test-takers arrive at their examination sites on time. Police cars and motorcycles transport late test-takers to their venues, and junior students cheer them on at the school gates.

The identity of all this commotion is Suneung (수능), or the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT) — Korea's national university entrance exam. The 35 minutes when planes are stopped is for the English listening comprehension test. This scene, where even a single test noise is controlled, vividly demonstrates how 'serious' Korea is about education. Foreigners usually summarize this as "Koreans study really hard," but behind it lies centuries of history, a survival strategy for a resource-poor nation, and shadows of world-leading pressures. This article doesn't ask "which university is good," but rather explains why Korea has become this way, and its light and shadow, from a foreigner's perspective. This is the 26th installment of the Korean Culture explainer series.

> Key Takeaway: Korea's education fever is neither superstition nor pretense. The centuries-old belief that 'education = almost the only ladder for social mobility' was the engine that propelled a resource-poor nation into a developed country — and is now also its heaviest social burden.

⚠️ 3 Common Misconceptions Foreigners Have

1. "Isn't it just that all of East Asia studies hard?" ❌ — The degree is different. Korea spends 14-20% of its disposable income on private education, ranking overwhelmingly first in the OECD (verification recommended). Stopping planes and delaying stock market openings is unheard of in any other country. This isn't just 'diligence'; it's a phenomenon where the entire social system is reorganized around a single exam.

2. "Korea must be rich, so they invest in education, right?" ❌ — Quite the opposite. Korea was a country with neither natural resources nor vast land, and in the 1950s, devastated by war, it was one of the poorest nations in the world. The desperation that 'people (human capital)' were the only asset to rely on is the root of its education fever. It's closer to the truth to say that Korea became wealthy through education, rather than educating because it was wealthy.

3. "Since it's a country that excels in academics, students must be happy, right?" ❌ — This is the most painful misconception. Korean adolescents rank among the highest in the OECD for academic stress, depression, and suicidal ideation (verification recommended). Just as bright as the light of achievement is the depth of its shadow — this is why this article must cover the ★duality.

① What is Gyoyugyeol (교육열)? — A National Aspiration Beyond Just 'Studying'

Translated into English, it's "education fever" — literally, 'education heat.' It refers to a cultural phenomenon where it's not just about students studying hard, but the entire family and society mobilizing all resources and emotions for children's education.

  • Parental devotion as a core driver: In Korea, children's education is almost a parental 'duty.' Sending children to a good university is considered a parent's greatest achievement and source of pride (→ directly linked to [#1155 Hyo Culture](1155_korean_hyo_culture.md)).
  • 'Mom-led admissions': There's a strong culture where mothers manage their children's hagwon (학원) schedules, information, and mental well-being. The parental role as an 'education manager' is socially expected.
  • ★Gireogi Appa (기러기 아빠) (goose father): A phenomenon where children are sent to English-speaking countries (such as the US, Canada, Australia) for early study abroad, and the father remains alone in Korea, sending tuition fees while separated from his family. This emerged in the mid-1990s, with estimated numbers once reaching hundreds of thousands (verification recommended). It symbolizes extreme dedication, where families endure separation for the sake of education.

In other words, gyoyugyeol (교육열) is not just an individual's effort but a project for the entire family, and furthermore, the way society as a whole operates.

② The Scene on Suneung (수능) Day — A Day When the Nation Stops

Suneung (수능) is a single national exam taken by hundreds of thousands of people across the country on the same day and at the same time every November. As the most critical gateway to university admissions in Korea, Korean society transforms in these ways on that day:

  • ✈ Aircraft Control: During the English listening comprehension test (around 13:00 (1 PM) for about 35 minutes), all aircraft takeoffs and landings nationwide are completely controlled. Only emergency and urgent flights are exceptions, and planes already in flight wait at high altitudes. This signifies a commitment to not allowing even a single listening noise.
  • 📈 Stock Market & Banks Delayed by 1 Hour: The Korea Exchange (한국거래소) delays its usual 09:00 (9 AM) opening to 10:00 (10 AM), and bank operations are also delayed by an hour. This is to reduce rush-hour traffic congestion and prevent test-takers from being late.
  • 🚓 Test-taker Transport Operation: Police cars and motorcycles transport late test-takers to the examination venues. This is a 'regular heartwarming story' that appears in the news every year.
  • 🙌 Cheering Culture: Junior students and parents cheer at the school gates, sharing yeot (엿, traditional Korean taffy, symbolizing "sticking" to the university) and chapssaltteok (찹쌀떡, glutinous rice cakes). In temples and churches, parents pray for their children's success.
  • 🔇 Construction Site Silence: Noise-generating work near examination sites is halted.

To foreigners, this scene is shocking. However, for Koreans, Suneung (수능) day is "the day 12 years of study are decided," and this level of consideration is naturally accepted.

③ Hagwon (학원), Private Education, and Daechi-dong (대치동) — The 'Second School'

To understand Korean education, private education is as important as public education (school).

  • Hagwon (학원): Private cram schools that students attend after regular school hours. As of 2025, there are approximately 85,000 hagwon nationwide, with over 350,000 instructors registered (verification recommended), and about 78% of elementary, middle, and high school students participate in private education. The 'hagwon ppaengppaeng-i' (학원 뺑뺑이), where students attend two or three hagwon after school, is a common daily routine.
  • Scale of Private Education Costs: In 2025, the total private education expenditure reached an all-time high of approximately ₩29 trillion (about $20 billion USD), with an average monthly cost of about ₩600,000 (approximately $440 / ~3,100 RMB / ~66,000 JPY) per participating student (Statistics Korea announcement, verification recommended). High-income households significantly exceed this.
  • ★Daechi-dong (대치동): A dense cluster of hagwon (학원) in Gangnam-gu (강남구), Seoul. It is known as Korea's 'number one hub' for private education, where top-tier university entrance exam specialists gather. Some students in Daechi-dong are known to spend millions of won per month on private education (verification recommended). Recently, 'managed' hagwon that go beyond academics to sell mental and lifestyle management for students have increased, leading to analyses that private education is transforming into an 'anxiety management industry.'

> Foreigner's Note: The scale is different from 'tutoring' in the US or Europe. It's more accurate to view Korean hagwon (학원) as another educational system almost on par with public education.

④ Why Such Intensity? — Confucianism, Gwageo, Social Mobility, and Hyo

Korea's education fever didn't appear overnight. Its roots run deep.

(1) Confucianism and Gwageo (과거): Since the Goryeo (고려) Dynasty, when King Gwangjong (광종) adopted the Chinese-style Gwageo (과거, state examination system) in 958 CE, Korea maintained the tradition of selecting government officials through examinations for about a thousand years, until the Joseon (조선) Dynasty. Passing the Gwageo (과거) was almost the only official path to attaining yangban (양반, the ruling class) status. "Study hard, pass the exam, and bring honor to your family (ipseong-imyeong, 입신양명)" — this paradigm was deeply ingrained in society. It represents 'a long learning of meritocracy' through social advancement via examinations.

(2) A Survival Strategy for a Resource-Poor Nation: In the 20th century, Korea was devastated by war, with no natural resources or capital. The only asset to rely on was 'educated people' (human capital). The government and families invested all their efforts in human capital, and the result was the rapid growth often called the 'Miracle on the Han River (한강의 기적).' Education was the engine of national development.

(3) A Ladder for Social Mobility: The combination of the above two factors ingrained in Koreans the idea that education was almost the only path for 'a dragon to emerge from a stream' (i.e., for someone from a humble background to achieve great success). The belief in good university → good job → social advancement is powerful.

(4) Combined with Hyo (효): A child attending a prestigious university is considered a great act of hyo (효, filial piety) and an honor for the family. As parental devotion and children's achievements became tied together by the emotion of 'hyo,' education fever gained the weight of a moral obligation, not just simple ambition (→ [#1155 Hyo Culture](1155_korean_hyo_culture.md)).

⑤ SKY, Entrance Exam Competition, and N-su (N수) — The Pinnacle of the Landscape

  • SKY: Seoul National University (서울대) (S)·Korea University (고려대) (K)·Yonsei University (연세대) (Y) are the acronyms for the three top universities. In Korea, 'entering SKY' is used as a symbol of success. Competition for popular departments like medicine and dentistry is even fiercer.
  • Hakbeol (학벌) Society: There's a strong perception that which university one attended influences employment, social connections, and even marriage. This makes 'a single entrance exam' feel like it takes 12 years.
  • Jaesu (재수) / N-su (N수): A culture of preparing for an additional year (jaesu, 재수, one more year) or two (sam-su, 삼수, two more years) and retaking the exam if one doesn't get into their desired university or department. The term 'N-susaeng' (N수생), referring to someone who retakes the exam N times, is common in everyday language. It's a frequent choice to invest one or two more years of youth into the exam for a better outcome.
  • Ranking Pressure: The exam system, largely based on relative grading, creates a structure where friends become competitors, intensifying the pressure.

⑥ ★ Duality — We Must See Both the Light and the Shadow

Korea's education fever has produced remarkable achievements: world-class university enrollment rates, an excellent talent pool, and the driving force that propelled a resource-poor nation into a developed country. Korean students consistently rank high in international academic assessments like PISA. This is clearly the light.

However, on the flip side of the same coin, there is a deep shadow. When introducing Korean education to foreigners, omitting this part means seeing only half the picture.

(1) World's Highest Private Education Cost Burden: 14-20% of household disposable income goes to private education (highest in the OECD, verification recommended). There is strong criticism that while education is a ladder for social mobility, it has also become a 'channel for disparity' where wealthier families have an advantage.

(2) Adolescent Mental Health: Korean adolescents are reported to have among the highest rates of academic stress, depression, and suicidal ideation in the OECD (various surveys, verification recommended). The routine of attending hagwon (학원) until dawn and the pressure that 'failure means the end' weigh heavily on young students. It is pointed out that this is not unrelated to Korea's statistic of having the highest suicide rate in the OECD.

(3) Connection to Low Birth Rate: This is the most structural shadow. There are analyses that many prospective parents, after calculating the 'cost of raising one child competitively for education,' conclude, "even one is too much" or "we won't have children." The burden of private education costs is consistently cited as one of the causes of Korea's world-lowest birth rate (verification recommended).

> A Balanced Perspective: Korea's education fever is neither to be 'praised' nor 'condemned.' It is a desperate force that built up a resource-poor nation, and now a challenge for society to figure out how to lighten its burden — seeing both these facets is the way to truly understand Korea.

⑦ Korean Education Through Foreign Eyes — Between Admiration and Concern

  • Admiration: The country with the highest education fever in the world, a living testament to the 'power of education' that transformed it into a developed nation in a short time. Many developing countries benchmark Korean education.
  • Concern: At the same time, foreign media sometimes liken Korean education to a "national emergency" — unhappy students, unrelenting private education costs, and low birth rates.
  • For Travelers: If you travel in Korea and wonder, 'Why are there so many students studying with laptops in every cafe?' or 'Why are the hagwon (학원) streets still bright at 22:00 (10 PM)?', this education culture is the background. Knowing that it's not just simple diligence but a landscape shaped by centuries of history and social structure will make Korea appear different.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) for Foreigners

  • Is Suneung (수능) only held once? What if I fail? — It's held only once a year. If the desired results aren't achieved, it's common to prepare for another year and retake the exam (jaesu, 재수). This makes the pressure extremely high.
  • Do planes really stop? — Yes. During the English listening comprehension test (approximately 35 minutes), all aircraft takeoffs and landings nationwide are controlled. It's a unique spectacle repeated every year in Korea.
  • How are hagwon (학원) different from schools? — Schools are public education, while hagwon (학원) are private cram schools that students attend after school. Most Korean students study late into the night at hagwon after their regular school day. They are almost like 'second schools.'
  • Is SKY really that important? — In Korea, there's a strong perception that 'which university' one attends significantly impacts employment and social recognition, so entering the top-tier SKY universities holds great symbolic importance. However, recently, social reflection on the omnipotence of academic background is also growing.
  • Are Korean students happy? — Honestly, indicators of stress and depression due to academic pressure are among the highest in the OECD (verification recommended). The reality of Korean education is that the light of achievement and the shadow of mental health coexist.
