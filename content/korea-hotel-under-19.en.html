Can 18- and 19-Year-Olds Stay at a Korean Hotel? — The No-Panic Manual: Juvenile Protection Act Rules, Guardian Consent Forms, and Which Properties Actually Say Yes

Subtitle: In Japan, 18 is the age of adulthood — but Korean lodging rules run on age 19. A 3-second birth-year chart, the 5 things every consent form needs, real policies by accommodation type, and a pre-booking checklist

🎯 Why This Manual

On Japanese travel forums, questions like "Can two high schoolers book a hotel in Seoul for a concert?", "I'm 18 — am I still a minor in Korea?", and "What about siblings traveling together?" have kept coming up nonstop from 2024 through September 2026 (a single thread has over 2,000 views). The answer doesn't fit in one line — Korea draws a hard line between what the law actually bans (opposite-sex minors sharing a room) and what's left to each property's discretion (a minor staying alone), and policy varies property by property. This manual maps out only the paths that work. There are no tricks for getting around the rules — and none are given here.

★ The 3-Second Verdict — It's All About Birth Year

Under Korea's Juvenile Protection Act (청소년보호법), the law defines a "minor" as anyone under 19 — except that the exemption ends on January 1 of the year they turn 19. In other words, adult status starts not on your birthday, but on January 1 of that year.

If You're Traveling in 2026 Verdict Check-In
Born in 2007 or earlier (2007 is the birth year that turns 19 in 2026) Not a minor Standard check-in. Passport only
Born in 2008 or later A minor With a guardian, or a guardian consent form (보호자 동의서) (varies by property) · Sharing a room with an opposite-sex friend is not allowed
  • Japan lowered its age of adulthood to 18 in April 2022, but Korean lodging follows Korean law. Even if you're an adult in Japan, if you were born in 2008, you're a minor in Korea.
  • The verdict is based on the birth date on your passport. You don't need to count your age — just check the birth year in the table above (traveling in 2027 shifts every year forward by one).

⚖ The Law, in Two Lines

1. Opposite-sex cohabitation (이성 혼숙) is banned — Under Article 30 of the Juvenile Protection Act (청소년보호법 제30조), any business or venue that lets minors of different sexes share sleeping quarters is prohibited from doing so, and it's the property, not the minors, that gets penalized (imprisonment or a fine, plus an administrative surcharge). That's why properties check so strictly. Traveling with a parent removes the issue entirely.

2. A minor's contract — Under the Civil Act (민법), a legal act by a minor (including a lodging contract and payment) isn't finalized without consent from a legal representative (법정대리인). This is the legal basis for why properties ask for a consent form.

A group of minors of the same sex, or siblings, staying alone isn't something the law bans. But whether to accept them is up to each property, so many say no — and the ones that say yes ask for a consent form.

📝 5 Things Every Guardian Consent Form Needs

Combine the Toyoko Inn and WeHome templates and these are the items they share. If the property has its own official form, use that; if not, put together a document covering the items below and email it ahead of time to confirm they'll accept it.

1. Guardian's (parent or legal custodian's) handwritten signature + relationship to the guest

2. Copy of the guardian's ID (passport or driver's license — part of the ID number can be redacted)

3. Length of stay, property name, and number of guests in the room

4. Guardian's contact number (one the property can actually call to confirm — must be reachable internationally)

5. For foreign nationals, proof of family relationship (copies of both the guest's and guardian's passports, plus a family relation certificate (가족관계증명서) if needed)

  • Whether an English or Japanese version of the official form exists varies by property (this guide could not confirm this across the board). Many properties accept a Korean-language form annotated in English, so asking first is the fastest route.

⚠ 4 Traps to Avoid

1. "I'm an adult in Japan" doesn't count — the verdict runs on Korean law and birth year. The table above is the final word.

2. It's not your birthday that matters — adult status starts January 1 of the year you turn 19. Being born in December isn't a disadvantage, and being born in January isn't an advantage.

3. A confirmed app booking ≠ a successful check-in — Yanolja, Yeogieotae, and Airbnb don't block bookings based on age. If you're turned away at the check-in counter after showing your passport, refunds are slow and you're left with nowhere to sleep. Confirming directly with the property before booking is the only real fix.

4. Assume ID checks happen even with self-check-in kiosks — don't show up assuming there won't be a check. And opposite-sex cohabitation is illegal regardless of whether anyone checks.

⚙ 3 FAQs

1. Can two sisters (ages 17 and 15) travel together? — It's not something the law bans (same sex). But since it's up to each property, big hotels reject this often — pick one that has an actual consent-form process, like Toyoko Inn or WeHome. If someone in the group is 13 or under, some properties won't allow staying alone at all.

2. What if a parent comes along? — No problem at all. Sharing a room with a parent means a standard check-in.

3. I'm 19 but heard I'm a minor in Korea — Check your birth year. As of 2026, anyone born in 2007 is treated as an adult. Born in 2008, you're a minor.
