Same Brand, Different Formula — Korea-Japan Cosmetic Regulation Grammar and How to Read Ingredient Labels

Subtitle: "The product I used in Japan — why does the Korean version have different ingredients?" It's not a coincidence, it's a difference in regulatory systems. How Japan's quasi-drug approval system differs from Korea's functional cosmetics notification system, and what to check before you buy. The ingredient-literacy edition of K-beauty shopping.

ISSUE · #1335 · 2026-08-29

🎯 The Frame — That Question Is Exactly Right

A question keeps coming up in Japanese traveler communities: "It's the same product, but the ingredient list on the Japan version I bought at Donki and the Korean version are different." The short answer: they can differ, and it's not the maker's whim — it's a structural difference between the two countries' regulatory systems. Once you know this grammar, you'll read ingredient labels differently at the Olive Young (올리브영) shelf (#47).

① The Two Countries' Regulatory Grammar — Approval System vs. Notification System

🇯🇵 Japan 🇰🇷 Korea
Framework Cosmetics / Quasi-drug (医薬部外品) — two tiers Cosmetics / Functional cosmetics (기능성화장품) — two tiers
How it works Whitening and other "active ingredients" can only be formulated once approved as a quasi-drug — ingredients and concentrations are managed via an approval list Whitening, anti-wrinkle, etc. can be labeled "functional cosmetics" once they meet the ingredients and conditions notified by the MFDS (식약처)
Result Types and concentrations of active ingredients are conservatively controlled Meeting the notified-ingredient standard (e.g., niacinamide ≥2% for whitening, adenosine 0.04% for wrinkle care) allows the label — freedom for other ingredient combinations is relatively higher
Gut feel "Products with strong actives are rare" "Complex formulas and high-concentration claims are common"
  • The classic example is tranexamic acid: in Japan it's an approved active ingredient that can only go into quasi-drugs, so it can't be added to regular cosmetics. That's why even products in the same line can carry a different formula depending on the market.
  • In Korea, by contrast, shelves carry a much wider range of products built around retinol and complex actives — not because regulation is looser, but because the management approach differs.

② So What Does This Mean for Travelers — Three Sentences

1. Some formulas really do only exist in Korea — that's part of what makes K-beauty shopping fun, and why Japanese travelers buy the Korean version to take home.

2. "Stronger ingredient" doesn't mean "better" — be cautious with any ingredient or concentration your skin hasn't met before (see Guardrail ④).

3. The longer you've used a brand, the more you need the habit of checking the ingredient list before buying — a familiar name doesn't guarantee a familiar formula.

③ How to Check at the Shelf — 3 Steps

1. Look for the "functional cosmetics" label (기능성화장품) — if the package carries this designation, it means the product meets MFDS-notified standards (it will also state which function — whitening, anti-wrinkle, UV protection, etc.).

2. Cross-check the full ingredient list (INCI) — Korea requires full ingredient labeling, so everything is printed on the back. The surest way to compare with the product you already use is to take a photo of the Japan-version label beforehand and set them side by side.

3. When in doubt, photograph and translate — a translation app's camera reads ingredient names just fine. Large stores like Olive Young usually have testers out, so a patch test on the back of your hand is easy too.

5 Survival Words for the Shelf

Label Meaning
Functional cosmetics (기능성화장품) Marks that MFDS-notified standards are met — the key word of this issue
Whitening / anti-wrinkle (미백 / 주름개선) Type of function claimed
Full ingredients (전성분) The complete ingredient list
Sensitive / mild (민감성 / 저자극) A marketing claim, not a legal definition
Expiration date / after opening (유통기한 / 개봉 후) Use-by labeling

④ 4-Line Guardrail — This Is Not Medical Advice

  • With any active ingredient you're using for the first time (retinol, acids, etc.), start small and infrequent, and stop if your skin reacts — especially while traveling.
  • If you're being treated for a skin condition or just had a procedure, check with your doctor first before trying a new ingredient (for the procedure angle, see #508 and #764).
  • Concentration figures vary by product and by reformulation — this piece only explains the regulatory system; it does not guarantee any individual product's results.
  • Cosmetics are not medicine — the moment you expect a "cure," disappointment and risk arrive together.
