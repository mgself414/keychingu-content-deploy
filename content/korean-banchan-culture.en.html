Korean side dish culture — Why are there so many side dishes in one meal?

Korean table setting, table setting, fermentation, sharing — Explainer of Korean dining table culture for foreigners. Not ‘Where do you eat it?’ but ‘Why and how did it come into existence?’

There is one question that foreigners who eat at a Korean restaurant for the first time ask the most. "Why am I getting a bunch of these small plates even though I didn't order them? This... is it free?" Kimchi, bean sprouts, seasoned spinach, egg rolls, braised potatoes, stir-fried anchovies... We only ordered one main menu, but the table was filled with small dishes. This is banchan culture. This article is not about “which restaurant is delicious” (→ see the cross-link guide below), but explains more than 600 years of history, philosophy, and emotion behind those small plates from a foreigner’s perspective. This is the first part of the 'Korean Culture' series, which takes a deep look into Korean culture.

⚠️ 3 things foreigners often misunderstand

1. "Side dish = appetizer" ❌ — It does not "come out first" like a Western course. They come out at the same time as rice and soup and stay with them until the end of the meal. There is no set eating order.

2. "This is free, right? Charge later?" — In almost all Korean food, rice, and meat restaurants, side dishes are free, and when you finish eating them, they are refilled by saying "Please give me more side dishes." (except for some high-end restaurants and special side dishes).

3. "Banchan = Kimchi" ❌ — Kimchi is only part of side dishes. There are dozens of types of vegetables, stews, stir-fries, pancakes, pickled vegetables, salted fish, seasoned vegetables, etc., and they vary by region, season, and household.

① Why are there so many? — The structure of ‘one table setting’

While Western meals are arranged in chronological order (courses) as appetizer → main → dessert, the traditional Korean dining table consists of rice, soup, and side dishes all served at once. This is called one table setting.

  • Rice is the mainstay (staple), and side dishes are supporting roles that help you eat the rice in a delicious and balanced way.
  • The order of eating is free. A spoonful of soup, a spoonful of rice, a slice of kimchi, and a spoonful of vegetables — go back and forth depending on your taste.
  • So it looks like there are a lot of dishes on the table, but in fact, it is the result of hundreds of years of thinking about "How to eat a bowl of rice more deliciously?".

② History/Origin — From the Three Kingdoms period to the royal court

The roots of side dishes and shared meals date back to the Three Kingdoms Period (57 BC - 668 AD).

  • Influence of Buddhism: When Buddhism was introduced in the middle of the Three Kingdoms period and the royal family refrained from eating meat, vegetable-based cuisine developed. This is why vegetable side dishes such as vegetables and pickled vegetables have emerged as the center of Korean food (also connected to the tradition of temple food — this is a candidate for the next installment of the Korean Culture Series).
  • Joseon Dynasty (1392~1910): The number of side dishes indicated status and rank. The common people listed a few items, the nobility and nobility listed more items, and the royal family posted more than 12 items.
  • Wisdom of storage: In the cold winter climate of the Korean Peninsula, where vegetables are difficult to obtain, the agricultural wisdom of storing vegetables through fermentation and pickling has enriched the side dish culture.

③ Cheop banquet system — table format read by the number of side dishes

During the Joseon Dynasty, the level of table setting was divided by the number of side dish plates. Here, 'cheop' refers to the number of side dishes placed in the tray, **excluding rice, soup, kimchi, stew (jochi), and soy sauce.

Number of concubines status / status Remarks
3 Cheops Common people's daily meal table The most basic
5 Cheops A leisurely family home
7 Cheops Banga ~ Sadaebu It is so formal that there is even a saying, “7 cheops, double measures on a table”
9 volumes Luxurious table setting for noblemen The highest allowed by a nobleman
12 Cheops Surasang — King’s Dining Table The highest rank that only a king could receive
  • Titles also differed depending on status: table for subordinates, jinjisang for adults, and surasang for kings.
  • Remains as a record: In the late 1800s literature 『Siui Jeonseo (是議全書)』, the food composition and arrangement of 9-cheop, 7-cheop, and 5-cheop tables are drawn under the title 'Ban Sang-sikdo'.
  • Tip for foreigners: These days, restaurants don't care about the number of concubines. If you want to experience the formal traditional table setting (including a 12-cheop table setting) for yourself, go to a Korean restaurant/royal cuisine restaurant (→ cross-link below).

④ Why is it so diverse — the philosophy of ‘balance’

The real reason there are so many side dishes is not simply “plenty” but balance.

  • Five Colors: Five colors of red, yellow, green, white, and black — Pursuing harmony in both appearance and nutrition.
  • Five tastes: Balance of five tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter, and spicy.
  • Yaksikdongwon(藥食同源): “Food is medicine” — the idea of ​​taking care of your body with seasonal ingredients.
  • Hospitality and affection for guests: The more side dishes served, the more sincerity was considered. The heart to give at least one more thing to the customer, that is Korea’s affection culture. The fact that restaurants serve generous amounts of side dishes for free is an extension of this sentiment.

⑤ Fermentation — the heart of side dishes

Most of the deep flavor of Korean side dishes comes from fermentation.

  • Four major fermentation axes: Kimchi, Jang (soybean paste, soy sauce, red pepper paste), Saotgal, pickled vegetables.
  • Kimjang: A custom in late fall where entire families and neighbors gather to make large quantities of kimchi to survive the winter. In recognition of its cultural value, it was registered as a UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2013.
  • Jangdokdae: A space where jars (pots) were lined up on one side of the yard and fermented soybean paste. In each house, the ‘house taste of soybean paste’ was the identity of the food.

⑦ Sharing and refilling — affection on the table

The core sentiment of side dish culture is 'sharing'.

  • Shared meal: Side dishes are placed in the center of the table and everyone eats them together. Unlike Western food, where food is served to each person on their own plate, a bond is created by sharing the same bowl.
  • Origin of free refills: Although it was expensive to provide refills of rice in old restaurants, they filled customers by serving relatively inexpensive side dishes. This custom took deep root in Korean dining culture through war, reconstruction, and economic crisis.
  • Side dish store: There are also side dish specialty stores in every neighborhood where you can buy side dishes that are difficult to prepare at home — the solution to ‘home cooking’ for busy modern Koreans.

⑧ Practical experience for foreigners — Enjoying a meal

  • Manner: You can each eat your side dish with chopsticks, but if there is a shared cutlery (serving spoon), it is better to use it. It is not rude to leave a side dish without eating it all (culturally, it is natural).
  • Vegetarian/Halal traveler: There are surprisingly many plant-based side dishes, including vegetables, pickled vegetables, and kimchi. However, jeotgal, fish sauce, and salted shrimp** may be included in kimchi or seasoned vegetables, so be sure to ask “I’m vegetarian” or “Does it contain seafood?”
  • If you are worried about the spiciness: There are many non-spicy side dishes — bean sprouts, spinach sprouts, egg rolls, braised potatoes, braised lotus root, etc. Just avoid spicy kimchi.
  • Refill word: "Please give me more side dishes" (ban-chan deo ju-se-yo). Give more for free.
