Korean Banchan Culture — Why a Korean Meal Comes With So Many Side Dishes

> Han-sang-charim (한상차림) · Cheop Bansang (첩 반상) · Fermentation · Sharing — A Korean dining culture explainer for international visitors. Not "where to eat," but "why and how it came to be."

When foreigners first dine at a Korean restaurant, one question often comes up: "Why are there so many small dishes, even though I didn't order them? Are these... free?" Kimchi (김치), kongnamul (콩나물, bean sprouts), sigeumchi-muchim (시금치무침, seasoned spinach), gyeran-mari (계란말이, rolled omelet), gamja-jorim (감자조림, braised potatoes), myeolchi-bokkeum (멸치볶음, stir-fried anchovies)... You order just one main dish, but the table fills with small plates. This is the banchan (반찬) culture. This article isn't about "which restaurant is delicious" (→ see the cross-link guide below), but rather delves into the over 600 years of history, philosophy, and jeong (정, a unique Korean concept of deep affection and connection) behind those small dishes, explained from a foreigner's perspective. This is the first installment of the 'Korean Culture' series, offering a deeper look into Korean culture.

⚠️ 3 Common Misconceptions by Foreigners

1. "Banchan (반찬) = Appetizer" ❌ — They are not "served first" like a Western course meal. They come out simultaneously with rice and soup and are eaten throughout the meal until it ends. There's no set eating order.

2. "Is this free? Will I be charged later?" — In almost all Korean restaurants, baekban (백반, Korean home-style meals), and gogi-jip (고깃집, Korean BBQ restaurants), banchan (반찬) are free, and you can get refills by simply saying "Banchan deo ju-se-yo (반찬 더 주세요)," meaning 'More side dishes, please' (with the exception of some high-end restaurants or special banchan).

3. "Banchan (반찬) = Kimchi (김치)" ❌ — Kimchi (김치) is only one type of banchan (반찬). There are dozens of varieties, including namul (나물, seasoned vegetables), jorim (조림, braised dishes), bokkeum (볶음, stir-fries), jeon (전, savory pancakes), jangajji (장아찌, pickled vegetables), jeotgal (젓갈, salted fermented seafood), and muchim (무침, seasoned salads), which vary by region, season, and household.

① Why So Many? — The Structure of 'Han-sang-charim'

While Western meals typically follow a chronological order of appetizer → main → dessert, traditional Korean meals, known as han-sang-charim (한상차림), serve rice, soup, and all banchan (반찬) at once.

  • Rice is the center (staple food), and banchan (반찬) are supporting actors that help make the rice delicious and balanced.
  • The eating order is flexible. A spoonful of soup, a bite of rice, a piece of kimchi (김치), then a piece of namul (나물) — you move between them according to your taste.
  • So, while the table may seem crowded with dishes, it's actually the result of centuries of contemplation on "how to make a bowl of rice even more delicious."

② History and Origin — From the Three Kingdoms to the Royal Court

The roots of banchan (반찬) and communal dining can be traced back to the Three Kingdoms period (삼국시대, 57 BCE – 668 CE).

  • Influence of Buddhism: When Buddhism was introduced in the mid-Three Kingdoms period, the royal family began to abstain from meat, leading to the development of vegetable-based dishes. This is the background for namul (나물, seasoned vegetables) and jangajji (장아찌, pickled vegetables) becoming central to Korean cuisine (also connected to the tradition of temple food — a candidate for the next Korean Culture series).
  • Joseon Dynasty (조선시대, 1392–1910): The number of banchan (반찬) directly indicated social status and prestige. Commoners had a few, while yangban (양반, aristocrats) and literati had more, and the royal court served 12 or more.
  • Wisdom of Preservation: In the harsh Korean climate, where fresh vegetables were scarce in winter, the agricultural wisdom of fermenting and pickling vegetables enriched the banchan (반찬) culture.

③ Cheop Bansang (첩 반상) System — Reading the Meal's Prestige by the Number of Banchan

During the Joseon Dynasty (조선시대), the number of banchan (반찬) plates (jaengcheop, 쟁첩) determined the prestige of the meal. Here, 'cheop (첩)' refers to the number of banchan (반찬) served on jaengcheop (쟁첩), excluding rice, soup, kimchi (김치), jjigae (찌개, stew), and small dishes of condiments like ganjang (간장, soy sauce).

Number of Cheop Prestige / Social Status Notes
3 Cheop Commoner's everyday meal Most basic
5 Cheop Well-off ban-ga (반가, noble family)
7 Cheop Ban-ga (반가) to literati Considered highly formal, as in the saying "7-cheop bansang (첩 반상) with double jjigae (찌개)"
9 Cheop Luxurious meal for literati families The highest allowed for yangban (양반)
12 Cheop Surasang (수라상) — The King's Meal The highest prestige, exclusively for the king
  • Titles also varied by status: Bapsang (밥상) for subordinates, Jinji-sang (진지상) for elders, and Surasang (수라상) for the king.
  • Recorded in history: The late 19th-century document 『Siui Jeonseo (시의전서)』 contains a section titled 'Bansangsikdo (반상식도),' which illustrates the food composition and arrangement of 9-cheop, 7-cheop, and 5-cheop bansang (첩 반상).
  • Foreigner Tip: Modern restaurants generally don't adhere to the cheop (첩) system. To experience a formal traditional meal (including the 12-cheop surasang (수라상)), you can visit hanjeongsik-jip (한정식집, traditional Korean full-course restaurants) or gungjung-yori jeonmunjeom (궁중요리 전문점, Korean royal cuisine restaurants) (→ see cross-link below).

④ Why So Diverse? — The Philosophy of 'Balance'

The real reason for the abundance of banchan (반찬) is not merely generosity but balance.

  • Obangsaek (오방색): A harmonious blend of the five traditional Korean colors — red, yellow, green, white, and black — for both visual appeal and nutritional balance.
  • Omi (오미): A balance of the five flavors — sweet, sour, salty, bitter, and spicy.
  • Yaksikdongwon (약식동원): "Food is medicine" — the belief that seasonal ingredients can heal the body.
  • Hospitality and Jeong (정): More banchan (반찬) signified greater sincerity and care. The desire to offer guests even one more dish is the essence of Korea's jeong (정) culture. Restaurants serving generous and free banchan (반찬) are an extension of this sentiment.

⑤ Fermentation — The Heart of Banchan

Most of the deep flavors in Korean banchan (반찬) come from fermentation.

  • Four Pillars of Fermentation: Kimchi (김치), jang (장, fermented sauces like doenjang (된장, soybean paste), ganjang (간장, soy sauce), gochujang (고추장, chili paste)), jeotgal (젓갈, salted fermented seafood), and jangajji (장아찌, pickled vegetables).
  • Kimjang (김장): The tradition of families and neighbors gathering in late autumn to make large quantities of kimchi (김치) for the winter. Its cultural value was recognized, and it was inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2013.
  • Jangdokdae (장독대): A traditional Korean platform in the yard where earthenware jars (dok, 독) were lined up to ferment jang (장) — the 'taste of the house's jang (장)' defined the identity of each home's cooking.

⑦ Sharing and Refills — Jeong (정) on the Dining Table

A core sentiment of banchan (반찬) culture is 'sharing together.'

  • Communal Dining: Banchan (반찬) are placed in the center of the table for everyone to share. Unlike Western dining where each person has their own plate, sharing the same dishes fosters a sense of jeong (정, deep affection and connection).
  • Origin of Free Refills: In old restaurants, rice was expensive to refill, but relatively inexpensive banchan (반찬) were offered more generously to fill guests' stomachs. This custom became deeply ingrained in Korean dining culture through periods of war, reconstruction, and economic hardship.
  • Banchan-gage (반찬가게): There are specialized side dish stores in every neighborhood, where people can buy banchan (반찬) that are too bothersome to make at home — a solution for busy modern Koreans to enjoy 'home-cooked meals.'

⑧ Practical Tips for Foreigners — Enjoying the Meal

  • Etiquette: While it's generally acceptable to pick banchan (반찬) with your own chopsticks, it's better to use serving spoons if provided. It's not rude to leave some banchan (반찬) uneaten (it's culturally natural).
  • Vegetarian/Halal Travelers: There are surprisingly many plant-based banchan (반찬) like namul (나물), jangajji (장아찌), and some kimchi (김치). However, jeotgal (젓갈, salted fermented seafood), aekjeot (액젓, fish sauce), or saeu-jeot (새우젓, salted tiny shrimp) might be used in kimchi (김치) or muchim (무침), so always ask, "Chaesik-ieyo (채식이에요)? (I'm vegetarian)" or "Does it contain seafood?"
  • If you're worried about spiciness: Many banchan (반찬) are not spicy — such as kongnamul (콩나물, bean sprouts), sigeumchi-namul (시금치나물, seasoned spinach), gyeran-mari (계란말이, rolled omelet), gamja-jorim (감자조림, braised potatoes), and yeongeun-jorim (연근조림, braised lotus root). Just avoid spicy kimchi (김치).
  • One phrase for refills: "Banchan deo ju-se-yo (반찬 더 주세요)." They will give you more for free.
