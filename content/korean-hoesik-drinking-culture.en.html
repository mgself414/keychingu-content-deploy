Korean Hoesik (회식) & Drinking Culture — Why Koreans Drink Together (and Why There Are So Many Rules)

Hoesik (회식), Judo (주도), two hands, turning head, 1st/2nd/3rd rounds, Somaek (소맥) — A Korean drinking culture explainer for foreigners. It's not about "where to drink," but "why, what it means, what the etiquette is, and ★how it's changing today."

If you watch K-dramas, you'll often see scenes where office colleagues gather at a Korean BBQ restaurant, clinking soju glasses. Someone receives a glass with both hands, and when drinking in front of a superior, they turn their head slightly to the side. It's never just one round; it often continues from the 1st round (ilcha, 일차) at a BBQ restaurant to the 2nd round (icha, 이차) at a noraebang (노래방, Korean karaoke), and sometimes even a 3rd round (samcha, 삼차). To foreign eyes, this might all be lumped together into an outdated impression that "Koreans are forced to drink a lot." However, what Koreans call Hoesik (회식, company dinner) and Judo (주도, drinking etiquette) is not simply a culture of heavy drinking. It contains a social skill for softening vertical hierarchies and building 'jeong (정),' a unique Korean bond — and above all, this culture is changing very rapidly today. This article isn't about "where to drink" (→ for that, see our bar guides), but rather explains the meaning of Hoesik (회식), essential practical etiquette, and the rapidly changing reality with the MZ Generation (MZ세대), all from a foreigner's perspective. This is Part 9 of our Korean culture explainer series.

⚠️ 3 Common Misconceptions for Foreigners

1. "Korean Hoesik (회식) = Always Forced Heavy Drinking" ❌ — While it's true that a culture of forced drinking once existed, it's rapidly disappearing now. A recent survey showed that only 2.1% of people felt drinking gatherings were 'coercive,' with over 80% responding that it was 'an atmosphere where declining an offer was acceptable.' Since the Workplace Harassment Prevention Act (직장 내 괴롭힘 방지법) in 2019, forced drinking is considered 'harassment' (see ⑥ below).

2. "Korean office workers have Hoesik (회식) every day" ❌ — Hoesik (회식) is usually an event held by a team or company a few times a year. With the 52-hour work week system (주 52시간 근무제) becoming established, large group Hoesik (회식) events themselves have significantly decreased, and 'lunch Hoesik (회식)' or 'alcohol-free Hoesik (회식)' are also on the rise.

3. "You can never refuse alcohol" ❌ — While it used to be a big burden, it's different now. Especially for foreigners, it's much more lenient. There are definitely ways to politely decline (see ⑦ and FAQ below). Accommodating those who 'can't drink' is the modern etiquette.

① What is Hoesik (회식)? — 'Eating Together' as Part of Work

Hoesik (회식) literally means 'gathering (會) to eat (食)'. It refers to a gathering where a team or company eats and drinks together. While similar to 'team dinners' in other countries, in Korea, it has a social function beyond simple dining.

The core idea is this: Korean workplaces have a relatively clear hierarchy (ser-yeol, 서열). In the office, it can be difficult for superiors and subordinates to talk comfortably due to job titles, honorifics, and formality. Hoesik (회식) is a mechanism to temporarily relax that formality. As drinks are exchanged and people sing together at a noraebang (노래방), a boss who is usually difficult to approach might seem a little different. Conversations that would never happen in a meeting room emerge in this setting.

That's why Koreans describe Hoesik (회식) as "a place to build jeong (정)" (for more on 'jeong (정),' a unique Korean bond, see #1135 Jeong (정) Culture). Hoesik (회식) is an extension of work, and at the same time, a social skill that connects people.

② Why Drink 'Together'? — The Logic of Jeong (정) and Relationships

In the West, alcohol is often a 'personal preference.' If you want to drink, you drink, and everyone fills their own glass. The traditional logic of Korean drinking culture is different — alcohol is drunk 'together,' and glasses are filled 'for each other.'

  • I don't fill my own glass (no jajak, 자작). Traditionally, pouring one's own drink, known as jajak (자작), was discouraged. Instead, the person next to you fills your glass, and you fill theirs as a basic custom. The act of exchanging glasses itself is a relational signal that says, "I care for you."
  • If someone's glass is empty, you fill it. It's considered thoughtful to observe if others' glasses are empty and to fill them.

In other words, Hoesik (회식) drinking etiquette isn't about 'how to drink' but 'how to care for each other.' Pouring with two hands, attending to superiors first, and checking on glasses — all these actions are ultimately gestures that express relationships. (However, as seen below, some customs like sharing glasses are declining due to hygiene concerns.)

③ Drinking Etiquette ① — Two Hands and Turning Your Head (Must Know)

If a foreigner knows just two things about Korean drinking etiquette, they'll pass 80% of the test. Both are manners observed in front of superiors (those older or higher in rank), rooted in Confucian respect for hierarchy.

1) Pour with two hands, receive with two hands.

  • When pouring alcohol for a superior — hold the bottle with both hands (or hold with one hand while lightly supporting that arm with the other hand, which is also OK).
  • When a superior is pouring for you — lift your glass with both hands to receive it.
  • Using two hands signifies "I respect you." Among friends or peers, one hand is perfectly fine.

2) When drinking in front of a superior, turn your head to the side.

  • When drinking with someone older or higher in rank, it was considered impolite to drink while looking directly at them.
  • Therefore, you turn your head slightly to the side (usually left) and lightly cover your mouth with your hand or glass as you drink. This is an expression of humility, not directly showing yourself drinking.

These two actions are the very scenes that appear endlessly in K-dramas. When foreigners do this naturally, Koreans almost always say, "Oh, how did you know?" and are delighted.

④ Drinking Etiquette ② — The Cheomjan (첨잔) Taboo and Toasts

Going a bit deeper, Korean drinking culture has a unique taboo called cheomjan (첨잔).

  • Cheomjan (첨잔) = pouring more alcohol into a glass that still has some left. In Korean tradition, this is considered a taboo. The notion is that refilling a glass before it's empty is the way alcohol is offered to the deceased (on a jesa-sang, 제사상, ancestral ritual table).
  • So, the principle is "empty the glass completely before refilling it." If you want to pour for someone, wait until their glass is empty or ask, "Another drink?"
  • (However, this is a traditional norm, and in casual settings today, it's sometimes observed more loosely. It's a unique Korean custom often mentioned in comparison to Japan.)

Toast (Geonbae, 건배) Etiquette: When clinking glasses with a superior, it's considered polite to keep your glass slightly lower than theirs. It's a small gesture, but it's an expression of hierarchy, saying "I elevate you."

⑤ 1st, 2nd, 3rd Rounds — Why Keep Changing Places?

Another characteristic of Korean Hoesik (회식) is the 'round (cha-su, 차수)' culture. It doesn't end in one place but continues by moving to different venues.

  • 1st Round (Ilcha, 일차): Usually at a Korean BBQ restaurant or eatery for both food and drinks. The 'main event' of the Hoesik (회식).
  • 2nd Round (Icha, 이차): Moving to another place for more drinks — a pub, bar, or noraebang (노래방).
  • 3rd Round (Samcha, 삼차): Noraebang (노래방, where you sing with drinks and snacks), pojangmacha (포장마차, street food tent), etc.

Why bother moving? Because as the venue changes, so does the atmosphere and the group dynamic. The formality of the 1st round (ilcha, 일차) loosens up in the 2nd round (icha, 이차), and people grow closer by singing together at the noraebang (노래방). As the rounds progress, only those who are comfortable with each other remain, leading to deeper conversations.

★ But this is also changing. Nowadays, "let's just do the 1st round (ilcha, 일차) and call it a night" is a new virtue. Endless 3rd and 4th rounds are considered an old culture that the MZ Generation (MZ세대) dislikes the most (see ⑥ below).

⑥ ★ The Great Modern Shift — Hoesik (회식) & Drinking Culture is Rapidly Declining

This is the most important section of this article. "Korea = forced heavy drinking" is already an outdated stereotype. In recent years, Korea's Hoesik (회식) and drinking culture has undergone structural changes.

1) 52-hour work week system (주 52시간 근무제) → Significant decrease in group Hoesik (회식). As regular quitting times became established, there was no longer a reason to linger around the office. Frequent group Hoesik (회식) events themselves have greatly reduced.

2) Workplace Harassment Prevention Act (직장 내 괴롭힘 방지법) (2019) → The end of forced drinking. Following this law, forcing someone to drink is considered 'harassment.' As a result, a recent survey showed that only 2.1% of people felt drinking gatherings were 'coercive,' and over 80% responded that it was 'an atmosphere where declining an offer was acceptable' or 'an atmosphere where everyone drinks at their own pace.'

3) MZ Generation (MZ세대) alcohol consumption itself has sharply decreased. The MZ Generation (MZ세대), who prioritize work-life balance (워라밸), individualism, and health management, simply drink less. In 2024, the percentage of young people aged 19-29 who rarely drink or drink once a month or less reached an all-time high of 56.0%. 'Sober life (의식적 절주, conscious moderation of alcohol)' has become a trend.

4) Cracks in the Soju (소주)-centric culture. Among the main alcoholic beverages consumed at gatherings, Soju's (소주) share plummeted by about 35 percentage points in just five years, from 77.1% in 2020 to 42.2% in 2025. Choices have diversified to include highballs, wine, and non-alcoholic options.

5) Changes in Hoesik (회식) formats. Lunch Hoesik (회식), alcohol-free Hoesik (회식), Hoesik (회식) at cafes, performances, or activities, '1st round (ilcha, 일차) only,' and voluntary attendance Hoesik (회식) have increased. Some companies have even eliminated mandatory attendance altogether.

Balancing Act: Hoesik (회식) and drinking culture certainly had its downsides — forced drinking, sexual harassment, and 'gapjil (갑질, abuse of power)' have long been criticized, and that criticism led to the changes above. At the same time, many people value the positive functions of Hoesik (회식) (relationship building, communication). The key is that Korean drinking culture is currently moving from "coercion" to "choice."

⑦ Practical Etiquette for Foreigners — This is All You Need to Know

If you're invited to a Hoesik (회식) or a Korean drinking gathering, you don't need to memorize all the complex etiquette. A few key points will suffice.

  • Remember two hands. Use two hands when pouring and when receiving. When drinking in front of a superior, turn your head slightly. Just doing these two things will make a great impression.
  • Watch others' glasses. Make a gesture to refill the glass of the person next to you if it's empty. "Would you like another drink?"
  • Accept the first drink. It's generally natural to accept the first drink poured by a superior if possible (refusing the first drink is more awkward than refusing the third or fourth).
  • It's okay not to drink — there are ways to decline. See the FAQ below. Reasons like health, driving, or having plans for the next day are respected. Actively participating in the atmosphere (conversation, toasts) instead of drinking will sufficiently convey your willingness to "be together."
  • Koreans are lenient with foreigners. Koreans are almost always impressed just by foreigners attempting the etiquette. Don't feel pressured; it's okay to make mistakes.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) for Foreigners

  • I can't drink at all. How do I refuse? — It's perfectly acceptable to politely decline. Reasons like "I can't drink today due to health reasons," "I have plans tomorrow," or "I have to drive" are respected. Keeping your glass full can also signal that you don't want more. It's also fine to participate in toasts with non-alcoholic drinks or soda.
  • Is it rude to refuse the first drink? — It's usually fine to accept the first drink poured by a superior and just touch it to your lips (or take a small sip). After that, you can comfortably decline further drinks without much issue in today's atmosphere. Refusing is much easier for the third or fourth drink.
  • What if I'm pressured to 'won-shot (원샷)' (bottoms up)? — Pressure to 'won-shot (원샷)' has almost disappeared nowadays. If you feel uncomfortable, you can smile and say, "I'll drink it slowly (I'll drink it in sips)." Foreigners are even less likely to be pressured.
  • Do women have to drink the same amount? — No. Drinking is a personal choice, regardless of gender. If you can't drink, you can politely decline.
  • Do I have to go to Hoesik (회식)? — In the past, it was practically mandatory, but now voluntary attendance is increasing. However, for building relationships, attending at least once can be a good experience for understanding Korean workplace culture.
  • What about jan dolligi (잔 돌리기, sharing a single glass)? — This used to be a symbol of jeong (정), but it has greatly decreased due to hygiene concerns. It has disappeared even faster since COVID-19. If you're offered and feel uncomfortable, you can politely decline without any problem.
