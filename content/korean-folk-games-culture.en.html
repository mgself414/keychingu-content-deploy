Korean Folk Games — Yunnori, Jegichagi & Kite-Flying That Fill the Holidays

Holiday games (yunnori (윷놀이), kite-flying) · street games (jegi (제기), ddakji (딱지), gonggi (공기), marbles) · heritage games (ganggangsullae (강강술래), ssireum (씨름)) · ★the traditional games inside Squid Game — a Korean folk-games explainer for foreigners. Not "how do you play this game," but "why are they clustered around the holidays, what meaning do they carry, and why do they all show up in Squid Game."

In 2021, the whole world watched the same scene. A giant doll sang "mugunghwa kkochi pieotseumnida" (무궁화 꽃이 피었습니다, "the hibiscus has bloomed"), people picked at dalgona (달고나, melted-sugar candy) with a needle, and slammed down ddakji (딱지, folded paper tiles) in an alley. What 〈Squid Game〉 showed wasn't a future dystopia, but the traditional games Korean children have played for decades, at the holidays and in the alleys. To foreigners it felt shockingly new, but to Koreans it was childhood itself.

This article isn't "where can I try these games" (→ place guide), but rather explains why Korean folk games are concentrated around the holidays, what meaning each game carries (prayers for abundance, warding off bad luck, community), and which of them Squid Game spread to the whole world. This is installment #68 of the Korean culture explainer series.

⚠️ 3 Things Foreigners Often Misunderstand

1. "Folk games are museum pieces — nobody plays them anymore." ❌ — Yunnori (윷놀이) is still played by the whole family in the living room on Lunar New Year. Children still play gonggi (공기) and ddakji (딱지) on the school playground. Most of the games Squid Game showed are games that are actually still alive.

2. "Squid Game must have invented these games." ❌ — Red Light Green Light, dalgona (달고나), ddakji (딱지), marbles, and tug-of-war weren't invented by the show — they're pre-existing Korean folk games that it borrowed. The drama merely introduced them to the world.

3. "They're just idle pastimes." ❌ — Korean folk games are deeply tied to sesi pungsok (세시풍속, seasonal customs/rites). First-month games are prayers for a good harvest (gipung (기풍)), kite-flying is warding off bad luck (aengmagi (액막이)), and ganggangsullae (강강술래) is abundance and community — each game has its season and meaning.

① What Are Folk Games — One Body With the Holidays and Seasonal Rites

Korean folk games developed not as something you do alone out of boredom, but as games the community plays together in step with the seasons and the holidays. Games like yunnori (윷놀이) and ssireum (씨름) are said to date from before the Three Kingdoms period. The key is their fusion with sesi pungsok (세시풍속, seasonal rites) — folk games split in meaning by their season.

  • First month / 2nd lunar month (spring) → gipung (기풍, harvest-prayer) rites — games that pray for a good harvest for the year (yunnori (윷놀이), tug-of-war, kite-flying).
  • Summer → growth rites — games praying for crops to grow well. / Autumn → harvest rites — games giving thanks for the harvest (ganggangsullae (강강술래)).

That's why Korean folk games are clustered around the holidays. On Lunar New Year there's yunnori (윷놀이), neolttwigi (널뛰기), and kite-flying; on Chuseok (추석) there's ganggangsullae (강강술래) and ssireum (씨름) — the games were essentially the ritual that filled the holiday (→ see #1136 holidays).

② Holiday Games — Yunnori and Kite-Flying, Time Spent Sitting Together as a Family

Yunnori (윷놀이) is the family game that represents Lunar New Year. You throw four yut sticks carved into half-moon shapes and set the score by the number of faces that land down or up — one face up is do, two is gae, three is geol, four is yut, and all faces down is mo. You move your tokens that many spaces around the board (malpan (말판)), and the side whose tokens all complete one full loop first wins. The rules are simple and the whole family (from kids to elders) can play, so it's still the scene you see most often in the living room over the Lunar New Year holiday. The board and rules differ slightly by region.

Yeon-naligi (연날리기, kite-flying) is a winter game. People mainly fly kites from around the last day of the 12th lunar month through Jeongwol Daeboreum (정월대보름, the first full moon). What makes it special is that it goes beyond simple play to be a ritual for warding off bad luck — you write last year's misfortune and bad events on the kite, and on Daeboreum you cut the string and let it fly far away. Meaning "send off the bad and welcome good fortune" (송액영복, songaek-yeongbok), it's a ritual of new beginnings that releases the year's stale worries into the sky. So once Daeboreum passes, kite-flying season ends too.

Point: Both are family-scale. Yunnori (윷놀이) gathers everyone indoors, and kite-flying sends misfortune into the winter sky. Korean holiday games weigh gathering together more than winning or losing.

③ Street Games — Jegi, Ddakji, Gonggi & Marbles, the Childhood of the Alleys

If holiday games belonged to the family, street (alley) games were the everyday of children. You could play them with just your hands, paper, and pebbles, with no money, so they spread even more widely.

  • Jegichagi (제기차기): Kick a jegi (제기) — a coin wrapped in hanji (한지, Korean paper) with the inside of your foot, and whoever kicks it most wins. It's similar to Western hacky sack, and the jegi was a craft item you made yourself.
  • Ddakji-chigi (딱지치기): Slam your own tile hard against a thickly folded paper tile to flip over your opponent's tile and win it. It's similar to the American 1990s game 'Pog', and it took hold after the Korean War, when tough paper became common.
  • Gonggi (공기): A game of throwing and catching five small stones or pebbles with one hand, similar to Western jacks. You still see it commonly at schools.
  • Gusulchigi (구슬치기, marbles): An alley game of flicking glass marbles with your fingers to hit or win your opponent's marbles.

Point: These games cost almost nothing (paper, stones, coins), so anyone could play — which is why they're a shared childhood memory for most Koreans, and part of why 〈Squid Game〉 struck such a chord.

④ Games That Became Heritage — Ganggangsullae and Ssireum

Some folk games go beyond mere play to be inscribed as UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

  • Ganggangsullae (강강술래): Mainly in the Jeolla region, on the full-moon night of Chuseok (추석, the 15th day of the 8th lunar month), women dress beautifully, join hands, and sing the 'ganggangsullae' refrain while circling in a round. It's a total art form blending song, dance, and music as one, inscribed as UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2009. It traces back roughly 2,000 years to Mahan (마한, an ancient state) and is an autumn harvest game carrying a spirit of cooperation, solidarity, and harmony.
  • Ssireum (씨름): Korean-style traditional wrestling where you grab the satba (샅바, belt) and topple your opponent, the signature contest game of the holidays and Dano (단오). Recognized for the value of training body and mind together and binding the community, it was inscribed as UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2018 — and it carries the historic significance of being the first item ever jointly inscribed by South and North Korea.

Point: Both ganggangsullae (강강술래, harmony) and ssireum (씨름, solidarity) were recognized for the value of 'togetherness'. At the foundation of Korean folk games lies the community.

⑤ ★ Folk Games Inside Squid Game — The World Rediscovers Them

In 2021, 〈Squid Game〉 Season 1 caused a global phenomenon and put the Korean folk games it featured onto the world stage all at once. The important thing is — these games weren't created by the show; they're pre-existing folk games.

Folk games that appeared in Season 1 (2021)

  • Mugunghwa kkochi pieotseumnida (무궁화 꽃이 피었습니다, Red Light, Green Light): A game where you can only move while the "it" person chants "mugunghwa kkochi pieotseumnida" (무궁화 꽃이 피었습니다, "the hibiscus has bloomed"). The mugunghwa (무궁화, rose of Sharon/hibiscus) is Korea's national flower and appears in the national anthem, a symbol of tenacious life that "blooms and blooms again."
  • Dalgona (달고나) candy-cutting: A game of removing a shape (a star, an umbrella) from a flat candy made of melted sugar without cracking it. It started as a common-folk snack from the era after the Korean War when candy was scarce.
  • Ddakji-chigi, marbles & tug-of-war: The ddakji (딱지) seen in ③ above, plus the signature games of alleys and school sports days, appeared as-is.

Season 2 (2024) & Season 3 (2025) — Going Deeper

Season 2 (released December 2024) set a record-breaking mark in Netflix's non-English TV category and introduced even more folk games. Inside a 'six-sided pentathlon', ddakji (딱지), gonggi (공기), top-spinning, and jegichagi (제기차기) were packed in as mini-games (top-spinning = a winter game of whipping a top on the ice to compete for the longest spin). Continuing through Season 3 (June 2025), Squid Game made folk games a common language for the whole world.

Point: The contrast between Squid Game's dystopian setting and the innocent folk games delivered the shock. But to Koreans, those games are nostalgia, not horror — and that gap was the drama's core device (→ for the globalization of K-drama, see #1183).

⑥ Seasonal Rites and Meaning — The Prayer, Warding, and Community in the Games

What sets Korean folk games apart from foreign games is that each game has a season and meaning embedded in it.

  • Prayers for abundance: First-month games (yunnori (윷놀이), tug-of-war) weren't simple amusement but gipung (기풍) rites praying for a good harvest. There was even a belief that the winning side would have a bountiful year.
  • Warding off bad luck (aengmagi (액막이)): Kite-flying is a purification rite where you write down your old misfortune and cut the string to send it flying. The game itself was a rite for shaking off bad energy.
  • Community and harmony: Ganggangsullae (강강술래, hand-joined round dance), ssireum (씨름, village contest), and tug-of-war all require doing it together with many people — through the games, the village became one.
  • The jeong (정) of togetherness: For holiday games, the point itself was the whole family and neighbors sitting in a circle, more than winning or losing (→ #1135 jeong culture).

In other words, Korean folk games are cultural rites in which the calendar, belief, and community melt together. The answer to "why are they clustered around the holidays" lies right here.

⑦ For Foreigners — How You Can Enjoy Them

Folk games shine more when you actually play them than when you watch.

  • Yunnori (윷놀이): With the easiest rules and easy-to-get tools, it's the best entry point for foreigners. You can try it on the spot at hanok (한옥) villages, folk villages, and holiday festivals, and a small yut set is also popular as a souvenir.
  • Jegi (제기), gonggi (공기) & ddakji (딱지): With simple supplies, they're great to make yourself and play right away. You can learn them on the spot at experience booths or with a handful of gonggi stones.
  • Holiday / festival timing: Go during the Lunar New Year (설날) or Chuseok (추석) holidays or to a regional folk festival, and you can actually see yunnori (윷놀이), neolttwigi (널뛰기), and kite-flying. At the holidays, many free experiences open up at old palaces and folk villages.
  • Kite-flying: From winter into early spring, you can fly kites at the Han River or in large parks. It's even more special when you know the meaning of writing down and releasing your misfortune.

Practical tip: If you've "come after watching Squid Game," start with dalgona (달고나) candy-cutting — it's common at cafés and experience venues, and there's real fun in cutting out the star shape without cracking it. Then move on to yunnori (윷놀이) to taste the true atmosphere of a Korean family game.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • Are the games in Squid Game really Korean games? — Yes. Red Light Green Light, dalgona (달고나), ddakji (딱지), marbles, and tug-of-war are all pre-existing folk games. The show didn't invent them; it introduced them to the world.
  • Do Koreans these days still play these games? — Yunnori (윷놀이) is still played by the whole family in the living room on Lunar New Year. Gonggi (공기) and ddakji (딱지) are still played by kids at school. They aren't museum relics but living games.
  • Are the rules of yunnori hard? — No. You throw four yut sticks, move your tokens by the number of faces that land down (do, gae, geol, yut, mo), and the side that completes the loop first wins. You learn it in 5 minutes — it's the easiest entry point for foreigners.
  • Is there a place to try the games myself? — At hanok (한옥) villages, folk villages, holiday festivals, and experience booths at old palaces, you can try yunnori (윷놀이), jegi (제기), kite-flying, and dalgona (달고나) yourself. There are especially many programs over the Lunar New Year (설날) and Chuseok (추석) holidays.
  • Why are ganggangsullae and ssireum special? — Both are UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage. Ganggangsullae (강강술래, 2009) is the women's round dance of Chuseok, and ssireum (씨름, 2018) is Korean-style wrestling and the first item jointly inscribed by South and North Korea.
