Korean Hanok Culture — Madang, Daecheong & Ondol — The House That Lets Nature In

> Hanok (한옥), Madang (마당), Daecheong-maru (대청마루), Sarangchae (사랑채), Cheoma (처마), Baesan-imsu (배산임수) — A Korean traditional house explainer for foreigners. Not "a pretty tiled-roof house," but 'why it was built this way · what it holds · how it lets nature in.'

Walking the alleys of Bukchon (북촌) or the Jeonju Hanok Village (전주 한옥마을), foreigners almost always react the same way — the tiled roof (giwa-jibung (기와지붕)) curving up slightly toward the sky, the lattice doors papered with mulberry paper, and, once inside, a cool wooden floor and an empty courtyard (madang (마당)) sitting right in the middle. Most snap a photo of it as a "pretty traditional Korean house" (hanok (한옥)) and move on. But a hanok is not merely an "old, charming house." From a single room, a single stretch of wooden floor, the line of an eave, down to where the courtyard sits — almost every element is the result of a design that follows Korea's nature, climate, and philosophy of living.

Here is the core idea. A hanok (한옥) is architecture built to withstand the Korean Peninsula's harsh summers and harsh winters within a single house. That is why ondol (온돌), which warms you from the feet up in winter (→ #1141), and a breezy wooden floor (daecheong (대청)) that lets the wind through in summer coexist under one roof. It also doesn't fight nature: it sits in a spot that puts a mountain at its back and faces the water (baesan-imsu (배산임수)), and it is built from natural materials like wood, earth, stone, and hanji (한지) — earning it the name "the breathing house." This article isn't about "which hanok village is prettiest" (→ a place guide), but explains a hanok's spatial composition, philosophy of nature, and why it was built this way at a foreigner's eye level. This is the 65th installment of the Korean Culture explainer series.

> Difference from Ondol (#1141): Ondol (온돌) covers one thing — the 'floor-heating technology that warms the floor.' A hanok covers the entire house, space, and architecture that holds that ondol — the courtyard, the daecheong, the sarangchae, the eaves, and geomancy. The two are the relationship of a part to the whole.

> Key: A hanok's identity isn't the "tiled roof" but the combination of "madang (마당, empty center) + maru (마루, summer) + ondol (온돌, winter) + cheoma (처마, light control)." These four let a single house endure all four seasons of the peninsula (see ②③④ below).

⚠️ 3 Things Foreigners Often Misunderstand

1. "Hanok (한옥) = just a pretty tiled-roof house" ❌ — The tiled roof is only the hanok's outer appearance. Its essence is spatial layout (madang · anchae · sarangchae) and nature-friendly design (cheoma · changho · baesan-imsu). Even among houses with the same tiled roof, it is the relationship between rooms, floors, and courtyard that makes a hanok a hanok.

2. "The madang is just a garden" ❌ — A hanok's madang (마당) is not a Western-style garden filled with flowers and lawn, but a deliberately emptied earthen ground. It reflects light to brighten the rooms, draws in the wind, and serves as a stage where household events like weddings, ancestral rites, and farm work unfold. The 'emptiness' is the function.

3. "A hanok is a cold old house with weak heating" ❌ — A hanok is a house equipped with a seasonal dual system: winter ondol (a warm room) + summer daecheong-maru (a cool wooden floor). Within one house, the winter space and summer space are separated, which actually makes it precisely tuned to the peninsula's climate.

① What Is a Hanok (한옥) — Not a 'House' but a 'Structure That Lets Nature In'

A hanok (韓屋) literally means 'Korea's house' — that is, the Korean traditional dwelling. Its greatest trait is that it never detaches the house from nature.

  • Materials are nature as-is: Pillars and beams are wood, walls are earth (hwangto (황토), ochre clay), the foundation stones and base are stone, and doors and windows are papered with hanji (한지). All are materials that come from nature and return to nature. This is why the hanok is often introduced in English as the "breathing house" — the earthen walls and hanji absorb and release moisture, regulating the indoor air.
  • The boundary between inside and out is blurred: From room → toenmaru (툇마루) → daecheong (대청) → madang (마당) → nature, the indoors flows step by step to the outside. Lift the doors (changho (창호)) and hook them onto the eaves, and the room and courtyard open up as one space. It is not a 'box sealed by walls' but a 'layer connected to nature.'
  • The goal is harmony between people and nature: Every device of the hanok — the angle of the eaves, the placement of the courtyard, the direction of the house — exists to draw in or block light, wind, water, and mountains without fighting them. This is what makes a hanok not a mere building but 'a structure that embodies a philosophy of nature.'

② Spatial Composition — Anchae, Sarangchae, Daecheong & Madang

A hanok, especially a sizable yangban (양반, noble) household, is not one large building but a structure of several quarters (棟, dong) gathered around a courtyard. Each quarter has a different role.

  • Anchae (內舍, inner quarters): The innermost part of the house, the living space of the mistress and other women. The main room, the opposite room, and the kitchen are gathered here — a private zone beyond the gaze of outsiders.
  • Sarangchae (舍廊): The space of the master (man) placed toward the outside, and the area for receiving guests · the study. As Confucian culture took hold, a trend arose to build the sarangchae large and handsome, sometimes with an attached raised floor called a numaru (누마루, 樓마루).
  • Haengnangchae (行廊): The annex space by the main gate. Servants stayed there, or it was used as storage or a stable.
  • Daecheong-maru (大廳): The widest wooden floor, set at the heart of the anchae. It is the hub of circulation and a buffer space linking the main room, the opposite room, and the courtyard. When it gets hot, meals, sleep, and leisure all happen on the daecheong; left empty, it becomes a hall for receiving guests or holding banquets (more in ④ below).
  • Madang (中庭): The empty earthen courtyard ringed by quarters. It can be divided per quarter into an inner courtyard (an-madang (안마당)) and a guest courtyard (sarang-madang (사랑마당)). It is the heart of the house — where grain is dried, weddings and ancestral rites are held, and light and wind come and go.

> The core is 'separation and connection.' Male/female (sarangchae/anchae), inside/outside (room/madang), private/public spaces are divided by quarters and courtyards yet linked by circulation. A single house holds the order and rhythm of Korean society as it was.

③ Natural Materials, Ventilation & Daylight — The Secret of the 'Breathing House'

The reason a hanok is called nature-friendly architecture lies in its materials and the flow of air.

  • Hanji changho (한지 窓戶): A hanok's doors and windows are papered not with glass but with hanji (한지), mulberry-bark paper. Hanji is world-renowned as a tough, neutral paper that insulates better than glass while letting fresh air pass through its fine pores for natural ventilation. In winter it traps warm air and filters light in softly. It is a window that is 'closed yet breathing.'
  • Hwangto earthen walls (황토): Earthen walls absorb moisture when it is humid and release it when it is dry, self-regulating indoor humidity. They stay cool in summer and hold warmth in winter.
  • Ventilation design: The daecheong-maru is open front and back so a cross-breeze (ventilation) passes through. In summer, the temperature gap between the hot air of the courtyard and the cool shaded air behind the house sends a natural wind through the house. It is a structure that cools the heat without air conditioning.
  • Daylight design: The bright earthen ground of the courtyard reflects sunlight, drawing light deep into the rooms. The madang is not a mere empty lot but acts as 'a reflector of light.'

In short, a hanok is a house that governs the climate without machines — through the natural materials of wood, earth, stone, and hanji, and the paths of air and light that link the madang, daecheong, and changho.

④ Ondol + Maru — Winter and Summer in One House

The single most important key to understanding a hanok is that within one house there is a separate 'winter space' and 'summer space.'

Ondol-bang (온돌방) (winter) Daecheong-maru (대청마루) (summer)
Floor Ondol (온돌) that heats stone and earth (→ #1141) Cool wooden floor
Principle Heats the floor (radiant heat) with firebox fire Open front and back so wind passes through (ventilation)
Use Winter sleeping · living Summer dining · rest · receiving guests
Sensation Warm from the feet up Cool from the soles up
  • Ondol (溫突): Korea's own radiant heating that passes the fire's heat beneath the floor to warm the floor. It is the device for surviving the bitter winter cold. (For the working principle, history, and floor-sitting life → see #1141 Ondol Culture.)
  • Daecheong-maru (大廳): A wooden floor raised off the ground so that wind passes underneath, making it cool in summer. When the heat arrives, the family's life shifts from the ondol room to the daecheong.

The Korean Peninsula is hot and humid in summer and long and cold in winter — two extreme climates. Traditional houses in most other countries are usually built for one of the two (cold or heat), but the hanok put ondol (winter) + maru (summer) together in one house, solving both extremes at once. This is the cleverest point of hanok spatial design.

⑤ Baesan-imsu & Geomancy — 'Where' to Place the House

A hanok looks at the site first, before building the house. That principle is geomancy (pungsu (풍수, 風水)), and its core is baesan-imsu (배산임수, 背山臨水).

  • Baesan-imsu (배산임수): Literally 'put a mountain at your back (背山) and face the water (臨水).' A mountain shields the rear and a river or stream flows in front, and ideally the house faces south.
  • Why it's done — rational even by natural science:
  • Mountain behind → blocks the cold northwest wind for winter warmth, and secures water and firewood coming down from the mountain.
  • Water in front → farming and drinking water, plus an open view for excellent daylight and ventilation.
  • South-facing → draws winter sunlight in deep, while the eaves block the summer sun.
  • Myeongdang (명당): In geomancy, such a spot is called a myeongdang (명당, 明堂, an auspicious site). It is not mere superstition but the result of choosing natural conditions good for healthy living — good sun, ventilation, drainage, and wind protection.
  • The angle of the eaves (cheoma (처마)) is an extension of the same philosophy. The length of the eaves is calculated to block the summer sun (which rises high) and admit the winter sun (which rides low). Through the same window, summer brings shade and winter brings sunlight.

In short, a hanok is not the design of a single building but a landscape design that includes 'land, mountain, water, sun, and wind.' It is a way of thinking that 'seats' a house within nature.

⑥ Regional Variation & the Modern Hanok — From Bukchon to Hanok-Stays

① The shape differs by region. Because the Korean Peninsula has a large north–south climate difference, a hanok's floor plan (the shape seen from above) also differs by region.

Region Plan Shape Trait
South (open type) 一 (il)-shape, a single line Rooms and daecheong open in a row. Centered on openness · ventilation against the heat
Central ㄱ (giyeok)-shape, an L A daecheong between the main room and the opposite room. A compromise between south and north
North (closed type) ㅁ (mieum)-shape, a square Buildings enclose the courtyard on all sides. Blocks the cold wind and preserves warmth

> The warmer the south, the more open the 一-shape; the colder the north, the more closed the ㅁ-shape. Even the same hanok stretches out or curls up depending on the climate — its form changes to fit nature.

② In the modern era, the hanok is coming back to life. A hanok is not a relic in a museum.

  • Bukchon Hanok Village (북촌 한옥마을, Seoul): Between Gyeongbokgung Palace (Gyeongbokgung (경복궁)) and Changdeokgung Palace (Changdeokgung (창덕궁)), a dense cluster of urban hanok that survived in the middle of the city. It is a village where actual residents live and a signature walking course.
  • Jeonju Hanok Village (전주 한옥마을) · Andong Hahoe Village (안동 하회마을): Jeonju is a representative hanok cluster of some 700 houses; Andong Hahoe Village, which preserves the form of an old clan village, is a UNESCO World Heritage site (Historic Villages of Korea).
  • Hanok-stay / hanok hotel (한옥스테이): Lodging where you spend a night in a hanok. Sitting on the floor and looking out at the courtyard, then sleeping in an ondol room — this experience is popular with foreign tourists.
  • Sinhanok (新韓屋) · living hanok: A new hanok that keeps the traditional timber structure and the aesthetics of eaves and courtyard, while supplementing insulation, plumbing, and the kitchen with modern technology. It is 'today's hanok,' built in the city.

⑦ A Foreigner's Hanok Experience Guide — How to Enjoy It

> Operating hours and fees vary by venue and season — checking official channels before visiting is recommended.

  • Walking: Bukchon (Seoul) · Jeonju Hanok Village · Andong Hahoe Village are the representative walking spots. Bukchon is a residential village, so it matters to keep quiet and mind your photo manners (early morning is uncrowded and good for photos).
  • Staying a night (hanok-stay): To experience the maru, madang, and ondol firsthand, a hanok guesthouse / hanok hotel is best. The experience of taking off your shoes and sleeping on the floor in an ondol room makes you understand the heart of the hanok with your body.
  • How to look so it goes deeper:
  • Look not at the roof but at the position of the madang and the open direction of the daecheong — the paths of light and wind become visible.
  • Look at the tip of the eaves (cheoma (처마)) — it is calculated at an angle to block the summer sun.
  • Touch the changho (한지 doors) — even closed, the way light seeps through is the very sensation of a hanok.
  • Manners: Residential hanok and traditional houses are often private property. Do not enter freely or peer into windows. Take off your shoes in front of the maru and rooms.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • Are a hanok and ondol the same thing? — No. Ondol (온돌, #1141) is the 'floor-heating technology that warms the floor,' while a hanok (한옥) is the whole house (madang · daecheong · sarangchae · cheoma · geomancy) that includes that ondol. Ondol is one part that makes up a hanok.
  • Why is the madang empty? — It is emptied on purpose. Because it is the stage for light reflection · ventilation · household events (weddings · ancestral rites · farm work). Its purpose differs from a Western lawn garden.
  • Is it cold to sleep in a hanok? — In winter the ondol room is warm, and in summer the daecheong-maru is cool. It is designed so you use the space that suits the season, which actually makes it comfortable.
  • Are all hanok yangban (noble) houses? — No. A large yangban household is divided into several quarters (anchae · sarangchae), but commoners' hanok (thatched houses, small tiled houses) share the same basic principles of maru, ondol, and madang.
  • Where can I see a hanok in Seoul? — Bukchon Hanok Village · Seochon (서촌) are the representative spots. As urban hanok in the city center, they are easy to reach. For deep rural hanok, Jeonju · Andong Hahoe Village.
