Korean Pojangmacha (포장마차) Culture — Why Koreans Drink Soju (소주) Under a Red Street Tent

> Pojangmacha (포장마차), pocha (포차), ya-jang (야장), soju (소주), anju (안주) — a Korean street drinking-spot culture explainer for foreigners. Not "where to go and eat," but "why, what it means, and how it's changing."

If you watch K-dramas, the same scene shows up late at night again and again. Inside a red (orange) tent (천막) lit by amber bulbs, the lead tips a glass of soju (소주) in front of a steaming pot. After a breakup, after getting chewed out at work, when finally opening up to a friend — Koreans habitually head for this tent. Foreigners tend to see it as just a "street food tent," but what Koreans call a pojangmacha (covered-wagon street bar, 포장마차) is no ordinary food stall. It's a commoner's living room (서민의 사랑방) with 70 years of history, a space of jeong (情/정, deep affection) where a bottle of soju (소주) washes away the day's fatigue, and the stage K-dramas love most for confessions. And right now this tent is splitting into two paths — on the streets it's vanishing under crackdowns, while it's being reborn as the MZ Generation's (MZ세대) retro-mood indoor pocha (실내 포차). This article isn't "which pocha tastes best" (→ that's a place guide); it explains the emotion, history, and change packed behind that tent, at a foreigner's eye level. ⚠️ 3 Things Foreigners Often Get Wrong

1. "Pojangmacha (포장마차) = just a street food stall" ❌ — The daytime street-snack carts selling tteokbokki (떡볶이) and hotteok (호떡) are a different flavor from the nighttime pojangmacha (포장마차) where you drink soju (소주). The latter is closer to a small bar where you sit under the tent, order anju (안주), and drink. When a Korean says "let's go to a pocha (포차)," it usually means "let's go grab a drink."

2. "The tent = poor and unhygienic" ❌ — Shabby as it may look, the pojangmacha (포장마차) has long been loved in Korean society as an equal space that ignores class (계층). Office workers, students, and taxi drivers drink shoulder to shoulder under the same tent. That's why dramas drag even wealthy leads to the pocha (포차).

3. "Odeng (오뎅) is Japanese, isn't it?" ❌ — "Odeng (오뎅)" comes from a Japanese word, but the fish-cake broth (어묵 국물) at a pojangmacha (포장마차) has settled in as a signature Korean anju (안주). The piping-hot fish-cake broth you sip from a paper cup on a cold night is the symbol of the pocha (포차). The name may be Japanese-rooted, but the feeling is fully Korean.

① What Is a Pojangmacha (포장마차)? — A 'Cloth-Wrapped Cart'

A pojangmacha (包裝馬車) is, literally, a 'cart (수레) wrapped in cloth (布).' In English, a "covered wagon."

  • Core build: an orange/red tent (천막) + a small wheeled handcart (cooking counter, 손수레) + a few folding plastic chairs + one or two bare bulbs (백열등) lighting the tent.
  • It mostly operates at night — a place for office workers to grab a drink on the way home, to swing by for round two or three after a gathering, or to have a late-night snack at dawn.
  • The menu centers on drinks (soju (소주)/beer) and anju (안주) — from snack-bar fare like tteokbokki (떡볶이) and sundae (순대) to drinking foods like dakbal (닭발) and gopchang (곱창).

For short, it's called pocha (포차). When a Korean asks, "pocha (포차) for a drink?" — it's not an invitation to a pricey bar, but to drink cheaply and comfortably while sharing jeong (정).

② Street Pocha (포차) vs Indoor Pocha (포차) — One Name, Two Spaces

It's easy for foreigners to get confused by the word 'pocha (포차).' Today's Korean pocha (포차) comes in roughly two kinds.

Street Pocha (street stall, 길거리 포차) Indoor Pocha (a shop, 실내 포차)
Form Tent + handcart (mobile, a stall) A bar inside a building (pocha-concept interior)
Mood Raw, warm, spontaneous Clean, trendy, retro-mood
Legal status Many are unlicensed stalls Properly licensed venues
Trend Declining (crackdowns, rent, hygiene) Rising (MZ revival, see ⑥ below)
Foreigner access High language/menu barrier Menus and English support are easy

→ The red tent (천막) you see in dramas is usually a street pocha (포차) (or a set modeled on one). Meanwhile, the place foreigners actually end up visiting today is often an indoor pocha (실내 포차) (a pocha-concept bar). The two share the same emotional roots but offer different experiences.

③ The Menu — Everything That Goes With Soju (소주)

A pojangmacha (포장마차) menu centers on 'food eaten with drinks,' that is, anju (按酒/안주). Here are the signature items, organized to be foreigner-friendly:

Soju (소주) Korea's signature distilled spirit Green bottle, small glass. The star of the pocha (포차)
Eomuk / Odeng (어묵/오뎅) Fish-cake skewers + hot broth The paper-cup broth on a cold night is the pocha (포차) symbol
Udon (우동) A hot noodle soup Popular as a soothing finish after drinking
Dakbal (spicy chicken feet, 닭발) Spicy chicken feet Spicy + chewy. The ultimate soju (소주) anju (안주)
Gopchang / Makchang (곱창/막창) Grilled beef/pork intestines Rich and nutty. A love-it-or-hate-it delicacy

Tteokbokki / Sundae (떡볶이/순대) Snack-bar dishes Sweet-and-spicy. Popular even without alcohol

> Foreigner tip: If it's your first time, start with a mild combo like fish-cake broth + gyeran-mari (계란말이) + tteokbokki (떡볶이). If you handle spice, try dakbal (닭발); if you're adventurous, take on gopchang (곱창). Start your drinks with soju (소주) (strong, small glass) or beer, and somaek (소맥) — soju (소주) mixed with beer — is common too.

④ Why Drink Under a Tent — Commoners, the Commute Home, and Jeong (情/정)

The real meaning of a pojangmacha (포장마차) lies not in the food but in the emotion. Why do Koreans go out of their way to a shabby tent?

  • A commoner's living room (서민의 사랑방): The pocha (포차) began in the poor years of the 1950s–60s as a commoner's space where you could drink cheaply. Precisely because it isn't fancy, anyone can step in comfortably — affordable prices, no formality.
  • Comfort on the commute home: At the end of a hard day, you loosen your tie, sit under the tent, and have a glass of soju (소주). For Korean office workers, the pocha (포차) is a rest stop where you set down the day's fatigue. One foreign outlet even described the pocha (포차) as a "psychiatrist's office (정신과 진료실)" where you drink and pour out your heart.
  • A space for sharing jeong (정): Inside the cramped tent, your shoulder brushes even a stranger's. After a drink or two, Korea's distinctive jeong (정) flows — a place to listen to a friend's worries, lay your heart bare, and offer comfort. (→ For 'jeong (정)' itself, see #1135.)
  • The stage dramas love: That's why K-dramas almost always set the scene where a character opens up their heart at a pocha (포차). A liquor-emboldened confession, crying to a friend, reconciliation — the rawest emotions burst out under this tent.

> Pocha (포차) scenes in K-dramas (works and context recommended to verify): the tipsy confession in Hospital Playlist (Seulgiroun Uisasaenghwal, 슬기로운 의사생활), the soju (소주) confession in Crash Landing on You (Sarangui Bulsichak, 사랑의 불시착), the drowning-sorrows scene in She Was Pretty (Geunyeoneun Yeppeotda, 그녀는 예뻤다), and more — the pocha (포차) is the official stage of 'where true feelings come out' in Korean dramas.

⑤ Its Relationship to Ya-jang (野場/야장) — Outdoor Drinking Without a Tent

A close cousin that's easy to confuse with the pocha (포차) is the now-trending ya-jang (野場/야장). They're alike but not the same.

  • Ya-jang (야장) = 'field (野) + courtyard (場)' — an outdoor drinking spot set up on a street or alley outside a building, with low platforms or tables. It's a ya-jang (야장) even without a tent (포장).
  • Famous hotspots: Euljiro Nogari Alley (Euljiro Nogari Golmok, 을지로 노가리 골목) and the Jongno 3-ga (종로 3가) area. By day they're streets of print shops and metal workshops, but at night the alleys fill with outdoor tables where tie-loosened office workers, college students, and foreign travelers mingle. Euljiro's (을지로) specialty is nogari (dried pollack, 노가리) with draft beer.
  • Relation to the pocha (포차): If a pocha (포차) is a 'mobile tent stall,' a ya-jang (야장) is the broader idea of 'a drinking spot set up outdoors outside a building.' Many venues, like Nogari Alley (노가리 골목), mix indoor and outdoor, so the two often overlap.

> Legal note: Under the current Food Sanitation Act (식품위생법), cooking and operating outside a building is, in principle, illegal. So street pocha (포차) and ya-jang (야장) always carry crackdown and hygiene controversies (see ⑥ below). Foreigners should enjoy the atmosphere but look after their own cash, belongings, and hygiene — recommended to confirm on site.

⑥ ★ The Change — Vanishing on the Streets, Reborn Indoors

Pojangmacha (포장마차) culture is now moving in two opposite directions. This is the most important part of this article.

(A) Street Pocha (포차) — The Dwindling Tent

  • A history of crackdowns: Ahead of the 1988 Seoul Olympics, pocha (포차) became targets of sweeping crackdowns and removals on the grounds that they "ruin the city's appearance." Since then, attitudes toward street stalls grew colder over obstruction of pedestrian rights (보행권), hygiene, and price-gouging (바가지요금) issues.
  • Why they're disappearing: Higher downtown rents and tighter management, hygiene regulations, and above all, public opinion that once defended illegal stalls has itself cooled. There used to be sympathy that "we're persecuting livelihood stalls," but now criticism over pedestrian and hygiene issues is louder. As a result, street-tent pocha (포차) have visibly dwindled.

(B) Indoor Pocha (포차) — A Retro Revival

  • At the same time, the pocha's (포차) sensibility has moved into clean indoor spaces and become newly trendy. The so-called 'indoor pojangmacha (실내 포장마차 / 실내 포차)' — a bar that recreates the pocha (포차) mood in a proper venue instead of a tent.
  • Among the MZ Generation (MZ세대), riding the 'young-tro (영트로)' (young + retro) wave, a mood pocha (감성 포차) dressed in '80s–'90s nostalgia has risen as a hotspot. A 'NEW-PO (뉴포)' concept — old posters, bare bulbs, and disco balls as retro interior, plus clean hygiene and trendy plating — draws people in their 20s and 30s.
  • Why MZ falls for it: a retro mood that consumes an era they never lived as 'hip novelty' + affordable prices + a photogenic atmosphere. It's a way of seeking, in a clean indoor version, the emotion of the real tents that are fading away.

> One-line summary: The real red tents are dwindling, pushed out by crackdowns, rent, and hygiene, while the retro indoor pocha (실내 포차) that mimics their mood is revived as an MZ hotspot. Koreans' longing for the pocha (포차) stays the same — only the vessel that holds it is changing.

⑦ Pocha (포차) in Practice for Foreigners — What's Good to Know Before You Go

> The below are experience tips, not recommendations of specific shops (for places, see a separate guide). Operations and prices vary by venue — recommended to confirm on site.

  • When: Pocha (포차) and ya-jang (야장) are a nighttime culture. Usually open from evening to dawn. Ya-jang (야장) alleys (Euljiro (을지로), Jongno (종로)) are busiest on warm-season evenings.
  • How much: Per person, one bottle of soju (소주) + 1–2 anju (안주) commonly runs ₩15,000–30,000 (about US$11–22). Mood indoor pocha (실내 포차) can cost a bit more for the atmosphere.
  • Payment and language: Traditional street pocha (포차) may take cash only or have Korean-only menus. For a foreigner's first visit, an indoor pocha (실내 포차) with a menu and card payment is the easier bet.
  • Ordering manners: You sit down, order anju (안주), then order drinks. For Korean drinking etiquette (pouring and receiving with two hands, the first glass, etc.) → see #1140. When a glass empties, refilling it is part of jeong (정). A light greeting with strangers seated nearby is also natural.
  • What to eat: A mild start is fish-cake broth, gyeran-mari (계란말이), tteokbokki (떡볶이); for spice, dakbal (닭발); for a delicacy, gopchang (곱창). Drinks: soju (소주), beer, somaek (소맥).
  • Enjoy the mood: The heart of the pocha (포차) is the emotion, not the food. Rather than eating fast and leaving, the real pocha (포차) experience is to tip your glass slowly and feel that warm air.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) for Foreigners

  • What's the difference between a pojangmacha (포장마차) and a regular street food stall? — Unlike the daytime snack stalls (tteokbokki (떡볶이), hotteok (호떡)), a pojangmacha (포장마차) is a small bar where you sit under a tent at night and enjoy drinks and anju (안주). "Let's go to a pocha (포차)" usually means "let's grab a drink."
  • Can I go alone? — Yes. The pocha (포차) is a place where having a solo glass of soju (소주) feels natural. That said, the mood is livelier with two or more. For safety, pace yourself when drinking alone late at night.
  • Is there an English menu? — Traditional street pocha (포차) often have Korean-only menus. English menus and card payment are easier at indoor pocha (실내 포차) or ya-jang (야장) alleys with lots of foreigners. If there's a photo menu, ordering by pointing is OK.
  • Can I skip the alcohol? — Yes. You're welcome even if you only have odeng (어묵), udon (우동), and tteokbokki (떡볶이). That said, the pocha (포차) mood comes alive most when paired with drinks and anju (안주).
  • I'm worried about hygiene. — Street stalls vary widely in hygiene. If you're concerned, choosing a properly licensed indoor pocha (실내 포차) is the clean option. It's also wise to look after your own cash and belongings.
