Korean food stall culture — Why do Koreans drink soju in red tents on the streets?

Pojangmacha, Pocha, Yajang, Soju, Snacks — Explainer of Korean street bar culture for foreigners. Not ‘Where do I go to eat?’ but ‘Why, what does it mean, and how has it changed?’

When watching K-dramas, the same scenes appear every late night. Inside the red (orange) tent lit with orange light bulbs, the protagonist tips his glass of soju in front of a steaming pot. When you break up with someone, break up at work, or confess your true feelings to a friend — Korean people usually head to this tent. Foreigners see this as just a “street food tent,” but the pojangmacha (包裝馬車) that Koreans refer to is not just a street food stall. It is a common people's love room with a 70-year history, a space of affection where you can relieve the day's fatigue with a bottle of soju, and the most loved confession stage in dramas. And now, this tent is splitting into two - on the one hand, it is disappearing from the streets due to crackdowns, and on the other hand, it is being reborn as a retro-style indoor gun carriage of the MZ generation. This article is not about “which pocha is delicious?” (→ location guide), but explains the emotions, history, and changes behind the tent from a foreigner’s perspective. Key line: Pojangmacha is a ‘cheap street bar’ and an ‘emotional space where Koreans reveal their true feelings.’ A place to go for the atmosphere and atmosphere rather than the food.

⚠️ 3 things foreigners often misunderstand

1. "Street food stall = just a street food stall" ❌ — A street snack stall selling tteokbokki and hotteok during the day and a stall selling soju at night are different. The latter is closer to a small bar where you sit inside a tent, order snacks, and drink alcohol. To Koreans, “let’s go to a food stall” usually means “let’s go for a drink.”

2. "Tents = poor and unsanitary" ❌ — Even though they look shabby, food stalls have been loved as equal spaces that do not discriminate between classes in Korean society. Office workers, students, and taxi drivers drink shoulder to shoulder in the same tent. This is why the drama even takes the rich protagonist to the carriage.

3. "Because it's oden (おでん), it's not a Japanese food." ❌ — Although 'oden' is a Japanese word, the fish cake soup at the food stall is a representative Korean-style snack. Hot fish cake soup served in a paper cup on a cold night is the symbol of Pocha. Even though the name is Japanese, the sentiment is completely Korean.

① What is a pojangmacha (cart wrapped in cloth)?

Pojangmacha (包裝馬車) literally means 'a carriage (cart) wrapped in cloth'. If translated into English it would be “covered wagon”.

  • Core components: orange/red tent + small handcart (countertop) on wheels + several folding plastic chairs + one or two incandescent lights to illuminate the inside of the tent.
  • Mainly open at night. A place for office workers to have a drink on their way home from work, a second or third drink after a drink, or a late-night snack in the early morning when hungry.
  • The menu focuses on alcohol (soju, beer) and snacks. From snacks like tteokbokki and sundae to alcoholic snacks like chicken feet and tripe.

It is called Pocha for short. When a Korean says, “Would you like a cup of Pocha?” — it is not an invitation to an expensive bar, but an invitation to have a comfortable and inexpensive drink.

④ 왜 천막에서 마시나 — 서민·퇴근길·정(情)

포장마차의 진짜 의미는 음식이 아니라 정서에 있다. 왜 한국인은 굳이 허름한 천막을 찾을까.

  • 서민의 사랑방: 포차는 1950~60년대 가난하던 시절, 값싸게 한잔할 수 있는 서민의 공간으로 출발했다. 화려하지 않아서 오히려 누구나 편하게 들어간다 — 부담 없는 가격, 격식 없는 분위기.
  • 퇴근길의 위로: 고단한 하루 끝, 넥타이를 풀고 천막에 앉아 소주 한 잔. 포차는 한국 직장인에게 하루의 피로를 내려놓는 정거장이다. 한 외신은 포차를 두고 "술 마시며 속을 털어놓는 정신과 진료실(psychiatrist's office)" 이라고 표현하기도 했다.
  • 정(情)을 나누는 공간: 좁은 천막 안에서는 모르는 사람과도 어깨가 닿는다. 술이 한 잔 들어가면 한국 특유의 정이 흐른다 — 친구의 고민을 들어주고, 진심을 털어놓고, 위로를 건네는 곳. (→ 한국의 '정' 자체는 #1135 참고)
  • 드라마가 사랑하는 무대: 그래서 K-드라마는 등장인물이 마음을 꺼내놓는 장면을 거의 항상 포차에 놓는다. 술기운을 빌린 고백, 친구에게 우는 장면, 화해 — 가장 날것의 감정이 이 천막 안에서 터진다.

K-drama 속 포차 장면 (작품·맥락 확인 권장): 〈슬기로운 의사생활(Hospital Playlist)〉의 취중 고백, 〈사랑의 불시착(Crash Landing on You)〉의 소주 고백, 〈그녀는 예뻤다(She Was Pretty)〉의 홧술 장면 등 — 포차는 한국 드라마에서 '진심이 나오는 자리' 의 공식 무대다.

⑤ 야장(野場)과의 관계 — 천막 없는 야외 술자리

포차와 헷갈리기 쉬운 게 요즘 뜨는 야장(野場) 이다. 둘은 닮았지만 다르다.

  • 야장 = '들 야(野) + 마당 장(場)', 즉 건물 밖 길·골목에 평상이나 테이블을 펴고 마시는 야외 술자리. 천막(포장)이 없어도 야장이다.
  • 대표 성지: 을지로 노가리 골목, 종로 3가 일대. 낮엔 인쇄소·철공소 거리지만 밤이 되면 골목 가득 야외 테이블이 펼쳐지고, 넥타이 푼 직장인·대학생·외국인 여행객이 뒤섞인다. 을지로의 명물은 노가리(말린 명태) 에 생맥주다.
  • 포차와의 관계: 포차가 '천막 친 이동식 노점'이라면, 야장은 '건물 밖 야외에 차린 술자리'라는 더 넓은 개념. 노가리 골목처럼 실내·야외를 겸한 가게도 많아 둘은 자주 겹친다.

법적 메모: 현행 식품위생법상 건물 밖 조리·영업은 원칙적으로 불법이다. 그래서 길거리 포차와 야장은 늘 단속과 위생 논란을 안고 있다(아래 ⑥). 외국인은 분위기를 즐기되, 현금·소지품·위생은 스스로 챙기는 게 좋다.

⑥ ★ 변화 — 길거리에선 사라지고, 실내에선 되살아나고

포장마차 문화는 지금 정반대 두 방향으로 움직이고 있다. 이 글에서 가장 중요한 부분이다.

(A) 길거리 포차 — 줄어드는 천막

  • 단속의 역사: 포차는 1988년 서울올림픽을 앞두고 "도시 미관을 해친다"는 이유로 대대적 단속·철거의 대상이 됐다. 이후로도 보행권 침해·위생·바가지요금 문제로 길거리 노점에 대한 시선은 점점 차가워졌다.
  • 사라지는 이유: 도심 임대료·관리 강화, 위생 규제, 그리고 무엇보다 불법 노점을 옹호하던 여론 자체가 식었다. 과거엔 "생계형 노점을 핍박한다"는 동정이 있었지만, 지금은 보행·위생 문제로 비판이 더 크다. 그 결과 길거리 천막 포차는 눈에 띄게 줄었다.

(B) 실내 포차 — 레트로로 부활

  • 동시에, 포차의 감성은 깔끔한 실내로 옮겨와 새로 유행한다. 이른바 '실내 포장마차(실내 포차)' — 천막 대신 정식 매장에서 포차 분위기를 재현한 술집이다.
  • MZ세대 사이에선 '영트로(young-tro)'(young + retro) 흐름을 타고, 8090년대 감성을 입힌 감성 포차가 핫플로 떴다. 옛 포스터·백열등·디스코볼 같은 레트로 인테리어에, 깨끗한 위생과 트렌디한 플레이팅을 더한 'NEW-PO(뉴포)' 콘셉트가 20·30대를 끌어모은다.
  • Why MZ is missing: Retro sensibility of consuming old times that you haven't experienced in person with 'hip newness' + affordable prices + photogenic atmosphere. It is like rediscovering the disappearing emotion of a real tent in a clean indoor version.

One-line summary: Real red tents are decrease due to crackdowns, rent, and hygiene, and retro indoor gun carriages that imitate that sentiment are being revived as MZ's hot spot. Koreans' longing for pocha remains the same, but only the vessel that holds it is changing.

⑦ Foreigner Pocha practice – What do you need to know?

Below are experience tips and are not recommendations for specific stores (refer to the separate guide for locations). Operations and prices vary by store — on-site confirmation is recommended.

  • When: Pocha and night market are night culture. Usually open from evening to early morning. Yajang Alley (Euljiro·Jongno) is most crowded in the evening during warm season.
  • How ​​much: A bottle of soju + 1-2 snacks per person ₩15,000~30,000 (approximately $11~22) is common. Emotional indoor pochas can be a little more expensive due to the ambiance attached.
  • Payment/Language: Traditional street pochas sometimes accept cash only and have menus only in Korean. For a foreigner's first visit, the indoor pocha with menu and credit card payment is convenient.
  • Ordering Manners: Sit down, order snacks, and order drinks. For Korean drinking etiquette (pouring and receiving with both hands, first drink, etc.), see → #1140. When the glass is empty, I like to fill it. It's natural to exchange simple greetings with guests you don't know.
  • What to eat: Start mildly with fish cake soup, egg rolls, tteokbokki, spicy with chicken feet, and delicious with offal. Alcohol is soju, beer, and somaek.
  • Enjoy the atmosphere: The core of Pocha is not the food, but the emotion. The real Pocha experience is to slowly drink a glass and feel the warm air rather than eating and waking up quickly.

❓ Foreigners Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • What is the difference between a street food stall and a regular street food stall? — Unlike snack stalls (tteokbokki, hotteok) during the day, a stall is a small bar where you can sit in a tent and enjoy drinks and snacks at night. “Let’s go to Pocha” usually means “Let’s have a drink.”
  • Can I go alone? — You can. Pocha is a natural space for people to enjoy a glass of soju alone. However, in terms of atmosphere, two or more are more exciting. For safety reasons, please pace yourself when drinking alone late at night.
  • Do you have an English menu? — Traditional street pochas often only have Korean menus. The easiest places to use English menus and card payments are the indoor pocha or the alley where many foreigners live. If there is a photo menu, you can order with your fingers.
  • Can I not drink alcohol? — You can. You are welcome even if you just eat fish cake, udon, and tteokbokki. However, the emotion of pocha comes to life the most when paired with alcohol and snacks.
  • I’m worried about hygiene. — There is a big difference in hygiene among street vendors. If you are worried, it is neat to choose an officially licensed indoor gun carriage. It is a good idea to take care of your own cash and belongings.
