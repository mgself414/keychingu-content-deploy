Korean food stall culture — Why do Koreans drink soju in red tents on the streets?

Pojangmacha, Pocha, Yajang, Soju, Snacks — Explainer of Korean street bar culture for foreigners. Not ‘Where do I go to eat?’ but ‘Why, what does it mean, and how has it changed?’

When watching K-dramas, the same scenes appear every late night. Inside the red (orange) tent lit with orange light bulbs, the protagonist tips his glass of soju in front of a steaming pot. When you break up with someone, break up at work, or confess your true feelings to a friend — Korean people usually head to this tent. Foreigners see this as just a “street food tent,” but the pojangmacha (包裝馬車) that Koreans refer to is not just a street food stall. It is a common people's love room with a 70-year history, a space of affection where you can relieve the day's fatigue with a bottle of soju, and the most loved confession stage in dramas. And now, this tent is splitting into two - on the one hand, it is disappearing from the streets due to crackdowns, and on the other hand, it is being reborn as a retro-style indoor gun carriage of the MZ generation. This article is not about “which pocha is delicious?” (→ location guide), but explains the emotions, history, and changes behind the tent from a foreigner’s perspective. ⚠️ 3 things foreigners often misunderstand

1. "Street food stall = just a street food stall" ❌ — A street snack stall selling tteokbokki and hotteok during the day and a stall selling soju at night are different. The latter is closer to a small bar where you sit inside a tent, order snacks, and drink alcohol. To Koreans, “let’s go to a food stall” usually means “let’s go for a drink.”

2. "Tents = poor and unsanitary" ❌ — Even though they look shabby, food stalls have been loved as equal spaces that do not discriminate between classes in Korean society. Office workers, students, and taxi drivers drink shoulder to shoulder in the same tent. This is why the drama even takes the rich protagonist to the carriage.

3. "Because it's oden (おでん), it's not a Japanese food." ❌ — Although 'oden' is a Japanese word, the fish cake soup at the food stall is a representative Korean-style snack. Hot fish cake soup served in a paper cup on a cold night is the symbol of Pocha. Even though the name is Japanese, the sentiment is completely Korean.

① What is a pojangmacha (cart wrapped in cloth)?

Pojangmacha (包裝馬車) literally means 'a carriage (cart) wrapped in cloth'. If translated into English it would be “covered wagon”.

  • Core components: orange/red tent + small handcart (countertop) on wheels + several folding plastic chairs + one or two incandescent lights to illuminate the inside of the tent.
  • Mainly open at night. A place for office workers to have a drink on their way home from work, a second or third drink after a drink, or a late-night snack in the early morning when hungry.
  • The menu focuses on alcohol (soju, beer) and snacks. From snacks like tteokbokki and sundae to alcoholic snacks like chicken feet and tripe.

It is called Pocha for short. When a Korean says, “Would you like a cup of Pocha?” — it is not an invitation to an expensive bar, but an invitation to have a comfortable and inexpensive drink.

② Street Pocha vs. Indoor Pocha — Same name, two spaces

It's easy for foreigners to get confused when they hear the word 'pocha'. Today, there are two main types of pocha in Korea.

Street Pocha (Stall) Indoor Pocha (Shop)
form Tent + cart (mobile, stall) A bar inside the building (pocha concept interior)
Mood Rawness·Nostalgia·Improvisation Clean, trendy, retro sensibility

→ The red tent seen in dramas is usually a street pocha (or a set built to imitate one). These days, though, foreigners more often end up at an indoor pocha (a pocha-concept bar). Both share the same emotional roots, but the experience differs.

④ Why Drink in a Tent — Common People, the Commute Home, and Jeong (정)

The real meaning of pojangmacha lies not in the food but in emotion. Why do Koreans deliberately seek out a shabby tent?

  • A living room for common people: Pocha began in the poor 1950s–60s as a space where ordinary people could afford a cheap drink. Precisely because it's unpretentious, anyone feels comfortable walking in — affordable prices, no formality.
  • Comfort on the way home: At the end of an exhausting day, loosen your tie, sit in the tent, and pour a glass of soju. For Korean office workers, pocha is a station to set down the day's fatigue. One foreign outlet even described pocha as "a psychiatrist's office where you drink and pour your heart out."
  • A space for sharing jeong: In a cramped tent, even strangers' shoulders touch. Once a drink or two is in, Korea's distinctive jeong starts to flow — a place to listen to a friend's worries, speak your true feelings, and offer comfort. (→ For jeong itself, see #1135)
  • A stage dramas love: That's why K-dramas almost always place the scene where a character bares their heart in a pocha. A confession fueled by liquid courage, crying to a friend, reconciliation — the rawest emotions erupt inside this tent.

Pocha scenes in K-dramas (verify title/context before watching): the drunken confession in 〈Hospital Playlist〉, the soju confession in 〈Crash Landing on You〉, the angry-drinking scene in 〈She Was Pretty〉, and more — pocha is the official stage in Korean dramas for "the moment true feelings come out."

⑤ Relationship to Yajang (야장) — Outdoor Drinking Without a Tent

Easily confused with pocha is the currently trending yajang. The two are similar but different.

  • Yajang = "ya (野, field)" + "jang (場, yard)," meaning an outdoor drinking spot set up with benches or tables on a street or alley outside a building. It's still a yajang even without a tent (pojang).
  • Signature spots: Euljiro's Nogari Alley and the Jongno 3-ga area. By day these are printing-shop and metalworking streets, but at night the alleys fill with outdoor tables, mixing tie-loosened office workers, university students, and foreign travelers. Euljiro's specialty is nogari (노가리) — dried pollack — with draft beer.
  • Relationship to pocha: If pocha is "a mobile stall with a tent pitched over it," yajang is the broader concept of "a drinking spot set up outdoors, outside a building." Many shops, like those in Nogari Alley, combine indoor and outdoor seating, so the two often overlap.

Legal note: Under current food sanitation law, cooking and operating a business outside a building is, in principle, illegal. That's why street pocha and yajang always carry the risk of crackdowns and hygiene controversy (see ⑥ below). Foreigners should enjoy the atmosphere but look after their own cash, belongings, and hygiene.

⑥ ★ Change — Disappearing on the Streets, Reviving Indoors

Pojangmacha culture is currently moving in two opposite directions at once. This is the most important part of this article.

(A) Street Pocha — Fewer Tents

  • A history of crackdowns: Ahead of the 1988 Seoul Olympics, pocha became a target of massive crackdowns and demolitions on the grounds that they "harmed the city's appearance." Since then, attitudes toward street stalls have grown steadily colder over pedestrian right-of-way, hygiene, and price-gouging.
  • Why they're disappearing: Rising downtown rents, tighter management, hygiene regulations, and above all, the public opinion that once defended illegal street stalls has cooled. In the past there was sympathy for "not persecuting livelihood stalls," but today criticism over pedestrian and hygiene issues runs stronger. As a result, street-tent pocha have visibly declined.

(B) Indoor Pocha — Revived as Retro

  • At the same time, the sensibility of pocha has moved indoors into clean, newly trendy spaces. These so-called "indoor pojangmacha (indoor pocha)" are bars that recreate the pocha atmosphere in a formal storefront instead of a tent.
  • Among the MZ generation, riding the "yeongtro (영트로)" (young + retro) wave, emotionally styled pocha dressed in 1980s–90s sensibility have become hot spots. A "NEW-PO (뉴포)" concept — retro interiors like old posters, incandescent bulbs, and disco balls, combined with clean hygiene and trendy plating — draws in people in their 20s and 30s.
  • Why MZ is missing: Retro sensibility of consuming old times that you haven't experienced in person with 'hip newness' + affordable prices + photogenic atmosphere. It is like rediscovering the disappearing emotion of a real tent in a clean indoor version.

One-line summary: Real red tents are decrease due to crackdowns, rent, and hygiene, and retro indoor gun carriages that imitate that sentiment are being revived as MZ's hot spot. Koreans' longing for pocha remains the same, but only the vessel that holds it is changing.

⑦ Foreigner Pocha practice – What do you need to know?

Below are experience tips and are not recommendations for specific stores (refer to the separate guide for locations). Operations and prices vary by store — on-site confirmation is recommended.

  • When: Pocha and night market are night culture. Usually open from evening to early morning. Yajang Alley (Euljiro·Jongno) is most crowded in the evening during warm season.
  • How ​​much: A bottle of soju + 1-2 snacks per person ₩15,000~30,000 (approximately $11~22) is common. Emotional indoor pochas can be a little more expensive due to the ambiance attached.
  • Payment/Language: Traditional street pochas sometimes accept cash only and have menus only in Korean. For a foreigner's first visit, the indoor pocha with menu and credit card payment is convenient.
  • Ordering Manners: Sit down, order snacks, and order drinks. For Korean drinking etiquette (pouring and receiving with both hands, first drink, etc.), see → #1140. When the glass is empty, I like to fill it. It's natural to exchange simple greetings with guests you don't know.
  • What to eat: Start mildly with fish cake soup, egg rolls, tteokbokki, spicy with chicken feet, and delicious with offal. Alcohol is soju, beer, and somaek.
  • Enjoy the atmosphere: The core of Pocha is not the food, but the emotion. The real Pocha experience is to slowly drink a glass and feel the warm air rather than eating and waking up quickly.

❓ Foreigners Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • What is the difference between a street food stall and a regular street food stall? — Unlike snack stalls (tteokbokki, hotteok) during the day, a stall is a small bar where you can sit in a tent and enjoy drinks and snacks at night. “Let’s go to Pocha” usually means “Let’s have a drink.”
  • Can I go alone? — You can. Pocha is a natural space for people to enjoy a glass of soju alone. However, in terms of atmosphere, two or more are more exciting. For safety reasons, please pace yourself when drinking alone late at night.
  • Do you have an English menu? — Traditional street pochas often only have Korean menus. The easiest places to use English menus and card payments are the indoor pocha or the alley where many foreigners live. If there is a photo menu, you can order with your fingers.
  • Can I not drink alcohol? — You can. You are welcome even if you just eat fish cake, udon, and tteokbokki. However, the emotion of pocha comes to life the most when paired with alcohol and snacks.
  • I’m worried about hygiene. — There is a big difference in hygiene among street vendors. If you are worried, it is neat to choose an officially licensed indoor gun carriage. It is a good idea to take care of your own cash and belongings.
