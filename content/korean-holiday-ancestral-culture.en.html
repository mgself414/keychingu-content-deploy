Korean holiday, ancestral rite, and ancestral rite culture — Why does the whole family gather together, prepare food, and bow?

Lunar New Year, Chuseok, ancestral rites, ancestral rites, New Year's greetings, gravesites, filial piety — Explainer of Korean holidays and ancestral rites for foreigners. Not ‘Where should we go for the holidays?’ but ‘Why, what, and with what meaning.’

If you live in Korea or watch K-dramas, you will encounter the amazing sight of the city being completely rainy twice a year. Half of the restaurants in Seoul are closed, the highways are blocked for over 10 hours, and the whole family gets in the car and heads home. When you arrive, you spend all day in the kitchen baking pancakes and making rice cakes, and in the morning you prepare a full table and bow twice to your ancestors. Foreigners usually translate this as “Korean Thanksgiving,” but that’s only half true. Korea's two major holidays Lunar New Year and Chuseok are not just holidays, but rituals where deceased ancestors are brought home to eat together and living family members gather together. This article is not about “where to go for the holidays” (→ cross-link), but explains why Koreans repeat this arduous ritual every year — the family, ancestors, and filial piety mentality behind it from a foreigner’s perspective. ⚠️ 3 things foreigners often misunderstand

1. "Chuseok = American Thanksgiving" ❌ — Both celebrate the fall harvest, but are crucially different. If Thanksgiving is a 'feast for living family and friends,' Chuseok is a holiday centered on ancestors, offering ceremonial ancestral rites to deceased ancestors with new grains and visiting their graves in search of oxygen. “Thanksgiving + remembering ancestors” is more accurate.

2. "Chae·Jisa = religious ceremony (worship)" ❌ — It looks like religion because you bow and offer food, but for most Koreans, this is not a worship service to God, but a 'family ritual to remember and serve deceased parents and grandparents'. Christian families sometimes hold memorial services instead of ancestral rites — the form varies from family to family.

3. "During the holidays, the whole of Korea is closed, so tourists have nowhere to go." ❌ — Many small shops and restaurants in the neighborhood are closed, but Myeong-dong, Insa-dong, Hongdae, palaces, department stores, convenience stores, and theme parks are generally open. Rather, forbidden palaces are often open for free + traditional performances on holidays (⑧ below).

① Lunar New Year and Chuseok — Korea’s two major holidays

There are numerous holidays in Korea, but when we say “holidays,” we usually refer to both Lunar New Year’s Day and Chuseok. Since both follow the lunar calendar, the solar date changes every year.

  • Lunar New Year (January 1st of the lunar calendar) — The first day of the lunar new year. We welcome the beginning of the year with our families, eat tteokguk, do saebae, and perform ancestral rites. In 2026, February 17th (Tuesday) will be a three-day holiday before and after.
  • Chuseok (August 15th of the lunar calendar) — The day when the full moon in the fall is the roundest. It is a holiday to give thanks to ancestors for the year's harvest, and is also called 'Hangawi'. It is a long-standing tradition that traces its origins back to the Silla period. Make songpyeon with new grains and visit the adult grave. The year 2026 is September 25 (Friday), a three-day holiday before and after.

The common core of the two holidays is homecoming — families who have been living apart gather together at their hometown. So holidays are 'almost the only time when the whole family gets together in one place' in Korea.

② Rites vs. ancestral rites — the most important distinction

This is a point that confuses even younger Koreans, not just foreigners. Both rites involve offering food to ancestors and bowing, but they differ in when and why they are held.

Charye (차례) Jesa (제사)
When On holidays (Lunar New Year, Chuseok) On the deceased's death anniversary
Time Morning (daytime) of the holiday Traditionally at night — often moved up to evening today
Purpose To announce the holiday's arrival to ancestors To host a meal for the deceased's spirit
Formality Simple — one cup of wine, a charye table setting Elaborate and painstaking — many food items
Who's honored Usually several generations of ancestors together Usually one person (that day's honoree)

Where the name comes from: The word charye originally meant a rite of offering tea — traditionally making it a simpler rite than jesa. In practice, a traditional charye was as brief as one cup of wine, one cup of tea, and a plate of fruit, and the written prayer read aloud was often skipped entirely.

There are also related rites: seongmyo (성묘) — visiting and tending an ancestor's grave — and seasonal rites held at graves or shrines for more distant ancestors. The two rites foreign visitors are most likely to encounter during the holidays are charye (at home) and seongmyo (at the grave).

③ The Charye Table and Its Food — the 'rules' actually differ from house to house

When people picture the holidays, they picture the charye table (jesa table) — a spread of fish, jeon (pan-fried dishes), namul (seasoned vegetables), fruit, and rice cakes arranged in neat rows. Online, 'rules' like hongdongbaekseo (홍동백서, 'red fruit east, white fruit west') and joyulisi (조율이시, 'jujube, chestnut, pear, persimmon from left to right') circulate widely. But there's a truth foreigners should know.

  • These 'rules' are not absolute law. Korea has a long-standing principle called gagarye (가가례) — 'every household has its own etiquette.' The saying 'Don't tell someone else how to arrange their own ancestral rite table' comes from exactly this idea.
  • Scholars and journalists have pointed out that terms like hongdongbaekseo and joyulisi actually solidified only in modern times, and Joseon-era scholars themselves never even knew them. In other words, they're closer to a later-generation custom than an 'authentic rule.'
  • Even Sungkyunkwan, the historic home of Korea's Confucian tradition, has recently recommended holding the rite 'simply, without empty formality, in a way that brings the family together.' The layout and number of dishes on a charye table vary by region and family, so don't assume there's a single 'correct' way — when in doubt, ask an elder in that family.

The consensus today is that what matters isn't the rules of the table setting, but remembering one's ancestors and being together as a family. For reference, the towering, elaborate charye tables foreigners often see in photos are usually from a head family (jongga) or a household that keeps to strict formality — the formality of the royal court's sura table is said to have influenced these elite ancestral-rite tables (→ 'Dig Deeper' #690) — while an ordinary household's table is far simpler.

Two dishes that define the holidays

  • Tteokguk (rice cake soup) — Lunar New Year — a white rice cake soup eaten on the first day of the new year. There's a saying: 'Eat a bowl of tteokguk and you turn a year older.' Under Korea's traditional age-counting system, everyone turned a year older together at New Year rather than on their individual birthday, and tteokguk symbolizes that shared birthday. Old texts even called it cheomsebyeong ('the rice cake that adds a year'). Its white color represents the purity of a fresh start, and the long rice-cake strands symbolize longevity and wealth.
  • Songpyeon — Chuseok — half-moon-shaped rice cakes steamed on a bed of pine needles. They're made from that year's newly harvested grain (fresh rice, sesame, beans, red beans), sharing the joy of the harvest and giving thanks to ancestors. The whole family sitting together to shape songpyeon is itself one of Chuseok's signature scenes.

④ Saebae, Seongmyo, and Gwiseong — the living customs of the holidays

If ancestral rites are the 'solemn half' of the holidays, the other half is the warm customs shared among the living.

  • Saebae (세배) — mainly on Lunar New Year, children and younger family members give a deep bow to their elders as a New Year's greeting. In return, the elder offers words of blessing, such as 'May you receive great fortune in the new year,' and gives the children saebaedon (New Year's money). It's a fixture of K-drama Lunar New Year scenes, and often the moment foreigners find most memorable when invited into a Korean home.
  • Seongmyo — mainly around Chuseok (and also Lunar New Year), families visit an ancestor's grave to bow and tend the site. Before Chuseok, families do beolcho (벌초) — cutting the grass around the grave — in advance. It's a time for the family to climb the mountain together and remember their ancestors.
  • Gwiseong (귀성, 'the homecoming') — Korea's 'great migration' — tens of millions of people travel to their hometowns around the holidays. This makes the holidays Korea's biggest traffic-jam season: KTX and express-bus tickets sell out weeks in advance, and a normal 4-5 hour drive can stretch past 10 hours. This is why foreign visitors who plan to travel domestically during the holidays should book train tickets early (see ⑧ below).

⑤ Why do all this — the roots in Confucianism and filial piety

If you had to sum up the root of the holiday rites in one idea, it would be hyo (孝, filial piety). Hyo means honoring one's parents and ancestors and repaying their kindness — a core value that Confucianism planted deep in Korean society.

  • In the Confucian worldview, family does not end with death. Deceased ancestors are still considered part of the family, and it has long been believed that when descendants honor them properly, the ancestors watch over the family through eumdeok (음덕) — a kind of unseen ancestral blessing. Charye and jesa are, in effect, occasions where the living family and the deceased family share a meal together.
  • That's why the central gesture of the rite is two deep bows (jaebae, 재배) — traditionally, the living receive one bow, but the deceased receive two. It's a gesture of respect and longing toward one's ancestors.
  • Interestingly, scholars believe this ancestor worship isn't purely a product of Confucianism (hyo) — it's also deeply intertwined with the ancestor-spirit and shamanistic beliefs that existed on the Korean peninsula before Confucianism arrived. In other words, the sense that 'ancestors watch over their descendants' runs through Korean culture from well before Confucianism was introduced.

In short, the reason a whole family gathers to prepare food and bow during the holidays is to remember where we came from (our roots), repay that debt of gratitude, and confirm that the family line continues unbroken.

⑥ How it's changing today — simplification, holiday syndrome, and the 'holiday spent traveling'

Holiday culture is changing fast right now — and understanding this gives foreign visitors a more three-dimensional picture of Korean society.

  • Simplifying or skipping charye — more and more families are drastically cutting down the food, splitting the labor between men and women, or skipping the rite altogether. Sungkyunkwan's own recommendation to simplify reflects this same trend.
  • Myeongjeol-jeunghugun (명절증후군, 'holiday syndrome') — a distinctly Korean term for the stress caused by holiday labor, long-distance travel, and family conflict. The main culprits are usually the kitchen workload that falls on the daughter-in-law's generation, a homecoming drive that can stretch past 10 hours, and relatives' unwelcome questions ('When are you getting married?' 'Do you have a job yet?'). It's also part of the backdrop behind Korean society's push to simplify the holidays.
  • The 'holiday spent traveling' — more families now simplify or skip charye entirely and use the holiday break to travel domestically or abroad. Incheon Airport packed with holiday travelers has become a new fixture of the season. In other words, 'every Korean holds charye during the holidays' is no longer true — families that keep tradition, families that simplify it, and families that travel instead now all coexist.

This shift is itself a snapshot of Korean society — tradition (filial piety, family) colliding with individualism and pragmatism, and the two negotiating a compromise.

⑦ If a foreigner visits Korea during the holidays — what will they actually see

Holiday season has clear pros and cons for foreign tourists — there are unexpected opportunities behind those 'closed doors.'

  • What closes: small neighborhood restaurants, shops, hair salons, and small galleries often close for 1-3 days on the holiday itself (especially Chuseok and Lunar New Year's Day proper). If it's your first trip to Korea, a downtown or tourist-area hotel is safer than one in an outlying residential neighborhood.
  • What stays open: major areas like Myeong-dong, Insa-dong, Hongdae, Bukchon, Gangnam, and the palace district, plus the main sights in Busan, Gyeongju, and Jeju, generally run as usual. Convenience stores (CU, GS25, 7-Eleven, emart24) stay open 24 hours as always, so even if the neighborhood restaurant is closed, you can still get a meal. Department stores, theme parks, and observation decks generally stay open too.
  • A holiday bonus: Seoul's royal palaces (Gyeongbokgung, Changdeokgung, and others) often open for free, with traditional performances and hands-on experiences, during the holidays. Wearing hanbok and enjoying a palace in an otherwise empty downtown is a special experience possible only during the holidays.
  • A transit warning: KTX and domestic flights sell out weeks in advance. If you're planning to travel from Seoul to Busan, Gyeongju, Jeju, or elsewhere during the holidays, book your tickets early. On the flip side, downtown Seoul is unusually quiet on the holiday itself, so normally crowded spots can be enjoyed at leisure.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions from Foreigners (FAQ)

  • Is Chuseok exactly the same as American Thanksgiving? — There are similarities (fall harvest, family gathering, abundant food), but the key difference is that Chuseok is an ancestor-centered holiday, built around holding charye for ancestors and visiting their graves. Think of it as 'Thanksgiving + honoring ancestors.'
  • Are charye and jesa religious? — For most Koreans, these are not a religious worship service but a family ritual and traditional culture. That said, Christian families sometimes hold a memorial service instead of charye — the form varies by household.
  • Does the charye table have to follow a fixed set of rules? — 'Rules' like hongdongbaekseo and joyulisi exist, but they're not absolute — they vary by region and family (gagarye). Even Sungkyunkwan recommends simplifying. Don't assume there's a single 'correct answer.'
  • Is it okay to travel to Korea during the holidays? — It's generally fine, since downtown areas, tourist spots, palaces, and convenience stores mostly stay open. Just note that small neighborhood shops may close, and train tickets to regional destinations sell out early — a downtown hotel plus early booking is recommended.
  • Can foreigners experience charye or saebae themselves? — If you're invited into a Korean home, you may see saebae or charye firsthand. Traditional experience events at the palaces during the holidays also let you get a light taste of holiday culture — bowing, games, food, and more.
