Korean Holiday & Ancestral Rite Culture — Why Korean Families Gather to Cook, Bow, and Remember

> Seollal (설날) · Chuseok (추석) · Charye (차례) · Jesa (제사) · Sebae (세배) · Seongmyo (성묘) · Hyo (孝, filial piety) — An explainer on Korean holidays and ancestral rites for foreigners. Not "where to go during holidays," but "why, what, and what it means."

If you live in Korea or watch K-dramas, you'll encounter a peculiar sight twice a year: entire cities emptying out. Half of Seoul's (서울) restaurants close, highways are jammed for over 10 hours, and families pack into cars, heading to their hometowns. Upon arrival, they spend all day in the kitchen frying jeon (전, savory pancakes) and making tteok (떡, rice cakes). In the morning, they set a table full of food and bow (절) twice to their ancestors. Foreigners often translate this as "Korean Thanksgiving," but that's only half true. Korea's two major holidays, Seollal (설날) and Chuseok (추석), are not just simple days off; they are rituals where deceased ancestors are invited home to share a meal, and living family members gather in one place. This article isn't about "places to go during the holidays" (→ cross-link), but rather, it explains why Koreans repeat these arduous rituals every year — the underlying family, ancestral, and Hyo (孝, filial piety) mindset from a foreigner's perspective. This is the fifth installment in our Korean culture explainer series.

> Key Distinction: Charye (차례) = a rite performed during holidays (Seollal (설날) and Chuseok (추석)) / Jesa (제사) = a rite performed on the deceased's memorial day (the day they passed away). This is the point foreigners most often confuse (see ② below). 2026: Seollal (설날) is around February 17 (Tuesday) and Chuseok (추석) is around September 25 (Friday) — exact holiday dates vary each year based on the lunar calendar, so checking before your visit is recommended.

⚠️ Three Common Misunderstandings by Foreigners

1. "Chuseok (추석) = American Thanksgiving" ❌ — Both commemorate autumn harvests, but they are fundamentally different. While Thanksgiving is a "feast for living family and friends," Chuseok (추석) is an ancestor-centric holiday where Charye (차례) is performed with new crops for deceased ancestors, and Seongmyo (성묘) is done by visiting their graves. "Thanksgiving + ancestral remembrance" is a more accurate description.

2. "Charye (차례) · Jesa (제사) = Religious Ceremony (Worship)" ❌ — While bowing and offering food may seem religious, for most Koreans, these are not worship directed at a deity, but rather "family rituals to remember and honor deceased parents and grandparents." Christian families may hold memorial services instead of Charye (차례) — the form varies by household.

3. "During holidays, all of Korea is closed, so tourists have nowhere to go" ❌ — Many small local shops and restaurants do close, but Myeongdong (명동), Insa-dong (인사동), Hongdae (홍대), palaces (궁궐), department stores (백화점), convenience stores (편의점), and theme parks generally remain open. In fact, royal palaces (고궁) often offer free admission + traditional performances during holidays (see ⑧ below). A convenience store (편의점) is a small retail outlet that sells a wide range of everyday items, snacks, and drinks, often open 24/7.

① Seollal (설날) and Chuseok (추석) — Korea's Two Major Holidays

While Korea has many commemorative days, "holidays" usually refer to Seollal (설날) and Chuseok (추석). Both follow the lunar calendar (陰曆, Eumnyeok), so their Gregorian dates change annually.

  • Seollal (설날, Lunar New Year's Day, Lunar January 1) — The first day of the lunar new year. Families welcome the new year together, eating tteokguk (떡국, rice cake soup), performing Sebae (세배, New Year's bows), and holding Charye (차례). In 2026, it's around February 17 (Tuesday), with a three-day holiday period.
  • Chuseok (추석, Autumn Full Moon & Harvest Holiday, Lunar August 15) — The day when the autumn full moon is brightest. It's a holiday to thank ancestors for the year's harvest, also called 'Hangawi (한가위)'. Its origins trace back to the Silla (신라) Dynasty, making it an ancient tradition. Families make songpyeon (송편, half-moon shaped rice cakes) with newly harvested grains and visit ancestral graves (성묘, Seongmyo). In 2026, it's around September 25 (Friday), with a three-day holiday period.

The common core of both holidays is Gwiseong (歸省, returning to one's hometown) — scattered family members gather at their ancestral home. Thus, holidays are 'almost the only time when the entire family gathers in one place' in Korea.

② Charye (차례) vs. Jesa (제사) — The Most Important Distinction

This is a point of confusion not only for foreigners but also for younger Koreans. Both are rituals of offering food and bowing to ancestors, but they differ in when and why they are performed.

Charye (茶禮) Jesa (祭祀, Gijesa)
When During holidays (Seollal (설날) and Chuseok (추석)) On the deceased's memorial day (忌日, Giiil)
Time On the holiday morning (daytime) Traditionally at night (자시, Jasi, 23:00-01:00) — nowadays often moved to evening
Purpose To inform ancestors that a holiday has arrived To invite the deceased's spirit and offer a meal
Formality Brief — one cup of alcohol, simple Charye (차례) table setting Formal and elaborate — many dishes
Subjects Usually multiple generations of ancestors together Usually that one individual (the main subject of the memorial day)

> Origin of the Name: The 'Cha (茶, tea)' in Charye (茶禮) originally meant a ritual of offering tea. This implies that it is a simpler ritual than Jesa (제사) in its traditional sense. In fact, traditional Charye (차례) was brief, often involving just a cup of alcohol, a cup of tea, and a platter of fruit, with the omission of a chukmun (축문, written prayer) being common.

In addition, there is Seongmyo (省墓) — visiting and tending to ancestral graves, and Sije (시제) / Myoje (묘제) — seasonal rites performed for distant ancestors or at grave sites. What foreigners most frequently encounter during holidays are Charye (차례) (at home) and Seongmyo (성묘) (at grave sites).

③ Charye (차례) Table and Food — 'Rules' Actually Vary by Household

What comes to mind when thinking of holidays is the Charyesang (차례상, ancestral rite table) — a table meticulously arranged with fish, jeon (전), namul (나물, seasoned vegetables), fruits, and tteok (떡). The internet circulates 'rules' like Hongdongbaekseo (紅東白西, red fruits on the east, white fruits on the west) and Joyullisi (棗栗梨枾, jujubes, chestnuts, pears, persimmons from left). However, there's a truth foreigners should know:

  • These rules are not absolute laws. Korea has an old principle called Gagarye (家家禮) — "each household has its own etiquette." The proverb "Don't tell others how to arrange chestnuts or pears at their Jesa (제사)" comes from this.
  • Scholars and media also point out that terms like Hongdongbaekseo (홍동백서) and Joyullisi (조율이시) were actually solidified after the modern era and were unknown to Joseon (조선) scholars. In other words, they are closer to later customs than "authentic rules."
  • Even Sungkyunkwan (성균관), the historical center of Korean Confucian tradition, recently issued a recommendation that Charye (차례) can be performed "simply, without excessive formality, in a way that fosters family harmony." The arrangement and number of dishes on the Charye (차례) table vary by region and clan, so do not assume "this is the correct answer" (if confused, it's recommended to ask an elder in that family).

The core idea, in today's trend, is not the rules of table setting, but the spirit of remembering ancestors and family togetherness. For reference, the 'mountain-like stacked Charye (차례) tables' that foreigners see in photos are usually from Jongga (종가, head family of a clan) or highly formal households (the formality of Surasang (수라상, royal court cuisine) influenced the Jesa (제사) tables of noble families → 'Dive Deeper' #690), and ordinary households' tables are much simpler.

Two Representative Holiday Foods

  • Tteokguk (떡국, Rice Cake Soup) (Seollal (설날)) — White tteokguk (떡국) eaten on New Year's Day. There's a saying, "Eat one bowl of tteokguk (떡국) and you get one year older." In Korea's traditional 'counting age' system (before the current international age system), everyone turned a year older with the new year, and tteokguk (떡국) symbolized this. Ancient texts sometimes referred to tteokguk (떡국) as Cheomsebyeong (添歲餠, 'age-adding rice cake'). The white color signifies purity for a new start, and the long garaetteok (가래떡, cylinder-shaped rice cake) symbolizes longevity and wealth.
  • Songpyeon (송편, Half-Moon Shaped Rice Cakes) (Chuseok (추석)) — Rice cakes shaped like a half-moon and steamed over pine needles. They are made with newly harvested grains (햅쌀, haepssal, new rice; 깨, kkae, sesame seeds; 콩, kong, beans; 팥, pat, red beans) of that year, symbolizing the joy of harvest and gratitude to ancestors. The act of the whole family sitting together to make songpyeon (송편) is itself a quintessential Chuseok (추석) scene.

④ Sebae (세배) · Seongmyo (성묘) · Gwiseong (귀성) — Living Holiday Customs

While ancestral rites are the 'solemn half' of the holidays, the other half consists of warm customs among living family members.

  • Sebae (歲拜, New Year's Bows) — Primarily on Seollal (설날), children and young adults perform a deep bow to their elders to offer New Year's greetings. The elders who receive the bows offer deokdam (덕담, words of blessing) like "Saehae bok mani badeura (새해 복 많이 받아라, Have a very happy New Year)" and give sebaetdon (세뱃돈, New Year's money/pocket money) to the children. This is a common scene in K-drama Seollal (설날) episodes and is often the most impressive moment for foreigners invited to a Korean home.
  • Seongmyo (省墓, Visiting Ancestral Graves) — Primarily on Chuseok (추석) (also on Seollal (설날)), families visit ancestral graves to bow and tend to the burial site. Before Chuseok (추석), they perform beolcho (벌초, weeding of graves) to clear grass around the graves. It's a time for families to climb mountains together and remember their ancestors.
  • Gwiseong (歸省, Returning Home) = Great Migration — Millions of people travel to their hometowns before and after the holidays. This makes the holidays Korea's biggest traffic jam season — KTX (Korea Train eXpress) and intercity bus tickets sell out weeks in advance, and what normally takes 4-5 hours can take over 10 hours. This is why foreign tourists planning domestic travel in Korea during the holidays should book train tickets early (see ⑧ below).

⑤ Why Do All This? — The Roots of Confucianism and Hyo (孝)

The root of holiday rituals can be summarized in one character: Hyo (孝, filial piety). Hyo (효) is revering parents and ancestors and repaying their kindness — a core value deeply embedded in Korean society by Confucianism (유교).

  • In the Confucian worldview, family is not severed by death. Deceased ancestors are still members of the family, and it has been believed that if descendants honor them sincerely, the ancestors will look after them with eumdeok (陰德, ancestral blessings). Charye (차례) and Jesa (제사) are thus 'occasions where living family and deceased family share a meal'.
  • Therefore, the core action of the ritual is two deep bows (再拜, Jaebae) — traditionally, one bows once to the living and twice to the deceased. This is a gesture of respect and longing for ancestors.
  • Interesting point: Scholars believe that these ancestral rites are not solely a product of Confucianism (Hyo (효)), but are also deeply intertwined with ancestral worship and shamanistic beliefs that existed on the Korean Peninsula even before Confucianism. That is, the sentiment that "ancestors watch over their descendants" has flowed through Korean culture since before the introduction of Confucianism.

In summary, the reason why the entire family gathers to prepare food and bow during holidays is to — "remember where we came from (our roots), repay that kindness, and affirm that the family continues unbroken."

⑥ Modern Changes — Simplification, Holiday Syndrome, and 'Holiday Travel'

Holiday culture is rapidly changing. Foreigners can gain a more nuanced understanding of Korean society by being aware of these shifts.

  • Charye (차례) Simplification/Omission — An increasing number of households are drastically reducing the amount of food, sharing the labor between men and women, or even skipping Charye (차례) altogether. Sungkyunkwan's (성균관) recommendation for simplification aligns with this trend.
  • Myeongjeol Jeunghugun (名節症候群, Holiday Syndrome) — A uniquely Korean term referring to stress caused by holiday labor, long-distance travel, and family conflicts. The burden of kitchen labor on daughters-in-law, 10+ hour drives home, and relatives' nagging ("When are you getting married?", "Did you get a job?") are cited as major causes. This is also a reason why Korean society is 'simplifying' holidays.
  • 'Holiday Travel' — With Charye (차례) simplified or skipped, an increasing number of families are using the holidays to travel domestically or internationally. Incheon International Airport (인천공항) bustling with departing crowds every holiday is a new sight. In other words, "all Koreans perform Charye (차례) during holidays" is no longer true — households upholding tradition, those simplifying, and those traveling coexist.

This change itself is a cross-section of Korean society — tradition (Hyo (효) and family) and individualism/pragmatism are clashing and finding a compromise.

⑦ What Foreigners See When Visiting Korea During the Holidays

> The holiday period has distinct advantages and disadvantages for foreign tourists. Behind the 'closed doors' are unexpected opportunities.

  • What Closes: Many small local restaurants, shops, hair salons, and small-scale art museums close for 1-3 days on the actual holiday (especially Seollal (설날) and Chuseok (추석)). If it's your first trip to Korea, staying in downtown/tourist areas rather than outer residential areas is safer.
  • What Stays Open: Major attractions in Myeongdong (명동), Insa-dong (인사동), Hongdae (홍대), Bukchon (북촌), Gangnam (강남), palace areas (궁궐 일대), as well as Busan (부산), Gyeongju (경주), and Jeju (제주), generally operate normally. Convenience stores (편의점) (CU, GS25, 7-Eleven, E-Mart24) are open 24 hours — you can still get meals even if local restaurants are closed. Department stores (백화점), theme parks (테마파크), and observatories (전망대) are also generally open.
  • Holiday Bonus: Seoul's (서울) royal palaces (고궁) (e.g., Gyeongbokgung (경복궁), Changdeokgung (창덕궁)) often offer free admission + traditional performances and experiences during holidays. Enjoying the palaces in hanbok (한복, traditional Korean dress) in a quiet city center is a special experience only possible during the holidays.
  • Traffic Warning: KTX (Korea Train eXpress) and domestic flights are sold out weeks in advance. If you plan to travel to provincial areas like Busan (부산), Gyeongju (경주), or Jeju (제주) during the holidays, you must book tickets early. Conversely, Seoul's (서울) city center is quiet on the actual holiday, allowing you to enjoy usually crowded places at a leisurely pace.

> All price, operation, and opening information varies by year and institution and is an estimate for 2026 — checking official channels (VisitKorea, individual attractions) before your visit is recommended.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) for Foreigners

  • Is Chuseok (추석) exactly like American Thanksgiving? — While there are similarities (autumn harvest, family gathering, abundant food), the key difference is that Chuseok (추석) is an ancestor-centric holiday where Charye (차례) is performed for ancestors and Seongmyo (성묘) is done at grave sites. Understand it as "Thanksgiving + ancestral remembrance."
  • Are Charye (차례) and Jesa (제사) religious? — For most Koreans, they are family rituals and traditional culture, not religious worship. However, Christian families may hold memorial services instead of Charye (차례), so the form varies by household.
  • Must the Charye (차례) table be set according to fixed rules? — While there are 'rules' like Hongdongbaekseo (홍동백서) and Joyullisi (조율이시), they are not absolute and vary by region and household (Gagarye (가가례)). Sungkyunkwan (성균관) also recommends simplification. Don't think there's only one "correct answer."
  • Is it okay for foreigners to travel to Korea during the holidays? — Downtown areas, tourist spots, palaces, and convenience stores (편의점) are generally open, so it's fine. However, small local shops may be closed, and train tickets to provincial areas sell out early, so we recommend downtown accommodation + early booking.
  • Can foreigners experience Charye (차례) or Sebae (세배)? — If you're invited to a Korean home, you can directly observe Sebae (세배) and Charye (차례). You can also get a taste of holiday culture, including bows, games, and food, at traditional experience events held at royal palaces (고궁) during the holiday period.
