Alley Walk ⑥ Seongbuk-dong (성북동) — The Alley Where a Gisaeng House Became a Temple, Past a Poet's House, to a Village Below the Fortress Wall

Subtitle: Seongbuk-dong's alleys hold stories stacked layer upon layer. A hanok that belonged to a National Museum director, a temple that was once an exclusive gisaeng house, the house where a poet spent his final years facing away from Japanese colonial headquarters, and a fortress village just outside a hidden gate in the old city wall — a literary alley course of about 2 hours 30 minutes, starting from Hansung Univ. Station. Seoul Alley Walks Expansion 3.

ISSUE · #1345 · 2026-09-02

🎯 The Frame — A Neighborhood of Writers

Seongbuk-dong (성북동) is a neighborhood where modern Korean writers and artists once gathered to live. Sitting on a mountainside just outside the old city wall, it was quiet — and that quiet is what drew people who wrote for a living. This route strings together the traces they left — old houses, a temple, a fortress village — in uphill order. At the end, the whole neighborhood spreads out below you from the Hanyangdoseong (한양도성) fortress wall.

  • Total time: about 2 hours 30 minutes (3 hours with indoor visits) · Best time: start 1–2 PM → finish at the fortress-wall view
  • Incline: moderate — a gentle, sustained uphill that steepens through the Bukjeong Village (북정마을) stretch.

🚶 The Route — In Walking Order

① Hansung Univ. Station (한성대입구역) Exit 6 → Choi Sun-u's Old House (최순우 옛집) (about a 7-minute walk)

  • A hanok where Hyegok Choi Sun-u (1916–1984), former director of the National Museum of Korea, once lived. He's the writer behind Leaning Against the Entasis Pillar of Muryangsujeon (Muryangsujeon Baeheullim Gidunge Gidaeseo) — a beloved essay collection on Korean traditional beauty. Fittingly for the home of someone who put Korean beauty into words, the courtyard and the grain of the lattice doors read like a textbook.

② Choi Sun-u's Old House → Seonjamdanji (선잠단지) → Gilsangsa Temple (길상사) (about a 20-minute walk, uphill)

  • Pass Seonjamdanji, where Joseon queens once held rites to the goddess of silkworms, and climb the alley to reach Gilsangsa Temple.
  • 📖 Story 1 — How a Gisaeng House Became a Temple: Gilsangsa's site was originally Daewongak (대원각), one of Seoul's most renowned high-end gisaeng houses. Late in her life, the owner donated the entire property and its buildings to the monk Beopjeong, and it was reborn as a temple in 1997 — a story often retold for the bond between the monk known for his book Non-Possession and the gisaeng house owner, and arguably the most dramatic change of use of any space in Seoul. The structure of the old gisaeng-house annexes still remains, giving the temple a different feel from any other.

③ Gilsangsa Temple → Simujang (심우장) (about a 15-minute walk)

  • The house where the poet and independence activist Manhae Han Yong-un spent his final years. The story that defines this house: he had it built facing north because he refused to face the Japanese Governor-General's building. Free admission (09:00–18:00 as of writing; check hours before you go).

④ Simujang → Bukjeong Village (북정마을) (about a 10-minute walk, steep uphill)

  • 📖 Story 2 — A Village Below the Fortress Wall: On the slope just outside an amun (암문, a hidden emergency gate) of the Hanyangdoseong fortress wall sits a fortress village of several hundred small tile-roofed houses, built in the 1960s–70s. Only a handful of places in Seoul still look like this. The alleys are narrow and entirely residential, so passing through quietly is the courtesy this stretch calls for.

⑤ Bukjeong Village → the Hanyangdoseong Amun → Finish at the Fortress-Wall View (about a 10-minute walk)

  • Pass through the amun and stand on the path inside the fortress wall, and the Seongbuk-dong valley and every alley you've just walked spread out below in one view. This is the ending of the route.
  • Ways to end it: follow the fortress-wall trail down toward Hyehwamun (혜화문) (connecting to the #1239 history course), or retrace your steps down and rest in Seongbuk-dong's café alley.

🙏 Alley Manners

  • Gilsangsa is an active temple where monks practice — no photography inside the main hall, and keep quiet. Dress modestly on the grounds.
  • Bukjeong Village is 100% residential — don't photograph gates or yards, don't cluster in groups, and keep your voice to a minimum.
  • Simujang and Choi Sun-u's Old House both have set visiting hours — if you're starting in the late afternoon, put the indoor visits first.

💡 Where You Might Get Thrown Off

  • Nearly the entire route is uphill — add 20% to whatever a map app estimates.
  • Gilsangsa gets crowded on free-shuttle days and event days — avoiding the period around Buddha's Birthday keeps this a quiet course.
  • The fortress wall's amun has set opening hours (some sections close at night) — pass through before sunset.
