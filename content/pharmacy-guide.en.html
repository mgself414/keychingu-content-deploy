Using a Pharmacy in Korea, the Zero-Panic Manual — Just Show the Symptom Card

> Subtitle: The complete guide to pharmacies, Olive Young, and convenience-store OTC medicine in Korea — the 3-category breakdown · 6 one-sentence symptom cards (just show them to the pharmacist) · the pharmacy master flow · the algorithm for getting sick at night · guidance on prescription medicine · 13 survival words

ISSUE 04 · #1278 · 2026-08-15 · korea-first-timer series ⑨

🎯 How to Read This Manual — Card Notation Rules

The whole point of this piece is showing, not saying. Even without a single word of Korean, holding up the card below to the pharmacist is enough to finish the job.

  • Phrases to show the pharmacist appear as code: gamgiyak juseyo (감기약 주세요)
  • Shop signs and signage appear in quotes: "yak (약) — pharmacy/medicine"
  • Actions you do by hand are bold: hand over the card exactly as it appears on your screen

> Important: this piece never tells you which medicine to take or how much. Every symptom card leads to the same conclusion — "show this to the pharmacist." Diagnosis and dosage are entirely the pharmacist's call.

① Where to Buy — The 3-Category Breakdown

Category What You Can Buy When to Go
Pharmacy (a "yak (약)" sign, green cross) Every type of over-the-counter medicine, plus consultation with a pharmacist When you have a symptom and don't know what to buy — this is always the right answer
Drugstores like Olive Young A limited set of low-risk items (pain patches, antiseptic, masks, vitamins, etc. — the medicine section is small) When the pharmacy is closed and you only need something minor
Convenience stores (CU, GS25, 7-Eleven) Only about 11 types of designated OTC safety medicines — Tylenol (fever/pain relief) · Pancol (판콜) / Panpyrin (판피린) (cold medicine) · Beaje (베아제, digestive aid) · antacids · pain-relief patches, etc. Late at night, early morning, or for minor symptoms
  • The pharmacy is always the first choice. Think of Olive Young and convenience stores as temporary alternatives for when the pharmacy is closed.
  • None of these three locations can sell prescription-only medicine (like antibiotics) that requires a prescription → see ⑤.

② One-Sentence Symptom Cards — Show the Pharmacist and You're Done

These 6 cards are the core of this piece. Screenshot them or bookmark this page.

Symptom Card to Show
Headache dutongyak juseyo (두통약 주세요)
Upset stomach sohwaje juseyo (소화제 주세요)
Cold gamgiyak juseyo (감기약 주세요)
Cut/wound sangcheoe bareuneun yeon-go juseyo (상처에 바르는 연고 주세요)
Motion sickness meolmiyak juseyo (멀미약 주세요)
Allergy allereugiyak juseyo (알레르기약 주세요)
  • If you're currently on medication or there's a chance you're pregnant, show this line alongside your card too: imsin jungieyo (임신 중이에요) — "I'm pregnant" — or dareun yak meokgo isseoyo (다른 약 먹고 있어요) — "I'm taking other medicine."
  • If your symptom isn't on this list, type it into the Papago app and just show the translated Korean screen (app setup is covered in #1230).

③ The Pharmacy Master Flow — From Walking In to Walking Out

Step What to Do Tip
1 Look for a large "yak (약)" sign or a green cross These cluster along main roads and near subway stations
2 Walk in and go to the counter — no number ticket If there's a line, just wait your turn
3 Hand over the card from ② exactly as it is You don't need to say anything — pointing at where it hurts works too
4 Answer the pharmacist's questions with a nod or gesture If you already showed the allergy/pregnancy line on your card, this can be skipped
5 Take your medicine and pay Card or cash both work. The receipt (yeongsujeung (영수증)) comes printed with dosage instructions
  • There's no buzzer, no waiting number — it's a direct hand-off at the counter.

④ The Algorithm for Getting Sick at Night — Try in Order

1. Mild symptoms (a headache, low fever, mild stomach discomfort) → buy an OTC safety medicine at the nearest 24-hour convenience store (see ①) → this resolves most cases

2. Convenience-store medicine isn't enough → look for a late-night pharmacy (simya yakguk, 심야약국). Seoul alone has roughly 39 of them across its 25 districts, generally operating from about 10 PM to 1 AM — search "simya yakguk (심야약국)" on Naver Map or Kakao Map, or check in real time on the Emergency Medical Information Center portal (e-gen.or.kr)

3. Warning signs like a fever of 39°C/102°F or higher, severe pain, difficulty breathing, or fading consciousness → don't hesitate — call 119 or go to a 24-hour emergency room. If you have travel insurance, you can typically file a claim for treatment costs through your insurer.

> Why the 1→2→3 order matters: going to the ER for something mild means hours of waiting, while trying to tough out an emergency warning sign with convenience-store medicine is dangerous.

⑤ If You Need Prescription Medicine — A Doctor's Prescription Comes First

Prescription-only medicine, like antibiotics or strong pain relievers, can't be sold directly by a pharmacy. You need to see a doctor (at an internal medicine clinic, ENT clinic, etc.) and receive a prescription (cheobangjeon, 처방전) — your passport serves as ID at check-in. Take that prescription to any pharmacy and they'll fill it (joje, 조제 — "dispensing"). This guide doesn't recommend any specific hospital or clinic — just search "naegwa (내과)" — internal medicine — or "byeongwon (병원)" — hospital — on a map app and go to whichever is closest to your accommodation.

⑦ 3 Panic Scenarios — What Actually Happens, and How to Cope

1. The pharmacy is closed — Some are closed on Sundays and holidays, or have a break-time closure. Filter for "yeongeopjung (영업중)" — open now — on Naver Map or Kakao Map to find another pharmacy, or if it's mild, tide yourself over with convenience-store medicine (see ①).

2. The pharmacist barely speaks English — No need to panic. Hand over the card from ② and you don't need to say a word. For a symptom not on the card, show a Papago voice or text translation instead.

3. You don't know the exact name of the medicine you want — Just describing the symptom (or showing the card) is enough — the pharmacist will recommend something. Korean pharmacies default to symptom-based recommendations, so not knowing a product name is never a problem.
