Korean Han (恨) Emotion — Where Does That Deep Sorrow in K-Drama Come From?

> Han (恨)·Sakim (삭임)·Seunghwa (승화)·Heung (興)·Jeong (情) — A Korean emotional explainer for foreigners. A balanced look at "the true nature of that deep sorrow," including its meaning, history, and ★conceptual debates.

When binge-watching K-dramas, foreign viewers often leave comments like, "Why are Korean works so deep and sad?" The desperate rage of Squid Game, the boiling powerlessness of Parasite, the pent-up revenge of Oldboy, the heartfelt emotion of ballads, even a single verse of han (恨) from a pansori (판소리) master singer — Anglophone critics, trying to explain this emotion, always arrive at the same single word: han (恨). It's often translated as "deeply accumulated sorrow, injustice, or resentment," but many say no single English word (sorrow, resentment, grief) can fully capture it. However, there's a twist that most foreigners are unaware of: a heated debate within Korean academia about whether han (恨) is truly an inherent essence of the Korean people. This article does not mystify han (恨) as "Koreans' sad DNA." Instead, it explores its meaning, history, and methods of sublimation, while also examining the ★criticism that the concept of han (恨) itself was constructed during the Japanese colonial period. This is Part 7 of the Korean Culture explainer series.

★ At a Glance — The Trio of Han (恨), Heung (興), and Jeong (情)

When discussing Korean emotions, looking only at han (恨) is seeing only half the picture. Scholars view han (恨), heung (興), and jeong (情) as organically linked emotions.

Emotion Core Meaning One-liner Key Expressions
Han (恨) Accumulated sorrow/resentment An emotion formed by long-settled feelings of injustice and longing Pansori (판소리) Gyemyeonjo (계면조), the poignant sadness of Arirang (아리랑)
Heung (興) Exuberance/Surging joy Joy that "releases" sorrow — also interpreted as the sublimation of han (恨) Samulnori (사물놀이), ttechang (떼창), makchum (막춤)
Jeong (情) Strong bond/attachment Affection that slowly builds between people (→ [#1135 Korean Jeong (情) Culture](1135_korean_jeong_culture.md)) "Did you eat?", Sharing

> Key: Han (恨) is not a "sadness that makes you collapse" but is talked about as one pillar of an emotional cluster that is released through heung (興) and endured through jeong (情). To say "Koreans are only sad" is a misunderstanding that completely misses this pairing.

⚠️ 3 Common Misconceptions Foreigners Often Have

1. "Han (恨) = just deep sadness?" ❌ — Han (恨) is not simple sadness; it's described as a complex emotion where sorrow, injustice, longing, and anger have long settled and solidified into a lump. The core is the attitude of not erupting it but slowly digesting and maturing it internally, enduring it, and ultimately seeking to sublimate it. It's considered different from resignation.

2. "Han (恨) = Koreans are a gloomy people?" ❌ — Generalizing han (恨) directly to "the gloomy essence of Koreans" is risky. Within the same Korean emotional spectrum, heung (興, exuberance) and jeong (情, communal bond) exist as a pair, and above all, the notion that 'han (恨) equals national character' is itself a subject of academic debate (see ⑥ below). The daily lives of Koreans are filled not only with sorrow but also with heung (興) and jeong (情).

3. "Han (恨) is something every Korean feels, all the time?" ❌ — While often discussed this way, it's difficult to assert that han (恨) is an emotion "all Koreans always hold." There are significant generational and individual differences, and many point out that younger generations, especially the MZ generation (a term used in Korea to refer to millennials and Generation Z, typically those born between the early 1980s and early 2000s), rarely use the word han (恨). This is why most expressions in this article are "is often said to be...".

① What is Han (恨)? — 'Maetchim (맺힘)' and 'Sakim (삭임)'

Han (恨) is an emotion difficult to define in a single word. The Encyclopedia of Korean Culture (한국민족문화대백과사전) and other sources generally define han (恨) as: a mind where desires and will are frustrated, and the resulting life crises and wounds are deeply entangled and solidified in the conscious and unconscious for a long time. There are two key verbs:

  • Maetchim (맺힘): The process where feelings of injustice, longing, and sorrow are unable to be resolved and solidify internally into a lump.
  • Sakim (삭임): The process of slowly settling and maturing that lump internally instead of letting it erupt outwards. It's a word with the same nuance as the "fermentation (sakim, 삭힘)" of Korean food (→ [#1134 Fermentation and Jang (醬) Culture](1134_fermentation_jang_culture.md)).

Therefore, han (恨) is often described not as passive resignation, but as active endurance that tolerates and digests, hoping to resolve it someday (i.e., sublimate). However, han (恨) is also interpreted as the condensation of anger — deep anger over an unresolvable situation accumulates internally, to the extent that Korea even has a concept called hwabyung (火病) (a Korean culture-bound syndrome characterized by physical symptoms resulting from suppressed anger and frustration), which views this as a physical symptom. In essence, han (恨) is a broad emotion that oscillates between 'sorrow' and 'suppressed anger.'

② Historical Context — Why Do We Talk About 'Han (恨)'?

When discussing han (恨), Korea's arduous history is often cited as the background.

  • Geopolitically surrounded by powerful nations, Korea experienced frequent foreign invasions.
  • In the 20th century, it underwent Japanese colonial rule (1910–1945), the Korean War (1950–1953), division, and extreme poverty in the post-war period.
  • The frustrated han (恨) of the vulnerable (women, commoners) under the class system and patriarchal system is also frequently mentioned.

This collective trauma and frustration left traces on individual and communal emotions, and their artistic expression is explained as han (恨). However, a caution: this narrative of "history of hardship → sad national character" is itself a key target of academic criticism, as seen in section ⑥. While historical hardship is a fact, the logic that directly reduces it to "Koreans' inherent emotion" requires separate verification.

③ Sublimation — Releasing Han (恨) Through 'Art' (Pansori (판소리) and Arirang (아리랑))

What makes han (恨) interesting is that it doesn't stop at sadness but sublimates into art. There's a trend that explains the aesthetics of traditional Korean art through han (恨), heung (興), and jeong (情).

  • Pansori (판소리): A pansori (판소리) singer relies solely on a drum beat to sing for several hours. The Gyemyeonjo (계면조) melody, which expresses sorrow, represents the emotion of han (恨). The gugak (국악, traditional Korean music) community describes singers as "soothing their lives and slowly digesting their deep sorrow through solo singing", and this "sound of sakim (삭임)" is precisely how han (恨) is released.
  • Arirang (아리랑): Korea's representative folk song. The poignancy of "My beloved who leaves me" is often cited as an expression of han (恨). However, Arirang (아리랑) should not be viewed one-sidedly — scholars consider Arirang (아리랑) a song that encompasses all three emotions: soothing han (恨) in adversity, longing for jeong (情), and drawing out heung (興). It is a song of sorrow, but also a song for communal singing and release.
  • Transition to Heung (興): Aesthetics like samulnori (사물놀이) (a genre of traditional Korean percussion music performed with four instruments) and ttechang (떼창) (group singing, often spontaneously, at concerts or public events), which transform settled han (恨) into shinmyeong (신명, exuberance) (heung, 흥), form a complementary pair. The interpretation that "Heung (興) is the sublimation of han (恨)" stems from this.

In Korean art, han (恨) is strongly characterized as "something to be resolved," not "something to succumb to."

④ The Pair with Heung (興) and Jeong (情) — Misunderstanding Han (恨) in Isolation

To reiterate, han (恨) does not exist in isolation. It is said that all three emotions operate together within the same person and culture.

  • When frustration and longing accumulate, they solidify into han (恨).
  • When that is released through song, dance, and play, it becomes heung (興).
  • The strong bond of enduring together with people is jeong (情) (→ [#1135 Korean Jeong (情) Culture](1135_korean_jeong_culture.md)).

If foreigners only perceive "deep sorrow" in K-content and conclude that Korea is a 'sad country,' they will completely miss the heung (興) of group singing (ttechang, 떼창) and the jeong (情) of a restaurant owner (ajumma, 아주머니) that fill Koreans' daily lives. Han (恨) is just one color in that larger picture.

⑤ 'Han (恨)' in K-Content — A Frequent Keyword for Foreign Critics

In Western criticism, han (恨) is a common keyword used to explain the emotions in Korean films and dramas. However, remember that this is 'a framework for interpreting works,' not the sentiment of all Koreans.

  • Squid Game: Critics interpret the core emotion of this work as han (恨) — anger and powerlessness in the face of an inescapable structure, not colonialism but capitalism. Creator Hwang Dong-hyuk translated the despair of society's bottom into a game, and many interpret this emotion as connecting with han (恨).
  • Parasite and Oldboy: Bong Joon-ho and Park Chan-wook's films are praised for their han (恨) being "palpably alive" through "boiling anger and self-loathing" and the pent-up revenge that finds no outlet. One critic wrote, "In their world, han (恨) is always larger than man."
  • Ballads and Trot (트로트): The heartfelt emotion of K-pop ballads and the han (恨)-filled vocal techniques of trot (트로트) (a genre of Korean popular music known for its distinctive two-beat rhythm and often sentimental lyrics) are also frequently mentioned as popular expressions of han (恨).

> A Balanced View: This "han (恨) lens" enriches the reading of works, but it can also easily become a cliché that reduces all Korean content to sadness. Korea also has plenty of comedic, romantic, and healing content.

⑥ ★ Debates Surrounding the Concept of 'Han (恨)' — Is It Really the Essence of Koreans?

This is the most important part of this article. While most foreign sources omit it, the notion that "han (恨) = the Korean soul" is strongly debated in academia. This counter-argument must be understood before mystifying han (恨).

Core Criticism: The concept of 'han (恨) = national emotion' that we use today may actually be a construct formed and exaggerated during the Japanese colonial period.

  • "Beauty of Sorrow" (비애의 미) Controversy: Japanese art critic Yanagi Muneyoshi (柳宗悅, Yanagi Sōetsu) defined Korean art as "the beauty of sorrow." Critics argue that this perspective aligns with the colonial gaze that justified Japanese rule — "Joseon (조선) (the last dynastic kingdom of Korea) is inherently a pitiful and passive nation."
  • "Descendant of Colonial Stereotypes": Some scholars even argue that today's concept of han (恨) is "a postcolonial translation of a Japanese colonial construct" (e.g., the academic paper "Korean Han and the Postcolonial Afterlives of 'The Beauty of Sorrow'"). That is, the self-definition of "Koreans are a people full of han (恨)" may paradoxically be the result of Koreans internalizing a framework injected by colonial Japan.
  • "Modern Myth": Scholar Minsoo Kang and others criticize the narrative that "a history of foreign invasions created the sad character of Koreans" as a modern myth that is still circulated. They also point out that the stereotype of viewing Koreans as "emotional and quick-tempered" stems from the justification of colonial rule (essay "Against 'han'").

There is also another perspective: On the other hand, scholars like Korean literature scholar Kim Yeol-gyu (김열규) (e.g., Hanmaekwonryu (恨脈怨流)), have deeply researched han (恨) as a real tradition in Korean literature and emotion. Thus, the position that "han (恨) existed since pre-modern times" clashes with the position that "it was invented and exaggerated during the colonial period." It is difficult to assert that either side is the complete truth.

> Therefore, the conclusion: Han (恨) is a useful emotional keyword for interpreting Korean art, but it should not be accepted as essentialism that "Koreans are inherently a sad people." That is precisely the stereotype that academia warns against. When you meet Koreans, you will much more often see heung (興) and jeong (情) than han (恨).

⑦ Modern Day — Is 'Han (恨)' Still Alive?

What is the place of han (恨) in Korea today?

  • Generational Gap: It is a tangible emotion for older generations who directly experienced colonialism, war, and poverty, but many point out that the MZ generation, who grew up in prosperity, rarely use the word han (恨) itself. Han (恨) is gradually shifting towards being a 'historical and aesthetic concept.'
  • Resource for K-Content: At the same time, han (恨) is being re-evaluated as a source of emotional depth that allows K-dramas, films, and music to resonate globally. The worldwide empathy for Parasite and Squid Game is convincingly interpreted as being due to han (恨) connecting not just with 'Korean sadness' but with universal emotions like inequality and frustration.
  • Reinterpretation: In academia, instead of essentializing han (恨), a trend has taken root that critically examines even its formation process. Such a balanced understanding is recommended for foreigners as well.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) for Foreigners

  • Can 'han (恨)' be translated into a single English word? — It's difficult. Sorrow, resentment, grief, and unresolved longing overlap somewhat, but none are complete. That's why it's often simply written as han in English-speaking contexts.
  • Do all Koreans live with 'han (恨)'? — You shouldn't generalize that way. While often discussed as a Korean emotion, there are significant generational and individual differences, and younger generations rarely use the word. "Koreans = a sad people" is a stereotype criticized by academia.
  • So, are K-dramas sad because of 'han (恨)'? — It's just an interpretive framework often used in criticism; Korean content is also full of comedies, romances, and healing stories. The "han (恨) lens" is just one perspective.
  • Are 'han (恨)' and 'hwabyung (火病)' the same? — No, they are different. While han (恨) is an emotional and aesthetic concept, hwabyung (火病) is a unique Korean clinical concept that views suppressed anger as manifesting in physical symptoms. It connects with the 'pent-up anger' aspect of han (恨).
  • Can I experience 'han (恨)' during my trip? — As it's an emotion, it's not something you can 'tour,' but you can feel its essence by attending pansori (판소리) performances, gugak (국악), watching Korean films, or listening to Arirang (아리랑). More importantly, the heung (興) and jeong (情) you encounter in restaurants and alleyways are the more everyday faces of Korean emotion.
