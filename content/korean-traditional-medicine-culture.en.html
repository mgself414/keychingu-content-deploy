Korean Traditional Medicine — Boyak, Acupuncture & Moxibustion, Korea's Own System of Healing

> Hanuiwon (한의원, oriental clinic — a system separate from Western medicine) · Acupuncture · Moxibustion · Cupping · Hanyak (한약, herbal medicine) · Boyak (보약, restorative herbal medicine) · Sasang Constitutional Medicine · Donguibogam (동의보감) — a Korean traditional-medicine explainer for foreigners. Not "herbal medicine is good for you," but rather: "Why does Korea have a hanuiwon (한의원, oriental clinic) standing apart from the regular hospital, why do Koreans try to manage the body through 'balance,' and how does that work alongside modern medicine?"

Walk down a street in Korea and you'll spot a sign that makes foreigners tilt their heads. Right next to a regular hospital (uiwon, 의원, clinic), there's another sign that also offers medical treatment — but it reads hanuiwon (한의원, oriental clinic). Step inside and you'll find a doctor in a white coat taking your pulse, placing needles (침, ch'im, acupuncture needle) in your body, burning mugwort for moxibustion (뜸, tteum, moxibustion), and prescribing a bitter herbal remedy (한약, hanyak, herbal medicine). The foreigner's first question is almost always the same — "Is this a hospital, or folk medicine?"

The answer is neither. Korea's hanuihak (한의학, Korean traditional medicine) is a medical system that is legally equal to, yet separate from, Western medicine. A doctor of Korean medicine (hanuisa, 한의사, doctor of Korean medicine) graduates from a six-year university program and passes a national board exam — a licensed medical practitioner — and the hanuiwon (한의원, oriental clinic) is an institution covered by national health insurance. This article isn't about "which oriental hospital is good" (→ that's a place guide). Instead, it unpacks why Koreans see the body in terms of 'balance,' what Korean medicine treats and how, and how it runs in parallel with modern medicine. We won't make sweeping claims about efficacy; we'll honestly convey traditional understanding and the current state of research. This is Part 74 of the Korean Culture explainer series.

⚠️ 3 Things Foreigners Often Get Wrong

1. "Hanuiwon (한의원, oriental clinic) = unlicensed folk remedy" ❌ — In Korea, Korean medicine is licensed care, equal to Western medicine. A hanuisa (한의사, doctor of Korean medicine) must graduate from a six-year college of Korean medicine (or graduate school) and pass a national exam, and the hanuiwon (한의원, oriental clinic) is a proper medical institution covered by national health insurance. It's different from unlicensed street procedures.

2. "Herbal medicine is 'natural,' so it must be totally safe" ❌ — 'Natural' doesn't equal 'harmless.' Herbal remedies (hanyak, 한약, herbal medicine) can carry risks of constitutional mismatch, drug interactions, and liver burden, so if you're taking any Western medication, it's safest to tell both your doctor of Korean medicine and your physician. Pregnant people and those with chronic conditions should especially consult before taking anything.

3. "Korean medicine vs. Western medicine — you have to pick one" ❌ — Most Koreans use both, in alternation. It's common to combine them — Western medicine for acute illness, surgery, and tests; Korean medicine for chronic pain, recovery, and constitutional care. The real picture is not opposition but concurrent use (병용, byeongyong, combined use).

① What Is Korean Medicine — Not "Killing the Disease" but "Tuning the Body"

Korean medicine starts from a different premise than Western medicine. Where Western medicine often focuses on finding and removing the pathogen or lesion, Korean medicine sees illness as arising when the body's overall balance (균형, gyunhyeong, balance) is disrupted. The core concepts that explain that balance are yin-yang (음양, eumyang, yin and yang) and gi (기, vital energy).

  • Yin-yang (음양, eumyang): The body is viewed through the balance of opposing pairs — cold/hot, inside/outside, deficiency (허, heo, deficiency) / excess (실, sil, excess). Tip too far to one side, and illness follows.
  • Gi (기, vital energy): The life energy that circulates through the body. When gi is blocked or weak, it is thought to surface as symptoms like pain, fatigue, or indigestion.

So even for the same 'headache,' a hanuisa (한의사, doctor of Korean medicine) looks at the patient's constitution, complexion, pulse, and lifestyle and approaches each person differently. The key point is that it's not "one disease name, one drug," but *"what in this person has tipped out of balance." This thinking also extends into yaksik-dongwon (약식동원, food and medicine share the same root) — "medicine and food come from the same source" — connecting to the Korean culture of nourishment (boyang, 보양, nourishing the body), the idea of managing the body through everyday eating (→ see #1198 for the flagship root of boyang*).

> ※ Yin-yang and gi are the theoretical framework (explanatory model) of Korean medicine, and differ from modern biological measurement concepts. Here we introduce them as a cultural lens on "how Koreans view the body."

② Acupuncture, Moxibustion & Cupping — The First Korean Medicine Foreigners Meet

You've probably seen these scenes at least once in a K-drama or period drama. All three are treatments that stimulate the body's surface rather than relying on ingested medicine.

  • Acupuncture (침, 鍼, ch'im): Needles as thin as a hair are placed at specific points called acupoints (경혈, gyeonghyeol, acupoints) to regulate the flow of gi. Traditionally it's widely used for pain, the musculoskeletal system, and stiffness. It's also the most widely researched and practiced Korean medicine treatment worldwide.
  • Moxibustion (뜸, 灸, tteum): Dried mugwort (쑥, ssuk, mugwort) is bundled and burned to warmly stimulate acupoints. It can be done directly near the skin (direct moxibustion) or indirectly (indirect moxibustion). Traditionally used for cold sensitivity, digestion, and chronic fatigue.
  • Cupping (부항, 附缸, buhang): A glass or plastic cup is made into a vacuum and applied to the skin to stimulate circulation in that area. It leaves round marks where removed — known abroad from athletes who apply it to their shoulders and backs. Traditionally used for shoulder/back stiffness and knots.

These procedures should be done hygienically at a qualified hanuiwon (한의원, oriental clinic). Practices like using single-use sterile needles are upheld at proper medical institutions.

> In practice: If a foreigner is experiencing Korean medicine for the first time, acupuncture and cupping have a low barrier to entry. People often get them for pain, travel fatigue, or stiffness. But if you have bleeding issues, are pregnant, or have skin conditions, be sure to mention it beforehand.

③ Herbal Medicine and Boyak — A Culture of Nourishment in a Packet of Bitter Tonic

When you think of Korean medicine, you picture a bitter, thick brown decoction. Herbal medicine (hanyak, 한약, herbal medicine) is several herbs decocted according to a prescription, taking forms like decoction (탕약, tangyak, herbal decoction — the boiled liquid), pills (환, hwan, herbal pill), or powder. You usually receive it as cheopyak (첩약, packeted herbal dose), packaged one dose at a time.

Among herbal remedies, the one most familiar to Koreans is boyak (보약, restorative herbal medicine). Rather than directly attacking a disease, boyak is a prescription focused on replenishing one's energy (보, bo, to replenish) and restoring the body. A classic example is Kyungokgo (경옥고, Kyungokgo — a restorative formula), a nourishing formula using ginseng, bokryeong (복령, poria mushroom), and honey, passed down since the Joseon (조선, Joseon Dynasty) era. Koreans especially seek boyak during the change of seasons when energy dips, before big exams or for students, postpartum recovery, and the sweltering summer — summer's rising consumption of nourishing foods and boyak stems from the belief that you replenish (보, bo) a body worn out by the heat (summer nourishment and boknal (복날, the dog days of summer) culture pairs well with the #1198 ginseng/red ginseng explainer).

> ※ The efficacy of boyak and herbal medicine is based on traditional understanding and clinical experience, and the level of scientific proof for any individual formula varies by prescription and symptom. The key is not "take it and you'll definitely get better," but that a doctor of Korean medicine prescribes it to fit your constitution and condition and monitors your progress. If you have medications you're taking or an existing condition, always disclose it.

④ Sasang Constitutional Medicine — A Uniquely Korean Medicine of "Everyone Has a Different Constitution"

Even within Korean medicine, a theory Korea developed independently is Sasang Constitutional Medicine (사상체질의학, sasang-chejil-uihak, four-constitution medicine; also 사상의학, sasang-uihak). The late-Joseon physician Lee Je-ma (이제마, Lee Je-ma) systematized it in his work "Donguisusebowon" (동의수세보원, Longevity and Life Preservation in Eastern Medicine) (written in 1894, published in 1901).

The core is that "people are born into one of four constitutions, and each constitution has different bodily strengths and weaknesses, personality, and well-suited foods and medicines." Therefore, it holds that you shouldn't give the same drug for the same illness, but a prescription matched to the constitution.

  • Taeyangin (태양인, greater-yang type) · Soyangin (소양인, lesser-yang type) · Taeumin (태음인, greater-yin type) · Soeumin (소음인, lesser-yin type) — four constitutions in all.
  • Koreans are said to have the highest proportion of the Taeumin (태음인, greater-yin type).
  • The idea that you should also differ your lifestyle and dietary regimen (섭생, seopsaeng, health regimen) by constitution shows well the Korean-medicine philosophy of "looking at the whole body."

Sasang medicine is received with interest for having emphasized personalization early on, over "one standardized treatment," but the objective criteria for constitutional classification and the reproducibility of diagnosis remain subjects of ongoing academic research and debate. For foreigners, we recommend approaching it as a cultural curiosity — "Korea has a traditional medical theory that sees people as four types."

⑤ Donguibogam and the Medicine Market — Korean Medicine's 'Book' and 'Marketplace'

Korean-medicine culture has two symbolic pillars. One is a book, the other a marketplace.

  • Donguibogam (동의보감, Principles and Practice of Eastern Medicine): The greatest medical text of Joseon (조선, Joseon Dynasty), completed in 1610 by the royal physician Heo Jun (허준, Heo Jun) and published in 1613. It compiled the medical theory and prescriptions of its time into an original classification system, and is regarded as one of the most distinguished works in East Asian medical history. Its value was internationally recognized when it was inscribed on the UNESCO Memory of the World register in 2009. Featured many times in dramas and films, it's familiar to Koreans.
  • Seoul Yangnyeongsi / Gyeongdong Market (서울약령시·경동시장, Seoul Yangnyeongsi · Gyeongdong Market): Korea's largest specialized herbal-medicine market, formed around the Jegi-dong (제기동, Jegi-dong) area of Dongdaemun-gu, Seoul. It started as Gyeongdong Market in 1960, was officially named "Seoul Yangnyeongsi" in 1995, and is said to handle a large share of the nation's herbal-medicine distribution. Alleys piled mountain-high with all manner of herbs, the scent of medicinal ingredients, and rows of shops that decoct and package cheopyak (첩약, packeted herbal dose). The Seoul Korean Medicine Promotion Center and the Korean Medicine Museum are here too, making it a great place to see and learn about Korean medicine firsthand.

> In practice: If a foreigner wants to 'browse' the Korean-medicine atmosphere, a stroll through Seoul Yangnyeongsi (Jegi-dong) is a good choice. Rather than carelessly buying and ingesting herbs out of curiosity, we recommend experiencing them by sight and smell and going through a hanuiwon (한의원, oriental clinic) consultation before taking anything.

⑥ Running Alongside Modern Medicine, and the Debate Over Scientific Evidence (Objectively)

This is the part this article must handle most honestly. Korean medicine is popular and institutionalized care, but its efficacy is not all equally proven by modern science. Let's look at both sides in balance.

The fact that it runs in parallel within the system

  • Korea has a dual medical system (이원화, iwonhwa, dualization). Western-medicine doctors and doctors of Korean medicine each hold their own license, and hanuiwon (한의원, oriental clinic) and oriental hospitals are institutions covered by national health insurance (the 1951 National Medical Services Act laid the legal foundation for the dual system).
  • So Koreans use Korean and Western medicine as options, not as opposites — going Western for tests, surgery, and emergencies; Korean for chronic pain, recovery, and constitutional care.

But the scientific evidence varies by area

  • There's plenty of clinical research on acupuncture, moxibustion, and the like, but many studies within Korea are noted for methodological weaknesses such as small sample sizes, control-group design, and limits in follow-up observation, so the common assessment of systematic reviews is that there are many areas where it's hard to clearly conclude "it works / it doesn't."
  • For some pain and symptoms there are studies suggesting possibility, while in other areas results show a lack of convincing evidence. In short: "traditionally used" and "scientifically proven" are separate things.
  • Academia holds that more rigorous research and an integrative-medicine approach are needed.

So a balanced attitude means

  • Neither blindly trusting Korean medicine (overstating its efficacy) nor disparaging it (treating it wholesale as superstition). It's most accurate to accept together both the fact that it's functioning institutional care in Korean society and the fact that proof of efficacy is ongoing, area by area.

> A safety principle for foreigners: Experience Korean medicine, but ① get it at a qualified hanuiwon (한의원, oriental clinic), ② always disclose medications, existing conditions, and pregnancy, and ③ for acute or serious symptoms, prioritize Western medical care. Korean medicine isn't a replacement for Western medicine; viewing it as an option that goes alongside is the safest approach.

⑦ A Foreigner's Korean-Medicine Experience — How Best to Approach It

Korean medicine can be a distinctive cultural experience on a trip to Korea. Just approach it as a cultural/wellness experience rather than expecting 'treatment,' and there's no pressure.

  • A light introduction: A hanuiwon (한의원, oriental clinic) visit for acupuncture or cupping for travel fatigue, stiffness, or pain. Wellness-style experiences like herbal tea (hanbangcha, 한방차, Korean medicinal tea) and Korean-medicine spas (steam therapy) also have a low barrier to entry (herbal tea connects to the #1192 traditional-tea explainer).
  • Browsing/learning: See herbal ingredients and the history of Korean medicine with your own eyes at Seoul Yangnyeongsi and the Korean Medicine Museum. An experience of looking and smelling rather than buying to consume.
  • Must-dos: ① at a proper hanuiwon, ② check whether interpretation or English is available, ③ disclose allergies, medications, pregnancy, and existing conditions in advance, ④ be wary of exaggerated ads that make sweeping efficacy claims.

> In practice: Expecting Korean medicine to be a "mysterious cure-all" is a recipe for disappointment, and dismissing it as "superstition" means missing one thread of Korean culture. The mindset that fits best is: "I'm experiencing the Korean way of thinking that sees the body as balance."

❓ Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) for Foreigners

  • What's the difference between a hanuiwon and a regular hospital? — A hanuiwon (한의원, oriental clinic) is a Korean-medicine institution that manages the body's balance with acupuncture, moxibustion, cupping, and herbal medicine, while a regular clinic/hospital is a modern Western medicine institution. In Korea both are licensed care covered by national health insurance, and many Koreans use them in parallel.
  • Is herbal medicine safe? — Being 'natural' doesn't make it unconditionally safe. There can be risks of constitutional mismatch, drug interactions, and liver burden, so it's safest to tell your hanuisa (한의사, doctor of Korean medicine) and physician about your medications, existing conditions, and pregnancy. Prescription and management are key.
  • Does acupuncture hurt? — Needles are very thin, so strong pain is generally uncommon, it's said. But sensation differs by person and area, so if it's your first time, talk it over with the hanuisa in advance about whether you can receive it comfortably.
  • Does Korean medicine really work? — It varies by area. For some pain and symptoms there are studies suggesting possibility, but there are many areas where scientific proof is insufficient or still ongoing. Remember that "traditionally used" and "proven" are separate, and for serious symptoms it's safest to prioritize Western medical care.
  • Can foreigners receive Korean medicine? — Yes. Just check in advance for a hanuiwon (한의원, oriental clinic) with English or interpretation available, and disclose your allergies, medications, and existing conditions. Acupuncture, cupping, and herbal-tea experiences are a smooth introduction.
