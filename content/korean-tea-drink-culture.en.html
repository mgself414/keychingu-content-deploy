Sikhye, Sujeonggwa & Korean Teas — The Non-Alcoholic Traditional Drinks Koreans Actually Love

> Sikhye (식혜), Sujeonggwa (수정과), herbal teas, fruit teas, grain teas — A Korean non-alcoholic traditional drink culture explainer for foreigners. Beyond soju and makgeolli, this is the 'why, what, and how' of the drinks Koreans have sipped as dessert, medicine, and everyday water.

Korean liquor (soju, makgeolli) is fairly well-known to foreigners. But sit down at a Korean restaurant and, without ordering anything, you'll get free brown water that's either hot or cold; at the end of a holiday meal, a sweet drink with rice grains floating in it appears; and at a hanok (한옥, traditional house) teahouse in an Insadong (인사동) alley, you're served a bright-red, tart tea topped with pine nuts. This is all part of Korea's non-alcoholic traditional drink world. Koreans have drunk tea (차, cha) and traditional beverages as part of daily life, holidays, and medicine just as much as they drink alcohol. Sikhye (식혜, sweet rice drink) is a jjimjilbang (찜질방, Korean spa) staple, sujeonggwa (수정과, cinnamon-persimmon punch) is a Lunar New Year dessert, daechucha (대추차, jujube tea) and saenggangcha (생강차, ginger tea) are the 'medicinal teas' drunk for colds, and boricha (보리차, barley tea) and oksususcha (옥수수차, corn tea) are lifelong drinks consumed in place of water. This article isn't "which teahouse is good" (→ cross-link), but rather explains what these drinks are and why people drink them, from a foreigner's perspective. This is a Korean culture explainer.

⚠️ 3 Things Foreigners Often Misunderstand

1. "The brown water at restaurants is tea? And it's free?" ✅ — Yes. At Korean restaurants, instead of water you get free, unlimited refills of boricha (보리차) or oksususcha (옥수수차) — grain teas made by roasting and boiling barley or corn. They have almost no caffeine, so daycares and kindergartens serve them like water. The aroma isn't strong, so even if you think "this tastes like plain water?", that's normal — it's a nutty everyday drink.

2. "Korean 'tea' must all be green tea" ❌ — In Korea, tea (차, cha) isn't just green tea. Grains (barley, corn), fruits (citron, quince), roots and berries (ginger, jujube), and herbal-medicine ingredients (ssanghwa, 쌍화) — anything steeped or boiled is all called 'tea.' Most of these contain no tea leaves at all.

3. "Isn't sikhye (식혜) similar to makgeolli (막걸리, rice wine)?" ❌ — Both involve rice and fermentation, but sikhye (식혜) is non-alcoholic. It's a sweet drink made by fermenting cooked rice with malt, and contains virtually no alcohol. It's a holiday dessert and jjimjilbang (찜질방) drink, not liquor.

① What Are Korean Traditional Drinks — The Line Between 'Medicine' and 'Dessert'

The biggest characteristic of Korean traditional drinks is the idea of "let's nourish the body (補, bo) while drinking something delicious." Sources from the Korea Heritage Service (국가유산청) and Korean-cuisine references name sujeonggwa (수정과) and sikhye (식혜) as Korea's representative traditional drinks, citing as their common point "using body-friendly herbal-medicine ingredients or grains to bring out flavor and aroma while nourishing the body." That's why many traditional drinks have two faces at once: after-meal (dessert) and medicine (藥, yak).

  • Dessert drinks: sweet drinks like sikhye (식혜) and sujeonggwa (수정과) that aid digestion after rich holiday food.
  • Medicinal teas (약차, yakcha): herbal teas like daechu (대추), saenggang (생강), yuja (유자), mogwa (모과), and ssanghwa (쌍화), drunk 'as medicine' for colds, fatigue, and digestion.
  • Everyday drinks: grain teas like boricha (보리차) and oksususcha (옥수수차), drunk constantly in place of water.

In the West, drinks are usually a matter of 'taste and preference,' but Korean traditional drinks are tied to season, body condition, and holidays. Ginger tea when it's cold, citron tea for a cold, sikhye (식혜) and sujeonggwa (수정과) on holidays — there's a grammar of choosing what to drink based on the situation.

② Sikhye & Sujeonggwa — Two Desserts of the Holiday Table

Two drinks that almost always appear at the end of a holiday (Seollal, 설날 / Chuseok, 추석) meal. They're the first traditional drinks most foreigners encounter.

Sikhye (식혜) — Floating Rice Grains, a Sweet Rice Drink

  • What it is: a non-alcoholic sweet drink made by fermenting cooked rice with yeotgireum (엿기름, malted barley flour / malt). Finished sikhye (식혜) has fermented rice grains floating on top, and pine nuts are sometimes added.
  • Taste: a subtle, smooth sweetness. The aroma isn't strong, so it's the signature drink even foreigners enjoy without hesitation.
  • Why it's a dessert: the saccharifying enzyme in malt (amylase) aids digestion, so it's been drunk after eating lots of rich holiday food.
  • Where you'll find it: holiday tables, a jjimjilbang (찜질방) staple (paired with boiled eggs), and commonly sold in cans and PET bottles at marts.

Sujeonggwa (수정과) — A Spicy Cinnamon-Ginger Punch

  • What it is: a deep reddish-brown drink made by boiling cinnamon and ginger, sweetening with sugar or honey, and floating gotgam (곶감, dried persimmon) and pine nuts.
  • Taste: the spicy, fragrant kick of ginger and cinnamon + sweetness. It has more bite than sikhye (식혜).
  • When: a winter / Lunar New Year drink served cold. Because it needs dried persimmons, it's made from late autumn on.
  • Point: even as a 'holiday dessert,' sikhye (식혜) is a smooth sweetness while sujeonggwa (수정과) is a spicy punch — opposite charms.

> One-line comparison: Sikhye (식혜) = smooth and sweet (rice grains). Sujeonggwa (수정과) = spicy and fragrant (cinnamon, ginger, dried persimmon). Both are non-alcoholic desserts.

③ Herbal Teas — Teas Drunk 'Like Medicine' (Jujube, Ginger, Citron, Quince, Ssanghwa)

The most distinctive point in Korean drink culture: drinking tea as medicine. VisitKorea even covers hanbang tea (한방차, herbal medicine tea) as immunity and wellness content — that's how everyday the sense of 'if something feels off, drink the matching tea' is in Korea.

Herbal tea Taste Commonly 'for when'
Daechucha (대추차) Rich sweetness, mild Fatigue, stress, trouble sleeping
Saenggangcha (생강차) Hot-spicy, warm Colds, body aches, digestion, cold days
Yujacha (유자차) Sweet-and-sour (citrus) Cold prevention, scratchy throat, vitamin C
Mogwacha (모과차) Fragrant, slightly astringent Cough, phlegm, digestion
Ssanghwacha (쌍화차) Strong herbal-medicine aroma Energy recovery, nourishment (fatigue)
  • Daechucha (대추차, jujube tea): made by simmering dried jujubes for a long time (sometimes 8 hours on low heat). The sweetness is rich, and it's been traditionally drunk for insomnia and stress relief.
  • Saenggangcha (생강차, ginger tea): a spicy, body-warming winter standby. It's considered good for digestion and restoring vitality.
  • Yujacha (유자차, citron tea): yuja (유자, a citrus cousin of the small grapefruit) is made into a cheong (청, sugar preserve) and dissolved in hot water. With three times the vitamin C of a lemon, it's the national drink for cold prevention and throat health.
  • Mogwacha (모과차, quince tea): a tea steeped from fragrant mogwa (모과, quince). Traditionally used for the bronchi, coughs, and phlegm.
  • Ssanghwacha (쌍화차, herbal tonic tea): the name means "ssanghwa (쌍화, 雙和) = harmony of two energies." It's a rich tonic tea made by decocting several herbal-medicine ingredients like Korean angelica, rehmannia, cinnamon, peony, and licorice. Opinions split, but it's the ultimate 'medicinal tea you drink when tired.' Some teahouses serve it topped with an egg yolk or pine nuts.

> Key: these teas are often chosen by 'effect, season, and body condition' rather than 'taste.' When a Korean friend recommends "yujacha (유자차) for a cold" or "ssanghwacha (쌍화차) when you're tired," that's this grammar.

④ Fruit & Berry Tea — Omija, the Five Flavors

Omijacha (오미자차, five-flavor berry tea) is like the 'mascot' of Korean traditional drinks. Omija (오미자, 五味子) literally means the 'five-flavor berry' — a red berry (omija vine, magnolia berry) that packs sweet, sour, salty, bitter, and spicy flavors into a single fruit.

  • Taste: it turns astringent if boiled, so it's traditionally made by steeping it in cold water for a long time (naengchim, 냉침, cold infusion). The result is a bright-red, tart drink. The sourness stands out most.
  • When: a cold drink especially enjoyed chilled in summer. It's served with pine nuts on top, and mixed with pear into hwachae (화채, fruit punch).
  • Meaning: because "it has all five flavors," it's often used as a metaphor that it resembles Korea itself. It's also used in herbal medicine, with a tradition of linking it to the five viscera (오장, 五臟).
  • Where: a signature of traditional teahouses in Insadong (인사동) and Bukchon (북촌). A cup of omijacha (오미자차) with floating pine nuts is the essence of the hanok (한옥) teahouse experience.

(Yuja and mogwa are also fruit teas, but they have a stronger 'medicinal tea' character, so they're placed in ③. Omija is more of a beverage / traditional tea, so it's set apart here.)

⑤ Everyday Grain Teas — Boricha & Oksususcha, Drunk Instead of Water

The drink foreigners are most confused by yet encounter most often. It's the brown drink served instead of water at Korean restaurants and homes.

  • Boricha (보리차, barley tea): a nutty tea made by boiling roasted barley. Zero caffeine, considered good for digestion. It's offered free with unlimited refills, like water, at restaurants, and boiled at home to keep in the fridge. In Korea, it takes the place of water.
  • Oksususcha (옥수수차, corn tea): the subtle sweetness of roasted corn. The corn's sweetness balances barley's mild bitterness, so a mixed barley + corn tea is also common. Golden in color, savory and a touch sweet.
  • Why instead of water?: the aroma is barely there, so it doesn't clash with food, and with no caffeine it can be drunk all day from children to the elderly. Daycares and kindergartens serve it in place of water.

> Foreigner tip: the brown water at restaurants is free. Don't panic at "why is the water this color?" — just drink it. It's nutty boricha (보리차) or oksususcha (옥수수차).

⑥ Traditional Teahouses — Where to Drink It 'Properly'

If you want to enjoy traditional drinks with the atmosphere too, a hanok (한옥) traditional teahouse is the answer.

  • Insadong (인사동): every alley is packed with hanok (한옥) and antique-furniture traditional teahouses. Some old-school teahouses have been running since the 1970s. The classic order is omijacha (오미자차), daechucha (대추차), or yujacha (유자차) + hangwa (한과, traditional sweets) as a set.
  • Bukchon Hanok Village (북촌): enjoy both traditional and modern teas at teahouses and cafes converted from hanok (한옥). A quiet vibe, sipping while gazing at the courtyard.
  • What to order: if it's your first time, try omijacha (오미자차, tart and memorable) or daechucha (대추차) / yujacha (유자차, an easy sweetness). Save ssanghwacha (쌍화차) for a 'medicinal tea challenge,' since the herbal aroma is strong.
  • What comes with it: pine nuts (floating in almost every traditional tea) and hangwa (한과, traditional sweets like yakgwa and gangjeong). Tea + hangwa makes a set.

> The MZ generation also enjoys traditional tea reinterpreted at trendy hanok (한옥) cafes (traditional tea + bingsu / desserts). For the cafe-culture context, see → #1158 Cafe Republic.

⑦ Practical Guide for Foreigners — How to Enjoy It

> Prices and hours are estimates as of 2026 and vary by venue — confirming before you visit is recommended.

  • At restaurants: the brown water (boricha / oksususcha) is free, self-serve refill. Hot and cold versions exist. Just drink it like water.
  • At marts / convenience stores: buy sikhye (식혜) and sujeonggwa (수정과) cans/PET (about ₩1,500–3,000), or yujacha / saenggangcha as a cheong (청, jar) to dissolve in hot water. Teabags of boricha and oksususcha are common too. The cheapest, easiest entry point.
  • At traditional teahouses: about ₩7,000–12,000 (about $5–9) per cup (varies by venue). Sets with hangwa (한과) too. Insadong (인사동) and Bukchon (북촌) are easy to reach.
  • At jjimjilbang (찜질방): the sikhye (식혜) + boiled egg set is an almost ritual combo — be sure to try it.
  • First-try order: ① sikhye (식혜) (sweet and mild, no fail) → ② yujacha (유자차) (familiar sweet-and-sour) → ③ omijacha (오미자차) (bright red, five flavors, memorable) → ④ sujeonggwa (수정과) / ssanghwacha (쌍화차) (spicy / herbal, a challenge).
  • Manners & tips: the pine nuts in traditional tea are a garnish meant to be eaten along with the drink, so don't pick them out. Cold or hot is up to you.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions for Foreigners (FAQ)

  • Does sikhye (식혜) contain alcohol? — Virtually none. It's a fermented rice drink, but it's non-alcoholic. Children drink it too.
  • The brown water at restaurants — do I have to pay? — No. Boricha (보리차) / oksususcha (옥수수차) come free with unlimited refills by default.
  • Is there caffeine? — Most grain teas (barley, corn), fruit teas, and herbal teas have no caffeine or very little. Light enough to drink in the evening.
  • Ssanghwacha (쌍화차) tastes too much like herbal medicine. Is that normal? — Yes. It's a tonic tea brewed from several herbal-medicine ingredients, so opinions vary widely. If it's too much, go with daechucha (대추차) or yujacha (유자차).
  • Yujacha (유자차) and saenggangcha (생강차) are teas — why drink them 'like medicine'? — In Korea there's a culture of drinking tea to match season and body condition. Citron for a cold, ginger when it's cold — they're often chosen by 'effect and situation' rather than 'taste.'
  • Can I buy them to take home? — Yuja cheong, ginger cheong, sikhye / sujeonggwa cans, and boricha / oksususcha teabags are popular gifts and souvenirs. They're light and easy to store.
