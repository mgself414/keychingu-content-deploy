Korean temple food culture — Why is Korean Jeolbap so clean, without meat or garlic?

Temple Food, Oh Sinchae, Balu Gongyang, Monk Jeonggwan — Explainer of Korean Jeolbap culture for foreigners. Not ‘Introduction to a vegetarian restaurant’ but ‘Why is it so empty?’

Foreigners who encounter Korean Jeolbap for the first time are surprised twice. At first "There's no meat at all", then he put a piece of vegetable in his mouth "But why is it so deep and clean?". Even though I didn't add garlic or onion, the taste wasn't empty. Instead of fancy seasonings, it is seasoned with seasonal ingredients and the depth of aged fermented soy sauce. This is temple food — a food culture called 'eating is also spiritual practice' developed by Korean Buddhism in 1,700 years. In May 2025, Korea designated temple food as a national intangible heritage (official announcement — check recommended), and thanks to Monk Jeong Gwan appearing on Netflix's 'Chef's Table', it is also a K-food that the world's gourmet world has paid attention to. This article is not about “where to eat” (→ cross-link), but explains why to remove, why to empty, and why it is a practice from a foreigner’s perspective. Korean culture explainer series Part 6.

Key line: The aesthetics of temple food is not ‘addition’ but ‘removal (emptiness)’. The philosophy is not about what you put in, but what you leave out.

⚠️ 3 things foreigners often misunderstand

1. "Jeolbap = bland, tasteless vegetarian food" ❌ — Temple food is not food that gives up the taste, but food that has a different way of making it taste. Instead of hitting it hard with garlic and onions, the umami is built up with the natural taste of seasonal vegetables and years-old soybean paste, soy sauce, pickled vegetables, mushrooms, and perilla seeds. Reduce stimulation and preserve depth — it's close to minimalism.

2. "Special food eaten only by monks" ❌ — It was originally a meal for ascetics, but now it has been widely opened to the general public and foreigners thanks to the temple stay (템플스테이), temple food class, specialty store, and vegan trends. On Buddha's Birthday, MZ generation and foreigners line up.

3. "If I eat it, do I have to convert to Buddhism?" ❌ — Experiencing temple food is not an introduction to religion, but a food culture and wellness experience. Anyone can enjoy it, including vegans, vegetarians, people looking for detox and mindfulness, and anyone just curious about the ‘roots of K-food.’ Temples do not force faith.

① What is temple food — ‘Eating is also a practice’

Temple food is literally food eaten in temples. However, it is not simply “temple-cooked vegetarian food.” The key point is viewing food as an extension of spiritual practice.

  • In the 1,700 years since Buddhism was introduced to Korea, temples have been self-sufficient and have developed their own cooking methods.
  • Consider food as medicine — not to please the tongue, but as the bare minimum to support the body to continue practicing.
  • So the keywords for temple food are not splendor but simplicity, neatness, and moderation. It does not use artificial seasonings, chemical additives, or preservatives, and preserves the original properties of the ingredients.
  • Buddhism's 'Middle Way' — avoiding extremes and finding balance — also applies to the dining table. Neither too little nor too much.

In other words, eating Jeolbap is not just a vegetarian meal, but an experience with one's mouth of the Korean Buddhist worldview of 'reducing and emptying'.

② Ban on five vegetables - Why are garlic and green onions removed?

What foreigners find most surprising about temple food. I understand that excluding meat is a ‘vegetarian diet’, but why are they also removing garlic and onions, which are perfectly fine vegetables?

Oshinchae (오신채) means "the five pungent vegetables." Traditionally this refers to garlic, green onion, chives, dallae (wild chives), and heunggeo (興渠, asafoetida) (★ accurate). In modern usage it's sometimes broadened to cover the allium family in general, including onion.

The reason for excluding these five isn't a simple dietary rule — it's the logic of spiritual practice.

  • Stimulation unsettles the mind: Buddhist scripture (such as the Surangama Sutra) holds that these pungent vegetables, eaten raw, stir up anger, and eaten cooked, stir up desire. Either way, they break the mind's calm and disturb meditation.
  • They agitate the body: their strong scent and stimulation are thought to overstir appetite and energy, unsettling a practitioner's body when it should stay calm and focused.
  • Consideration for the community: a practical reason is also passed down — avoiding strong breath odor that could bother others in a space where many people practice together.

Note — why is chili pepper okay? Chili pepper (chili powder) is not one of the oshinchae. Chili arrived on the Korean peninsula relatively late, around the time of the Japanese invasions of the 1590s, so it simply didn't exist when the oshinchae concept was established. That's why temple food often includes garlic-free "baek-kimchi" (white kimchi), or kimchi made with chili powder but without garlic or green onion. (Historical background — details recommended to verify)

The key point is that this isn't abstinence for its own sake — it's about emptying out what unsettles the mind so practice can be clearer.

③ Balwoo Gongyang (발우공양) — A Zero-Food-Waste Meal Ritual

The scene where temple food's philosophy shows most clearly is balwoo gongyang — the traditional meal ritual monks perform at temples according to a fixed form.

  • Balwoo (鉢盂) = each practitioner's personal set of bowls (usually four, nested inside one another). They're unstacked to the sound of a clapper (jukbi), and the ritual — chanting a sutra (Sosimgyeong) along the way — can take about an hour.
  • Take only as much as you'll eat, and finish every bit: leaving food behind isn't allowed. That's why the ritual produces almost no food waste.
  • Down to the last drop: once the meal is done, water is poured into the bowl to rinse it, and that rinse water (cheonsu-mul) is drunk as well. Not a grain of food or a drop of water is thrown away — the bowls end up clean enough that no separate washing is needed.
  • Meditation of gratitude: diners reflect on everything it took to bring this one meal to the table — a farmer's sweat, nature's labor, a donor's generosity — and eat as an exercise in letting go of greed.

Balwoo gongyang leaves a strong impression on foreigners, for one simple reason: today's global buzzwords of "zero-waste" and "mindful eating" are things Korean Buddhism has been practicing as ritual for centuries.

④ Seasonal and Natural — Receiving What Nature Gives at That Moment

Another pillar of temple food is seasonality (sisik) and staying true to nature.

  • Each season's food, in its season: wild greens (sannamul / 산나물) and dureup in spring, cucumber and eggplant in summer, mushrooms and doraji (도라지, balloon flower root) in autumn, dried greens and root vegetables in winter. You take what nature offers at that moment and let your body follow its rhythm.
  • No artificial flavor: no MSG, preservatives, or artificial additives. Even sweetness leans on natural sources like fruit and jocheong (rice syrup) rather than sugar.
  • Mushrooms, perilla seeds, and beans stand in for meat: nutrition comes from mushrooms, beans, and tofu instead of animal protein, while perilla seeds and nuts add nuttiness and umami.

This philosophy of "seasonality and restraint" isn't unique to temple food — it shares roots with Korean food culture at large, which balances itself through yin-yang and the five elements (→ #1132 on banchan culture's five colors and five flavors, #1134 on fermentation and jang culture).

⑤ Fermentation (醱酵) — The Secret to Deep Flavor Without Garlic

"How do you get flavor without garlic or onion?" — the answer to foreigners' most common question is fermentation.

  • Jang (fermented paste/sauce) does the work of seasoning: fermented jang like doenjang, ganjang, and gochujang sit at the center of temple food's flavor. Aged anywhere from months to years, they deliver a deep, round umami quite different from the quick hit of garlic. Time, in effect, is the seasoning.
  • The wisdom of jangajji (장아찌, pickled vegetables): seasonal vegetables preserved for a long time in jang or salt keep the ingredients from going to waste while fermentation builds up their flavor.
  • Garlic-free kimchi: temple kimchi is made without garlic, green onion, or salted seafood. Less pungent, but no less deep in fermented flavor.

Fermentation is also where temple food meets the modern wellness trend. As awareness spreads that fermented foods are good for gut health and immunity, "deep flavor built through fermentation instead of garlic" is being reappraised as sophisticated cuisine in its own right. (For the principles and types of fermentation → #1134 on fermentation and jang culture)

⑥ Going Global — Jeong Kwan and 'Chef's Table'

The decisive moment that put temple food on the world's culinary radar was Jeong Kwan (정관스님).

  • Jeong Kwan is a Seon (Zen) Buddhist nun residing at Baekyangsa Temple (Cheonjinam hermitage) in South Jeolla Province, cooking for fellow practitioners and the occasional visitor. She herself says, "I'm not a chef, I'm a monk," and treats cooking as one form of Buddhist practice.
  • She appeared on Netflix's 'Chef's Table' in 2017 (the first episode of Season 3), introducing Korean temple food to the world (★ accurate — Chef's Table 2017). The philosophy of a monk with no formal culinary training and no restaurant captivated chefs worldwide.
  • Influence on world-class chefs: chefs like René Redzepi of Noma and Eric Ripert of Le Bernardin are reported to have drawn inspiration from Jeong Kwan's philosophy (accounts vary by outlet — details recommended to verify).

Jeong Kwan's message stays consistent — cooking is a practice of emptying the self and connecting with nature and life. It was this "philosophy of emptiness," not flashy technique, that moved the world — which is exactly why temple food goes beyond simple vegetarianism.

⑦ Today — Meeting Temple Stays and Veganism

Once a strictly ascetic diet, temple food has entered everyday life today, carried by two converging trends.

  • Temple Stay & Temple Food Class: Foreigners can experience philosophy first-hand by preparing the ingredients themselves and eating them using the Balu offering method during a temple stay stay at a temple for 1-2 nights or a one-day temple food making class. A temple food cultural experience center in the center of Seoul (Anguk-dong, etc.) is also operated.
  • Global vegan demand and fit: Temple food is inherently close to complete vegetarianism (vegan) — meat, fish, vegetables, and artificial additives are excluded, making it safe even for picky vegan travelers. In line with the global vegan and plant-based boom, it has emerged as the number one experience for vegetarian travelers visiting Korea.
  • MZ·Wellness Keywords: ‘Mindful eating’, ‘detox’, ‘no additives’, ‘zero waste’ — These days, all the global wellness topics are already included in temple food. On Buddha's Birthday, the MZ generation and foreigners flock to temple food festivals and specialty stores.

Point: To foreigners, temple food appears as '1,700-year-old Korean vegan and mindful food culture' rather than 'religious food'. This angle significantly lowers the barrier to entry.

⑧ Foreigner Experience — How can I approach it?

For specific information on ‘where to eat’ (specialty restaurants, temple stay reservations), see the cross-link guide below. Here, only ‘mindset’.

  • Let go of the prejudice that ‘it won’t taste good’: For taste buds accustomed to strong seasoning, it may be boring at first. However, after two or three bites, the original sweetness and savory taste of the ingredients comes out. They say it’s a taste that your body likes, not your tongue.
  • Vegan/Allergy Safe: Since it excludes green vegetables, animal products, and artificial additives, it can be a particularly safe choice for vegans or travelers with garlic or onion allergies (recommended to check with each store).
  • Enjoy with 'Eating Meditation': Not quickly and a lot, but slowly, gratefully, and thoroughly. If you adopt the spirit of Balwoo Gongyang in one meal, the food feels different.
  • One step further to the experiential type: Rather than a simple meal, temple food class (making your own food) or temple stay (overnight at a temple) are deep experiences that reach into philosophy.

❓ Foreigners Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • Are all temple foods vegan? — Almost yes. In addition to meat, fish, eggs, and dairy products, it also excludes oshinchae (garlic and green onions), so it is more strict than regular vegans. However, the use of honey and dairy products may vary depending on the temple or region, so check recommended if sensitive.
  • If you take out the garlic and onions, wouldn’t it be tasteless? — That’s not true. Paste (soybean paste, soy sauce), pickled vegetables, mushrooms, and perilla seeds that have been aged for several years give it a deep umami flavor. It's just 'not irritating' and 'not tasteless'.
  • Can I eat it even if I am of a different religion? — Of course. Temple food and temple stays are open as a food culture and wellness experience rather than a forced belief. Non-religious and foreigners of other religions are also welcome.
  • Why is red pepper (spicy) included? — Red pepper is not included in Oshinchae (garlic, green onion, chives, chives, and heungeo). Because it was a late crop introduced to the Korean Peninsula, it was not subject to traditional taboos. Therefore, temple kimchi often contains red pepper powder but no garlic or green onions.
  • How ​​to truly experience temple food? — Rather than a simple meal at a restaurant, temple food class (cooking your own food) or temple stay (temple lodging + bowl offering) reaches into philosophy. (→ cross-link)
