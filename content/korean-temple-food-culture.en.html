Korean Temple Food (Sachal Eumsik) — Why Korean Buddhist Cuisine Drops Meat, Garlic & Onion

> Sachal Eumsik (사찰음식), Osinchae (오신채), Baru Gongyang (발우공양), Ven. Jeong Kwan (정관스님) — A Korean temple food culture explainer for international visitors. This isn't about "where to find vegetarian restaurants," but "why it's about emptying and letting go."

Foreigners encountering Korean temple food (jeolbap, 절밥) for the first time are often surprised twice. First, they notice, "There's no meat at all!" Then, after tasting a single piece of seasoned vegetable (namul, 나물), they exclaim, "But why is it so deep and refined?" Even without garlic or onions, the flavor isn't empty. Instead of flashy seasonings, it draws its depth from seasonal ingredients and long-fermented jang (醬, Korean fermented paste/sauce). This is sachal eumsik (사찰음식) — a culinary culture developed over 1,700 years of Korean Buddhism, where "eating is also a form of practice." In May 2025, Korea designated sachal eumsik as a Gukga Muyeong Yusan (국가무형유산, National Intangible Cultural Heritage) (official announcement – confirmation recommended). Thanks to Ven. Jeong Kwan (정관스님), who appeared on Netflix's 'Chef's Table,' it has also become a K-Food (K-푸드) attracting global gastronomic attention. This article isn't about "where to eat it" (→ cross-link), but rather why ingredients are omitted, why it's about emptying, and why that is a form of practice, explained from a foreigner's perspective. This is the 6th installment of the Korean Culture Explainer Series (Hanguk Munhwa Explainer Series, 한국 문화 explainer 시리즈).

> Key Takeaway: The aesthetics of sachal eumsik (사찰음식) are about 'subtracting' (emptying), not 'adding.' Its philosophy lies not in what is included, but what is omitted.

⚠️ 3 Common Misconceptions by Foreigners

1. "Temple food (jeolbap, 절밥) = bland, tasteless vegetarian food" ❌ — Sachal eumsik (사찰음식) is not food that gives up on flavor; it's food where the method of creating flavor is different. Instead of strong flavors from garlic or onions, it builds umami (gamchilmat, 감칠맛) with the inherent taste of seasonal vegetables, and years-aged jang (장, fermented pastes like doenjang (된장) and ganjang (간장)), jangajji (장아찌, pickled vegetables), mushrooms, and perilla seeds (deulkkae, 들깨). It reduces stimulation and enhances depth — closer to minimalism.

2. "A special diet only for monks" ❌ — While originally the diet of practitioners, it is now widely accessible to the public and foreigners, driven by Temple Stay (템플스테이), sachal eumsik (사찰음식) classes, specialty restaurants, and the vegan trend. On Buddha's Birthday (Bucheonim Osinnal, 부처님오신날), MZ generation (MZ sedae, MZ세대) and foreigners line up to experience it.

3. "Do I have to convert to Buddhism to eat it?" ❌ — Experiencing sachal eumsik (사찰음식) is not religious initiation, but a culinary and wellness experience. Vegans, vegetarians, those seeking detox or mindfulness, or simply curious about the 'roots of K-Food (K-푸드)' can all enjoy it. Temples do not force faith.

① What is Sachal Eumsik (사찰음식)? — 'Eating as a Form of Practice'

Sachal eumsik (사찰음식) literally means food eaten in a temple (jeol, 절). However, it's not simply "vegetarian food made in a temple." The core idea is that food is seen as an extension of spiritual practice.

  • For over 1,700 years since Buddhism arrived in Korea, temples have been self-sufficient, developing unique cooking methods.
  • Food is regarded as yak (藥, medicine) — not to please the palate, but as the minimum required to sustain the body for continued practice.
  • Therefore, the keywords for sachal eumsik (사찰음식) are not extravagance, but simplicity, refinement, and moderation. It avoids artificial seasonings, chemical additives, and preservatives, preserving the natural qualities of the ingredients.
  • Buddhism's 'Jungdo (中道, Middle Way)' — avoiding extremes and seeking balance — is applied to the dining table: neither too little nor too much.

Thus, eating temple food (jeolbap, 절밥) is not just a vegetarian meal, but an experience of Korean Buddhism's worldview of 'subtracting and emptying' through taste.

② Osinchae Geumji (오신채 금지) — Why Omit Garlic and Green Onions?

This is where foreigners find sachal eumsik (사찰음식) most intriguing. Omitting meat is understandable for a 'vegetarian' diet, but why omit common vegetables like garlic and onions?

Osinchae (五辛菜) = five pungent vegetables. Traditionally, these refer to garlic (maneul, 마늘), green onion (pa, 파), chives (buchu, 부추), wild rocambole (dallae, 달래), and asafoetida (heunggeo, 興渠, also known as a-wi, 아위) (accurate). In modern times, this can be broadly interpreted to include all allium plants like onions.

The reason for omitting these five is not merely a dietary rule but a logic of spiritual practice.

  • Stimulation disrupts the mind: Buddhist scriptures (e.g., Neungamgyeong, 능엄경) state that eating these pungent vegetables raw provokes anger (seongnaem, 성냄), and eating them cooked stimulates lustful desires (eumsim, 음심). Both disturb mental tranquility and hinder meditation.
  • Agitates the body: Their strong aroma and stimulation are believed to excessively boost appetite and energy, preventing the body of a practitioner, who needs quiet concentration, from stabilizing.
  • Consideration for the community: There's also a practical reason: to avoid strong breath odors that could inconvenience others in a communal practice setting.

> Note — Why is chili pepper (gochu, 고추) okay? Chili pepper (gochu, 고추) (or chili powder, gochutgaru, 고춧가루) is not included in osinchae (오신채). Chili peppers were introduced to the Korean Peninsula relatively late, around the Imjin Waeran (임진왜란, Japanese invasions of Korea) period, so they did not exist when the concept of osinchae (오신채) was established. Therefore, sachal eumsik (사찰음식) often features 'white kimchi' (baekkimchi, 백김치) without garlic, or kimchi (김치) made with chili powder (gochutgaru, 고춧가루) but without garlic or green onions. (Historical background — details recommended for confirmation)

The key is that this is not asceticism for its own sake, but 'emptying things that disturb the mind to practice more clearly.'

③ Baru Gongyang (발우공양) — A Meal Ritual with Zero Food Waste

The philosophy of sachal eumsik (사찰음식) is most clearly demonstrated in baru gongyang (발우공양) — a traditional meal ritual performed by monks in temples according to prescribed rules.

  • Baru (鉢盂) = A set of bowls for each practitioner (usually four bowls stacked together). They are unfolded to the sound of a bamboo stick (jukbi, 죽비) and the ritual can last about an hour, accompanied by the chanting of scriptures (e.g., Sosimgyeong, 소심경).
  • Take only what you need, eat everything: Leaving food behind is not permitted. As a result, almost no food waste is generated.
  • To the last drop: After the meal, water is poured into the bowls to rinse them, and this rinse water (cheonsumul, 천수물) is also consumed. Not a single grain of food or drop of water is wasted. The bowls are cleaned so thoroughly that no separate washing is needed.
  • Meditation of gratitude: It's a mental practice where one reflects on the countless efforts — the farmer's sweat, nature's toil, the benefactor's generosity — that went into bringing the meal to the table, letting go of greed (tamsim, 탐심).

Baru gongyang (발우공양) leaves a strong impression on foreigners. It highlights that Korean Buddhism has practiced 'zero-waste' and 'mindful eating' rituals for hundreds of years, aligning with contemporary global concerns.

④ Jecheol Jayeon (제철 자연) — Accepting What Nature Provides in Its Season

Another pillar of sachal eumsik (사찰음식) is jecheol (時食, seasonal) and natural ingredients.

  • What's in season, when it's in season: Wild greens (sannamul, 산나물) and wild asparagus (dureup, 두릅) in spring; cucumbers (oi, 오이) and eggplants (gaji, 가지) in summer; mushrooms (beoseot, 버섯) and balloon flower roots (doraji, 도라지) in autumn; dried greens (muknamul, 묵나물) and root vegetables in winter. They accept what nature offers at that time, aligning their bodies with its flow.
  • Excluding artificial flavors: No chemical seasonings (MSG), preservatives, or artificial additives are used. Sweetness relies on natural sources like fruits or rice syrup (jocheong, 조청) rather than sugar.
  • Mushrooms, perilla seeds, and beans replace meat: Instead of animal protein, mushrooms (beoseot, 버섯), beans (kong, 콩), and tofu (dubu, 두부) provide nutrition, while perilla seeds (deulkkae, 들깨) and nuts add savory and umami (umami, 우마미) flavors.

This philosophy of 'seasonal and moderation' is not exclusive to sachal eumsik (사찰음식) but is rooted in Korean food culture (Hanguk sikmunhwa, 한국 식문화) as a whole, which seeks balance through Eumyangohaeng (음양오행, Yin-Yang and Five Elements). (→ [#1132 Korean Banchan (Side Dish) Culture](1132_korean_banchan_culture.md) for obangsaek (오방색) and omi (오미) balance, [#1134 Korean Fermentation & Jang (Fermented Paste/Sauce) Culture](1134_korean_fermentation_jang_culture.md) for fermentation and jang (장) culture).

⑤ Balhyo (발효) — The Secret to Deep Flavor Without Garlic

"How do they create flavor without garlic or onions?" — The answer to this common foreign question is balhyo (발효, fermentation).

  • Jang (醬, fermented paste/sauce) replaces seasoning: Fermented pastes like doenjang (된장, fermented soybean paste), ganjang (간장, soy sauce), and gochujang (고추장, chili paste) are central to the flavor of sachal eumsik (사찰음식). Jang (장) aged for months to years creates a deep, rounded umami (gamchilmat, 감칠맛) that is different from the immediate punch of garlic. Time itself is the seasoning.
  • The wisdom of jangajji (장아찌): Jangajji (장아찌), seasonal vegetables pickled in jang (장) or salt and stored for a long time, not only preserves ingredients but also enhances their flavor through fermentation.
  • Kimchi (김치) without garlic: Kimchi (김치) in temples is made without garlic (maneul, 마늘), green onions (pa, 파), or fermented seafood (jeotgal, 젓갈). It has less pungency but retains the depth of fermentation.

Fermentation is also where sachal eumsik (사찰음식) meets modern wellness trends. As the understanding that fermented foods are good for gut health and immunity spreads, 'deep flavors created by fermentation without garlic' are being re-evaluated as sophisticated cuisine. (For the principles and types of fermentation, see → [#1134 Korean Fermentation & Jang (Fermented Paste/Sauce) Culture](1134_korean_fermentation_jang_culture.md)).

⑥ Global Reach — Ven. Jeong Kwan (정관스님) and 'Chef's Table'

The decisive moment when sachal eumsik (사찰음식) caught the attention of the global culinary world was through Ven. Jeong Kwan (정관스님).

  • Ven. Jeong Kwan (정관스님) is a Seon (禪, Zen) bhikkhuni (비구니, Buddhist nun) residing at Baekyangsa Temple (백양사) (Baekyangsa Cheonjinam Hermitage, 백양사 천진암) in Jeollanam-do (전라남도, Jeonnam). She cooks for fellow practitioners and occasional visitors. She states, "I am a nun, not a chef," viewing cooking as a form of Buddhist practice.
  • She appeared in the 2017 Netflix 'Chef's Table' (Season 3, first episode), introducing Korean sachal eumsik (사찰음식) to the world (accurate — Chef's Table 2017). The philosophy of a nun with no formal culinary training or restaurant captivated chefs worldwide.
  • Influence on top global chefs: Renowned chefs like René Redzepi of Noma and Eric Ripert of Le Bernardin are known to have been inspired by Ven. Jeong Kwan's (정관스님) philosophy (media reports vary — details recommended for confirmation).

Ven. Jeong Kwan's (정관스님) message is consistent — cooking is a practice of emptying oneself and connecting with nature and life. The fact that a 'philosophy of emptying' rather than elaborate techniques moved hearts worldwide is why sachal eumsik (사찰음식) transcends simple vegetarianism.

⑦ Today — Meeting Temple Stay (템플스테이) and Veganism

Sachal eumsik (사찰음식), once a traditional monastic diet, has now entered daily life through two main trends.

  • Temple Stay (템플스테이) & Sachal Eumsik (사찰음식) Classes: Foreigners can experience the philosophy firsthand by staying 1-2 nights at a temple (jeol, 절) through a Temple Stay (템플스테이) program or by taking a one-day sachal eumsik (사찰음식) cooking class, where they prepare ingredients themselves and eat in the baru gongyang (발우공양) style. Sachal eumsik (사찰음식) cultural experience centers also operate in central Seoul (e.g., Anguk-dong, 안국동).
  • Fits Global Vegan Demand: Sachal eumsik (사찰음식) is inherently close to complete veganism — by omitting meat, fish, eggs, dairy, osinchae (오신채), and artificial additives, it is a safe choice for even the most discerning vegan travelers. It perfectly aligns with the global vegan and plant-based boom, becoming a top experience for vegetarian travelers visiting Korea.
  • MZ (MZ세대) & Wellness Keywords: 'Mindful eating,' 'detox,' 'additive-free,' 'zero-waste' — all these contemporary global wellness keywords are already embedded in sachal eumsik (사찰음식). On Buddha's Birthday (Bucheonim Osinnal, 부처님오신날), the MZ generation (MZ세대) and foreigners flock to sachal eumsik (사찰음식) festivals and specialty restaurants.

> Point: For foreigners, sachal eumsik (사찰음식) is less a 'religious food' and more 'a 1,700-year-old Korean vegan and mindful eating culture.' This perspective significantly lowers the barrier to entry.

⑧ Foreigner Experience — How to Approach It

> For specific 'where to eat' (specialty restaurants, Temple Stay (템플스테이) booking), refer to the cross-link guides below. This section focuses on 'mindset.'

  • Let go of the prejudice that 'it will be tasteless': For palates accustomed to strong seasonings, it might seem plain at first. But after a few bites, the natural sweetness and umami (gamchilmat, 감칠맛) of the ingredients emerge. People often say it's a taste that the body likes, not just the tongue.
  • Vegan and allergy-friendly: By omitting osinchae (오신채), animal products, and artificial additives, it can be a particularly safe choice for vegans or travelers with garlic or onion allergies (confirmation with individual establishments recommended).
  • Enjoy it as 'eating meditation': Not quickly and abundantly, but slowly, gratefully, and without waste. Borrowing the spirit of baru gongyang (발우공양) for a meal can make the food feel different.
  • Go a step further with experiential options: More than just a meal, a sachal eumsik (사찰음식) class (hands-on cooking) or a Temple Stay (템플스테이) (overnight stay at a temple) offers a deeper experience that connects with the philosophy.

❓ Foreigner Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • Is all sachal eumsik (사찰음식) vegan? — Almost always. It omits meat, fish, eggs, dairy, and even osinchae (오신채, garlic, green onion, etc.), making it stricter than typical vegan diets. However, the use of honey or some dairy products can vary by temple (jeol, 절) or region, so confirmation is recommended if sensitive.
  • Doesn't it taste bland without garlic or onions? — Not at all. Long-fermented jang (장, such as doenjang (된장) and ganjang (간장)), jangajji (장아찌), mushrooms (beoseot, 버섯), and perilla seeds (deulkkae, 들깨) create deep umami (gamchilmat, 감칠맛). It's 'not stimulating,' not 'tasteless.'
  • Can I eat it even if I have a different religion? — Absolutely. Sachal eumsik (사찰음식) and Temple Stay (템플스테이) are open as culinary and wellness experiences, not religious coercion. Foreigners of no religion or other religions are welcome.
  • Why is chili pepper (gochu, 고추) allowed? — Chili pepper (gochu, 고추) is not included in the osinchae (오신채, garlic, green onion, chives, wild rocambole, and asafoetida). It was introduced to the Korean Peninsula later and was not traditionally a forbidden ingredient. Therefore, temple kimchi (kimchi, 김치) often contains chili powder (gochutgaru, 고춧가루) but no garlic or green onions.
  • How can I best experience sachal eumsik (사찰음식)? — A sachal eumsik (사찰음식) class (hands-on cooking) or a Temple Stay (템플스테이) (overnight stay at a temple with baru gongyang (발우공양)) offers a deeper experience that connects with the philosophy, more so than just a single restaurant meal.
