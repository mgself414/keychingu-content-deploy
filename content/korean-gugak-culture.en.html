Gugak — Pansori, Samulnori & Arirang, the Sound of Korea

> Pansori (판소리, sori singer·gosu drummer·han) · Samulnori/Pungmul (사물놀이) · Arirang (아리랑) · Korean instruments (gayageum (가야금)·haegeum (해금)) · Jeongak (정악, court music) vs minsogak (민속악, folk music) · Modern gugak hallyu — a gugak (국악, Korean traditional music) explainer for foreigners. Not "what instruments are there," but "what do Koreans express with this sound, and where can you hear it."

Long before K-pop shook the world, Korea had a sound all its own. Gugak (국악) — literally "the nation's music" — is Korean traditional music. A single sori singer (소리꾼, vocal storyteller) carrying an hours-long tale with just one drum in pansori (판소리), four percussion instruments exploding in samulnori (사물놀이), the Arirang (아리랑) that everyone hums — it's a world whose scales, rhythms, and emotions all differ from Western music.

This article isn't "which concert hall is good" (→ a place guide), but unpacks what gugak is, how it branches, and what Koreans express through that sound. Even within the same "traditional music," the majestic court jeongak (정악, court music) and the throbbing folk minsogak (민속악, folk music) are entirely different musics. This is installment #71 of the Korean Culture explainer series.

⚠️ 3 Things Foreigners Often Get Wrong

1. "Gugak = just old background music (BGM)" ❌ — Gugak isn't atmospheric sound effects but a sophisticated musical system. Its core lies in scales different from the Western do-re-mi (pyeongjo (평조)·gyemyeonjo (계면조)), a unique cyclic rhythmic frame called jangdan (장단) rather than the beat, and sigimsae (시김새, ornamental shaking/bending of notes). Not "simple," but "different."

2. "Pansori must be a short folk-song kind of thing" ❌ — Pansori is an epic that runs for hours per piece. One singer plays dozens of characters alone, alternating song (sori (소리)), spoken word (aniri (아니리)), and gesture (neoreumsae (너름새)). It's a one-person musical drama closer to opera, not a short song.

3. "Gugak is dead music, museum-only" ❌ — Far from it. Leenalchi's 'Tiger Is Coming' (범 내려온다) went viral on YouTube with hundreds of millions of views, and ADG7 (악단광칠) tours overseas. Fusion gugak (퓨전 국악) — traditional sound dressed in rock, funk, and electronica — is the new trend among Korea's young generation right now.

① What Is Gugak — the Two Streams of "the Nation's Music"

Gugak (국악) is the term for Korean traditional music as a whole. Named to distinguish it from Western music (yangak (양악)), it spans broadly from court music to ritual music to the songs and dances of the common people. The key to understanding it is that it divides into two branches.

  • Jeongak (정악): "correct music." The refined and restrained music enjoyed by the court and the yangban (양반, scholar-gentry). Jongmyo Jeryeak (종묘제례악), Yeongsanhoesang (영산회상, pungnyu (풍류)), and jeongga (정가, gagok·sijo) belong here. Slow and majestic, it leans toward governing emotion rather than revealing it.
  • Minsogak (민속악): the free and emotionally alive music the common people enjoyed. Pansori (판소리), sanjo (산조), folk songs (minyo (민요)), and pungmul (풍물, nongak) are all minsogak. It overflows with improvisation and sinmyeong (신명, ecstatic joy), pouring out sorrow and joy alike without restraint.

The pansori, samulnori, and Arirang that foreigners first meet are almost all on the minsogak side — which is why gugak is often felt as "emotionally rich and exhilarating." Another defining trait is jangdan (장단). Rather than counting 4/4 time, the music flows over named rhythmic frames like jinyangjo (진양조, slow)·jungmori (중모리)·jajinmori (자진모리, fast), progressing and gradually accelerating to build tension.

② Pansori — the Singer, the Drummer, and the Music of 'Han (한)'

Pansori (판소리) is the most dramatic branch of gugak. There are only two people on stage.

  • Sori singer (소리꾼, the vocalist): stands holding a single fan and sings. Moving among song (sori (소리)), spoken narration (aniri (아니리)), and gesture and body acting (neoreumsae (너름새)), the singer plays every character alone.
  • Gosu (고수, 鼓手, the drummer): sits on the floor, keeping the rhythm with a single drum (buk (북)), and lifts the mood with cries of "Eolssu!"·"Jota!" called chuimsae (추임새). The audience joins in the chuimsae too — it's music in which the listeners take part in the performance.

The Five Madang of Pansori (the five pieces handed down today):

Madang Story
Chunhyangga (춘향가) The love and fidelity of Chunhyang and Yi Mong-ryong, crossing class lines
Simcheongga (심청가) Devoted daughter Simcheong, who gives her life to open her father's eyes
Heungbuga (흥부가) Kind Heungbu and greedy Nolbu — a tale of brothers rewarding good and punishing evil
Sugungga (수궁가) A battle of wits between the rabbit and the turtle — a humor-filled animal fable
Jeokbyeokga (적벽가) An epic of heroes, the Battle of Red Cliffs from Romance of the Three Kingdoms set to song

The emotion of pansori, compressed into one word, is han (한, 恨). It pours out suppressed sorrow, longing, and pent-up grief in a poignant voice, yet humor and fun are mixed within it. The singer deliberately trains for years before a waterfall to forge a rough, husky voice (suriseong (수리성)) — not a smooth, pretty tone but a husky sound bearing the weight of life is the virtue of pansori (→ see #1138 for the han sentiment).

> UNESCO: Pansori was designated a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2003 (later inscribed on the Representative List in 2008).

③ Samulnori · Pungmul — the Sinmyeong Made by Four Instruments

If pansori is 'han,' samulnori is the exact opposite — sinmyeong (신명, ecstatic joy).

Samulnori (사물놀이) literally means "playing (nori) with four things (samul)." Four percussion instruments come together to build explosive rhythm.

Instrument What Symbol
Kkwaenggwari (꽹과리) Small brass gong, struck with a bamboo stick Thunder — the leader holds it and gives signals
Jing (징) Large gong, resonating deeply Wind
Janggu (장구) Hourglass-shaped, double-headed drum Rain
Buk (북) Log-bodied, double-headed drum, low-pitched Cloud

Originally these instruments came from pungmul (풍물, nongak). Pungmul is communal music in which everyone in a farming village played and danced together to pray for a good harvest and drive off the village's misfortune. This nongak, once spread out across open fields, was reorganized in 1978 by Kim Deok-su and others into a four-person seated stage performance — and that is samulnori. In other words, samulnori is a modern stage form born from tradition (pungmul).

The charm of samulnori is its steadily accelerating jangdan (장단). Starting slowly, it speeds up through jajinmori (자진모리) and hwimori (휘모리), sweeping the audience into excitement in an instant. Even without understanding any lyrics, foreigners find it the most intuitive gugak to enjoy with the whole body.

④ Arirang — Different in Every Region, the Song of the Korean People

Arirang (아리랑) is the national folk song that every Korean knows. It has no specific composer. It's a collective creation that ordinary people made and sang together over hundreds of years.

The structure is simple. The refrain — "Arirang, Arirang, Arariyo…" — is shared, while the verses (saseol (사설)) differ by region. So Arirang isn't a single song but a vast song family branching into about 60 types and over 3,600 variations.

  • Jeongseon Arirang (정선 아리랑) (Gangwon-do): considered the oldest archetype. A slow and wistful melody shaped by the isolation of deep mountain valleys in the Taebaek range.
  • Jindo Arirang (진도 아리랑) (an island in South Jeolla): influenced by Namdo shamanic music and sinawi, it has a thick, richly textured flair.
  • Miryang Arirang (밀양 아리랑) (South Gyeongsang): fast and lively, a cheerful tune close to a work song. It carries the color of southern farming culture, sung together while working the fields.

Arirang sings of sorrow (parting, a hard life), yet within it holds not resignation but comfort and jeong (정, affection). That's why it served as a song of resistance in the colonial era, and today is understood as a song of the nation that North and South can sing together.

> UNESCO: Arirang was inscribed on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2012.

⑤ Korean Instruments — Gayageum, Haegeum, Daegeum: the Grain of Korea's Sound

Gugak's distinctive tone comes from its instruments. These are representative instruments that seem like Western ones yet differ.

  • Gayageum (가야금, 12-string zither): the face of gugak instruments. A plucked instrument whose 12 silk strings are plucked and pressed with the fingers. Pressing the strings to shake the pitch — nonghyeon (농현, sigimsae) — produces a deep resonance. (Modern improved versions also come in 18, 21, and 25 strings.)
  • Geomungo (거문고, 6-string zither): a deep-toned plucked instrument with 6 strings. Played by striking the strings with a suldae (술대, rod), its lower, more contemplative sound than the gayageum made it beloved as the scholar's instrument.
  • Haegeum (해금, 2-string fiddle): a two-string bowed instrument (a Korean violin). Bowed to produce a mournfully bending sound like a human voice, it's the most popular melody instrument in fusion gugak.
  • Daegeum (대금, large bamboo flute): a large transverse bamboo flute. A reed membrane (galdaecheong (갈대청)) vibrates to produce its signature wind-like buzzing tone and a clear, pure sound.
  • Piri (피리, double-reed pipe): a small vertical bamboo double-reed wind instrument. Small in body but large in volume, it anchors the center of an ensemble.
  • Janggu (장구): the heart of percussion. Hourglass-shaped, its two faces produce different sounds (a high chae-pyeon (채편) and a low buk-pyeon (북편)), so it enters almost every ensemble.

Where Western instruments pursue exact pitch, gugak instruments value more the expression of shaking and pushing between notes (sigimsae (시김새)). It's a music of "grain and tremor" rather than "exactness."

⑥ Jeongak vs Minsogak — the Refinement of the Court and the Sinmyeong of the People

Let's unpack a bit more the difference between jeongak and minsogak noted in ①. Once you can tell the two apart, gugak sounds in three dimensions.

  • Jeongak (정악, court·upper-class): slow, majestic, restrained music. The representative is Jongmyo Jeryeak (종묘제례악) — the comprehensive art of music, song, and dance performed when the Joseon (조선) royal house held rites for past kings, carried on for over 500 years from the 15th century. Rather than exploding emotion, it expresses dignity and order. (Jongmyo Jerye·Jeryeak was inscribed on UNESCO in 2001.)
  • Minsogak (민속악, common people): fast, free, emotionally alive music. Pansori (판소리), sanjo (산조), folk songs, and pungmul (풍물) belong here. Improvisation and sinmyeong, han and fun, are revealed without restraint.

To put it by analogy, jeongak is a well-pressed suit, minsogak is comfortable everyday wear. If a foreigner feels "gugak is exhilarating and emotionally rich," that's usually because they heard minsogak; if they feel "it's solemn and slow," they heard jeongak. Both are gugak.

⑦ Modern Gugak Hallyu — the New Wave Opened by 'Tiger Is Coming'

Gugak is not a stuffed past. In the 2020s it has revived as one of the trendiest musics around.

  • Leenalchi (이날치): a seven-member band combining pansori vocals with bass and drums. 'Tiger Is Coming' (범 내려온다), which borrows a passage from the pansori Sugungga (수궁가), linked up with a Korea Tourism Organization promo video and went viral in reverse on YouTube with hundreds of millions of views.
  • ADG7 (악단광칠): a nine-member group that channels the energy of rock and funk using only traditional instruments, built on Hwanghae-do shamanic music and Seodo folk songs. They roam stages across the US and Europe, growing their overseas fan base.

Fans call this current fusion gugak (퓨전 국악). The key is that they reinterpret tradition without discarding it — keeping pansori vocalization, janggu jangdan, and haegeum melody while dressing them in modern clothes. Just as K-pop went out into the world, gugak is following in its own way.

⑧ A Foreigner's Gugak Experience — Where to Hear It and How to Learn It

Foreigners can fully enjoy and learn gugak. Even without understanding the lyrics, the rhythm and tone alone are powerful.

  • National Gugak Center (국립국악원, Seocho, Seoul): the home of gugak. Its Saturday regular performances (토요상설) (Saturday afternoons) are especially good for beginners — you sample court music, folk music, and dance all in one place. The International Gugak Workshop, carried on since 2001, is a program where foreigners learn pansori, haegeum, gayageum, and janggu firsthand.
  • A bite of performance·experience: you can taste it briefly at traditional theaters in Insa-dong and Jeong-dong, samulnori one-day classes, and regular hanok-village performances. Lately there are even showcases by foreign pansori singers.
  • Enter through the ears first: ease in with Leenalchi's 'Tiger Is Coming' (범 내려온다) → samulnori videos → Arirang, in that order. It's the course that breaks the "it's difficult" prejudice fastest.

> In practice: admission fees and times change each season, so check the official schedules of the National Gugak Center and VisitKorea before you visit. For samulnori and pansori, the closer to the front your seat, the more vivid the chuimsae and vibration.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • What exactly is gugak? — It's the term for Korean traditional music as a whole ("the nation's music"). It divides broadly into the court·upper-class jeongak (정악) and the common people's minsogak (민속악). Pansori, samulnori, and Arirang are on the minsogak side.
  • Why is pansori so long and the voice so rough? — A single pansori piece is an hours-long epic, and one singer plays every character. The rough, husky voice (suriseong (수리성)) isn't a flaw but a virtue earned through years of training — counted as a sound that carries the weight of life.
  • Why are there several versions of Arirang? — With no composer, it's a song that people in each region set to different verses, so it branches into about 60 types like Jeongseon·Jindo·Miryang. Only the refrain is the same; the melodies and verses differ.
  • Is gugak still heard today, or is it old music? — Both. Performances of the tradition as-is are lively, and fusion gugak like Leenalchi·ADG7 is popular among the young generation.
  • Can a foreigner learn it directly? — Yes. You can experience janggu and pansori at the National Gugak Center's International Gugak Workshop or at samulnori one-day classes. It's great to enjoy by rhythm even without knowing the lyrics.
