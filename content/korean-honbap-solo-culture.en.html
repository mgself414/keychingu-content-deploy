Korean Honbap & Honsul (Solo Dining) Culture — Why Koreans Now Eat and Drink Alone in a Country Built on Sharing

Honbap · Honsul · Honyeong · Honhaeng · Honjok — A Korean solo culture explainer for foreigners. This isn't about "restaurants recommended for solo diners" but rather "why, what it means, its clash with tradition, and the dual aspects of freedom and isolation."

Korean (한국) dramas and entertainment shows (예능) often feature two types of dining scenes. One shows an entire family or colleagues gathered around a round table, sharing banchan (반찬, free Korean side dishes), shouting "Eat a lot!", and clinking soju (소주, Korean distilled spirit) glasses – a scene of 'eating together.' The other type has become increasingly common lately: a protagonist eating a convenience store (편의점) dosirak (도시락, packed meal) alone, quietly grilling meat at a solo gogitjip (고깃집, Korean BBQ restaurant), or opening a can of beer while sitting on their bed, thinking, "Honsul (혼술) is the best!" To a foreigner, this might just seem like "Koreans (한국인) eat alone too." But for Koreans (한국인), this change tells a much bigger story. In a country where eating together was a virtue embodying jeong (정, deep emotional connection), and where people used to cast pitying glances at those eating alone, honbap (혼밥, solo dining) and honsul (혼술, solo drinking) have become a proud lifestyle in just over a decade. This article is not about "restaurant recommendations for solo diners" (→ matjip (맛집, popular restaurant) guide), but rather explains why this change occurred, how it clashes with tradition, and its light and shadow sides from a foreigner's perspective. This is the 28th installment in the Korean (한국) Culture Explainer series.

★ At a Glance — Six Types of 'Hon-OO' Culture

Hon-OO Meaning One-liner
Honbap (혼밥) Alone + Meal Eating alone. The starting point and representative of solo culture.
Honsul (혼술) Alone + Alcohol Drinking alone. Typically a light drink at home.
Honyeong (혼영) Alone + Movie Watching a movie alone. An easy introduction even for foreigners.
Honhaeng (혼행) Alone + Travel Traveling alone. Solo travel.
Hongong (혼공) Alone + Study Studying alone at a cafe or study cafe.
Honjok (혼족) Alone + Tribe A general term for people who enjoy the above activities and the culture itself.

Key Point: Korean (한국어) creates infinite combinations by attaching the prefix 'hon (혼)' from 'honja (혼자, alone)' to other words. After honbap (혼밥) paved the way, honsul (혼술), honyeong (혼영), honhaeng (혼행), hongong (혼공), and even honsho (혼쇼, solo shopping) followed. This word-formation itself signals a societal change where "people no longer hide doing things alone."

⚠️ 3 Common Misconceptions Foreigners Often Have

  1. "Korea (한국) is a country where people always eat together or in groups." ❌ — This is an outdated image. As of 2024, the most common household type in Korea (한국) is the 'single-person household' (1인 가구), accounting for 36.1% of all households (Statistics Korea (통계청), verification recommended). Honbap (혼밥) and honsul (혼술) are no longer exceptions but have become close to the default in daily life.
  2. "Eating honbap (혼밥) will make me look pathetic." ❌ — While such perceptions existed about a decade ago, it has now transformed into a symbol of self-care, freedom, and efficiency. The increase in solo gogitjip (고깃집, Korean BBQ restaurants) and other solo-friendly restaurants means eating alone is no longer awkward at all. In fact, many find it "comfortable because they don't have to worry about what others think."
  3. "Honsul (혼술) = alcoholism or depression." ❌ — Honsul (혼술) in Korea (한국) typically involves one or two light drinks at home, a personal time enjoyed with a drama. It's a culture diametrically opposed to the forced heavy drinking of hoesik (회식, company dinners/gatherings) (→ #1140 Hoesik Culture) — it actually emerged as a reaction to it.

① What is Honbap & Honsul? — Words with 'Alone' Attached

Honbap (혼밥) is a shortened form of honja (혼자, alone) + bap (밥, meal/rice), literally meaning eating alone. Honsul (혼술) is honja (혼자) + sul (술, alcohol), meaning drinking alone.

  • Both terms began to be used explosively from the mid-2010s, becoming a social phenomenon around 2017 (a period when the number of people eating, drinking, and traveling alone surged).
  • Once 'honbap (혼밥)' opened the door for this standard word-formation, honyeong (혼영, solo movie-watching), honhaeng (혼행, solo travel), hongong (혼공, solo studying), and honsho (혼쇼, solo shopping) followed suit.
  • The neologism that collectively refers to these people is honjok (혼족) — a combination of 'honja (혼자, alone)' and 'jok (족, a group of people).'

In other words, 'honbap (혼밥)' is not just a way of eating, but has become a key term representing an entire lifestyle of actively choosing and enjoying time spent alone.

② Why the Change? — The Era of Single-Person Households and Individualism

The biggest driving force is the change in household structure itself.

  • According to Statistics Korea's (통계청) 2024 Statistics on Single-Person Households (1인 가구), single-person households (1인 가구) account for 36.1% of all households, approximately 8.04 million households — surpassing 8 million for the first time in history and becoming the most common household type (figures are as of the publication date, verification recommended).
  • Single-person households (1인 가구) surpassed 30% around 2019. This means it hasn't been long since society became one where "one out of three homes is a single-person household," and the pace of change is very rapid.
  • Background: This trend is a confluence of bihon (비혼, non-marriage) and manhon (만혼, late marriage) (where marriage is a choice, not a necessity), the individualism of the MZ generation (MZ세대), an aging population (고령화) leading to more elderly single-person households (1인 가구), and the concentration of single-person households in the Seoul Metropolitan Area (수도권) (Seoul (서울) and Gyeonggi Province (경기) account for approximately 42.7%).

With no one to eat with at home, honbap (혼밥) becomes not a choice, but a way of life. And once people start eating alone, many discover that "it's actually more comfortable" — this is where honbap (혼밥) transitions from a necessity to a preference.

③ Clash with the Tradition of 'Eating Together' — From Jeong (情) to Solo

This is the core of this article. Korea (한국) was originally a society that placed strong emphasis on 'eating together.'

  • Sharing a single pot or banchan (반찬, free Korean side dishes) was a way of building jeong (정, deep emotional connection) (→ #1135 Jeong Culture). "Let's eat a meal together" is both a greeting and an invitation to connect in Korea (한국).
  • In the workplace, hoesik (회식, company dinners/gatherings), where the entire team gathered to eat and drink, was a ritual for building relationships and a sense of belonging (→ #1140 Hoesik Culture).
  • Because of this, people eating alone were once easily perceived as someone without friends or lonely. It was also common for restaurant owners not to welcome solo customers, or for menus to start from portions for two people.

This tradition and solo culture directly clash. What's interesting is that honbap (혼밥) and honsul (혼술) emerged not merely as the 'disappearance of tradition,' but also as a reaction to it. As generations tired of forced hoesik (회식, company dinners/gatherings) and group gatherings actively chose "time for myself," honbap (혼밥) became a liberation from the fatigue of 'eating together.' Rather than tradition vanishing, society has shifted to one where eating together vs. eating alone both exist.

④ Honbap Infrastructure — From 'Levels' to Solo Seats and Convenience Stores

As honbap (혼밥) culture spread, Korean (한국) society quickly developed infrastructure suitable for solo dining.

Honbap (혼밥) Levels (A Humorous Take on Honbap Difficulty)

On the Korean (한국) internet, there's a famous 9-level difficulty chart asking, "What's your honbap (혼밥) level?" It ranks places from easiest to most difficult (=requiring courage) for solo dining.

Level Place Perceived Difficulty
1~3 Convenience store (편의점) · Student cafeteria · Fast food Easy (for anyone)
4~5 Bunsikjip (분식집, snack bar) · Regular restaurant Normal
6~7 Matjip (맛집, popular restaurant) · Family restaurant Requires some courage
8 Gogitjip (고깃집, Korean BBQ restaurant) Expert (usually group menu)
9 Honsul (혼술, solo drinking at a bar/pub) Master

While this chart is a joke, the very recognition that "honbap (혼밥) has levels" demonstrates the spread of solo culture.

Actual Infrastructure

  • Solo Gogitjip (1인 고깃집, Korean BBQ restaurant): Grill meat at your own pace in a single seat with an individual burner — a symbolic change that redesigned gogi (meat), originally a representative 'shared' menu item, for honbap (혼밥, solo dining).
  • Partitioned Solo Seats: As Japan's Ichiran Ramen (이치란 라멘) style (a partition at every seat) became known, more restaurants in Korea (한국) now offer counter-style and bar seating (fully partitioned seating is still rare).
  • Convenience Store (편의점) Dosirak (도시락) and Ready Meals (HMR): Single-serving packaged and instant foods have grown explosively. The convenience store (편의점) is the easiest starting point for honbap (혼밥).
  • Delivery (배달): As single-serving delivery became commonplace, it underpins honbap (혼밥) and honsul (혼술) at home.
  • Korean media call this huge single-person consumption market the '1conomy (1코노미)' (1 + economy).

⑤ ★ Two Sides — The Light of Freedom and the Shadow of Isolation

Honbap (혼밥) and honsul (혼술) culture is neither something to glorify nor something to be pessimistic about. You have to look at both faces together to be accurate.

☀️ Bright Side (Freedom·Self-Care) 🌑 Dark Side (Isolation·Loneliness)
Time At my own pace, no worrying about others' opinions Absence of someone to share with
Relationships Liberation from hoesik (회식) and group fatigue Weakening of social connection
Emotion A complete 'time just for me'·self-care Risk of loneliness and depression
Society Respect for individuals·diversity Concern over social isolation·godoksa (고독사)

The bright side — honbap (혼밥) has changed from a sign of lack to a symbol of freedom and self-care. The joy of eating what you want at your own speed without minding others, the recovery of having time just for yourself, free from the fatigue of relationships. Shows like I Live Alone (나혼자산다) and Drinking Solo (혼술남녀) changed perceptions by portraying this enjoyment as normal and cool.

The dark side — at the same time, the surge in single-person households (1인 가구) brings the shadow of social isolation. One survey finds that about 1 in 3 adults in Korea (한국) are in a state of social isolation, with no one to rely on even when they need help (Ministry of Health and Welfare (복지부), verification recommended), and godoksa (고독사) (dying alone after living alone) is on a gradual but rising trend at about 3,924 cases in 2024 (verification recommended). The freedom of honbap (혼밥) and the loneliness of single-person households (1인 가구) are two sides of the same coin.

Balance: Honbap (혼밥) does not automatically mean loneliness. Many honjok (혼족) enjoy being alone voluntarily and contentedly. At the same time, the risk of isolation created by the rise of single-person living is real. Korean (한국) society is now working together on the homework of "enjoying freedom without losing connection."

⑥ If a Foreigner Wants to Eat Honbap (혼밥) — A Practical Guide

Prices and operations vary by venue — verification before visiting is recommended.

For travelers, honbap (혼밥) is in fact the freest way to enjoy Korea (한국).

  • The easiest start: Convenience stores (편의점) (dosirak (도시락), samgak-gimbap (삼각김밥), cup ramyeon (컵라면), with in-store eating counters), bunsikjip (분식집) (gimbap (김밥), tteokbokki (떡볶이)), and gukbap (국밥)·kalguksu (칼국수) restaurants take solo guests as a given, so it feels not at all awkward.
  • Honbap-friendly menus: Gukbap (국밥)·ramyeon (라면)·deopbap (덮밥)·solo gogitjip (1인 고깃집)·ramen (라멘) feel natural to go to alone. Conversely, bossam (보쌈)·jeongol (전골)·samgyeopsal (삼겹살) (at ordinary gogitjip (고깃집)) start from 2 servings at some places, so check "Irinbun doenayo? (1인분 되나요?)" (= Can I order one serving?).
  • Honsul (혼술): Korean-style honsul (혼술) is typically convenience store (편의점) beer or soju (소주) + anju (안주) at home or your accommodation. If outside, a bar seat at an izakaya (이자카야) or pocha (포차), or a place with a 'honsul welcome' (혼술 환영) sign, is comfortable.
  • Pro tips: Having a book or phone makes you feel at ease. Slightly avoiding peak time (lunch 12-1 PM) makes it easier to get a solo seat. Restaurants with kiosk ordering also put less language burden on you.
  • Mindset: Koreans (한국인) also eat honbap (혼밥) as an everyday thing now. No need to worry about others' opinions — it's in fact a way to experience Korea (한국) like a 'real local.'

❓ Foreigners' Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • Will people look at me strangely if I go to a restaurant alone? — Almost never. In an era when single-person households (1인 가구) are the most common, honbap (혼밥) is everyday. Gukbap (국밥)·ramyeon (라면)·bunsik (분식)·solo gogitjip (1인 고깃집) are especially comfortable.
  • Can I go alone to gogitjip (고깃집) or jeongol (전골) restaurants? — Some ordinary gogitjip (고깃집) start from 2 servings. Look for a solo gogitjip (1인 고깃집), or check "Irinbun ganeunghangayo? (1인분 가능한가요?)" (= Is one serving possible?) before ordering.
  • Where do I do honsul (혼술)? — The standard of Korean-style honsul (혼술) is to buy beer or soju (소주) + anju (안주) at a convenience store (편의점) and enjoy it lightly at your accommodation. If outside, a bar seat at an izakaya (이자카야) or pocha (포차) is comfortable.
  • Do honbap (혼밥)/honsul (혼술) have negative meanings in Korea (한국)? — They somewhat did in the past, but now the positive image of freedom and self-care is stronger. However, looking at society as a whole, concern about isolation from the rise of single-person living is also being discussed.
  • Doesn't that contradict 'hoesik culture (회식 문화)'? — Both exist. Collective hoesik (회식) (→#1140) and individual honbap (혼밥) coexist in Korea (한국) today. Honbap (혼밥) is also a reaction to hoesik (회식) fatigue.
