Korean Jjimjilbang/Bathing Culture — Why do we wash naked with strangers and spend the night in a sauna?

Jjimjilbang·Public bathhouse·Seshin(steamed rice)·Sheep head towel·Sikhye·Elbanseok egg — Explainer of Korean bathing and steaming culture for foreigners. Not ‘a strange place where you wash naked’ but ‘why, what, and how’.

There is a scene that appears frequently in K-dramas. The main characters wear sheep-shaped towels on their heads, drink sikhye through straws, and sleep lying on the floor. Families go together, couples go on dates, and business travelers spend the night there instead of a hotel. This is jjimjilbang. And in the bathing area next to it, Koreans wash themselves naked with strangers. To foreigners, this is shocking — “Why are you bathing naked in front of strangers?”, “Isn’t this a bit strange (=sexual)?”, “Is the sauna a hotel?” But to Koreans, this is not strange at all. It is the result of the overlap of the hundreds-year-old thermal tradition, the hygiene view that 'washing is not something to be ashamed of, but to be clean', and the social culture of being together naked makes you closer. This article is not about “Which sauna is best” (→ practical guide), but explains the way of thinking, history, and practical manners behind it from a foreigner’s perspective. ★ At a Glance — Jjimjilbang vs Bathhouse vs Sauna

The three words are often used interchangeably, but they are slightly different in Korea.

It's easy to think of it as a bathhouse inside a sauna. Korea's first modern jjimjilbang appeared in Busan in 1993, combining bathing and jjimjil facilities.

⚠️ 3 things foreigners often misunderstand

1. "Nudity in front of strangers = shame" ❌ — In Korean bathing culture, nudity is 'a natural condition for washing cleanly'. The bathing area is separated for men and women, and only people of the same gender are present. No one looks at other people's bodies — it's good manners not to even look sideways. It is not a feeling of shame, but an underlying sense of normality that 'we all come to wash the same way'.

2. "Nude bathing = sexual" ❌ — This is the biggest misconception. Nudity in Korean bathhouses is strictly non-sexual. It is for hygiene and relaxation, and has no sexual context whatsoever. This is clearly demonstrated by the fact that it is a family space where parents, young children, and grandmothers and granddaughters go together. (Sexual service establishments are completely different from ‘bathhouses’ and should not be confused with regular bathhouses and saunas.)

3. "Jjimjilbang = just cheap lodging" ❌ — It is true that it is open 24 hours a day, so overnight stays are possible, but the essence of the jjimjilbang is a complex space for thermal healing + socializing + relaxation. Accommodation is just an additional function, and most Koreans go to cultural spaces where they can ‘wash, sweat, rest, and play.’

① What is a jjimjilbang — a 24-hour complex space where you can wash, sweat, rest, and play?

Jjimjilbang literally means ‘a sauna room,’ but in reality, it is a unique Korean complex space that combines a bathhouse + sauna facilities of various temperatures + spaces for rest, entertainment, and food and beverages. Most operate 24 hours.

The typical jjimjilbang movement line is like this.

  • Entrance: Take off your shoes and put them in the shoe cabinet, pay the admission fee at the counter, and receive sauna clothes (short-sleeved T-shirt + shorts) and locker key.
  • Bathing area (men and women separated): Take off your clothes and go in naked to use the showers, tubs, and sauna. Here you can also get a sesin (세신, body scrub) — commonly called ttaemiri (때밀이) — if you want.
  • Common jjimjil area (shared by men and women): After bathing, change into your sauna clothes and step out into a large shared hall used by both men and women. Many places have themed heated rooms such as the bulgama (불가마, charcoal kiln room), hanjeungmak (한증막, steam hut), hwangto-bang (황토방, ocher clay room), a salt room, and an ice room, plus a sleeping room, a restaurant/snack counter, and sometimes even a comic-book room, PC room, karaoke room, or massage chairs.

Each jjimjilbang runs at a different temperature. Roughly, the bulgama runs 40–50°C, a dry sauna 70–100°C, and a hanjeungmak up to 70–130°C. Temperature and benefits are usually posted at the entrance, so you can check and choose.

② Bathhouse & Nude Bathing Culture — Why Wash 'Naked'

At the core of every jjimjilbang is the daejung mokyoktang (대중목욕탕, public bathhouse). This is where foreigners are most often caught off guard: nude bathing.

The rules are simple.

  • Men and women are completely separated. There is a men's bath and a women's bath, and only people of the same gender share a space.
  • Inside, everyone washes naked, without swimwear. Wearing a swimsuit or underwear is actually considered awkward.
  • Before getting into the tub, you must shower and wash your body thoroughly. Because many people share the same tub water, getting in unwashed is a serious breach of etiquette.

So why bother getting undressed and bathing together? For Koreans, nudity in the bathhouse is a matter of hygiene, not exposure. Washing thoroughly means washing your whole body, and everyone in the same-gender space does exactly the same thing. So it isn't something to be embarrassed about. In fact, being in the same space after shedding the social 'shell' of clothing tends to break down formality between people — mothers and daughters, friends, and neighbors often grow closer at the bathhouse.

③ ★ Sesin (세신, Body Scrub) — The Highlight of Korean Bathing

The one experience foreigners never forget is sesin (세신, 洗身) — often called ttaemiri (때밀이) — Korea's signature full-body exfoliation. It's such a defining part of Korean bathing culture that international media (CNN and others) have featured it as the 'Korean scrub.'

What happens — You lie down on a waterproof bed in the bathing area, and a sesinsa (세신사, bath attendant) scrubs you head to toe with a rough glove-shaped towel, sloughing off old dead skin ('dirt,' 때). Afterward your skin feels smooth and your circulation feels revived. Sesinsa were once simply called 'ttaemiri,' but in a 2007 revision of Statistics Korea's job classification, the role became the recognized professional title 'bathhouse care specialist' (목욕관리사).

What an 'Italy towel' actually is — The rough scrubbing cloth is called an 'Italy towel' (이태리타월) in Korean. Despite the name, it's a Korean invention. In 1967, Kim Won-jo (김원조), who ran a textile factory in Busan, made it from viscose rayon fabric — and because the fabric he used happened to be imported from Italy, the name 'Italy' stuck. Italians themselves don't actually scrub this way.

Sesin steps (for foreign visitors):

1. First, soak in the tub for at least 20–30 minutes to soften your skin thoroughly (the dead skin needs to soften before it scrubs off easily).

2. When the sesinsa calls you, lie down on the waterproof bed.

3. They scrub your whole body in order — head, back, arms, legs, feet. It's quite a firm scrub, so it may sting at first — that's normal.

4. Afterward you're rinsed off with water; some places finish with a cucumber or milk massage, or lotion.

5. Prices vary by shop and course, but a basic sesin usually runs ₩15,000–30,000 (checking before your visit is recommended).

Tip: Sesin is a strong stimulation for the skin, so if your skin is sensitive or you have a wound or skin condition, it's best not to push through it.

④ Jjimjilbang Icons — Sheep-Head Towel, Sikhye, and Elvan-Stone Eggs

Jjimjilbang have a set of 'unwritten rule' items that show up constantly in K-dramas. Know them going in, and you can enjoy the place like a local.

  • Yangmeori towel (양머리, 'sheep head') — Roll a towel inward from both ends and flip it onto your head, and it looks like sheep's ears (or rabbit ears) — the jjimjilbang's signature look. It has practical uses (absorbing sweat, protecting your hair), but above all it's the symbol of the jjimjilbang. How to make one: spread the towel out, roll both ends inward, then flip the middle over your head.
  • Sikhye (식혜) — A sweet traditional rice drink. A cold sikhye after sweating it out in the heated rooms is the classic jjimjilbang ritual. It's usually sold ice-cold at the snack counter in a blue-lidded container. Besides sikhye, plum (maesil), sujeonggwa (cinnamon-persimmon punch), and green tea drinks are also common.
  • Maekbanseok eggs (맥반석 계란, elvan-stone eggs) — Smoked eggs roasted using the jjimjilbang's own heat. Usually sold with a small packet of salt. Peel the shell, dip lightly in salt, and eat with sikhye — the classic jjimjilbang combo.
  • Seaweed soup and ramyeon — Larger jjimjilbang with a restaurant also serve hearty meals like seaweed soup (miyeokguk), ramyeon, and bibimbap.

⑤ A Social, Family Space — Somewhere You Go 'Together,' Not Alone

Another key part of the jjimjilbang and bathhouse is socializing. Where a Western spa is usually somewhere you go to 'rest quietly alone,' a Korean jjimjilbang is a space where you go together and mingle.

  • Family: A classic family outing that parents and children go on together. Some locations even have a children's play area, making it easy to bring kids along.
  • Friends and couples: A space for dates and friendship — lying together in the common hall in your sauna clothes, chatting, and sharing sikhye and eggs.
  • Coworkers: People sometimes drop in with colleagues after a work dinner, or while on a business trip.

Undressing together, bathing together, and sweating it out lying on the same floor — the formality between people disappears. This connects to the distinctly Korean feeling of 'jeong (정)' and 'uri (우리, we)' — the closeness that builds up from being physically together.

⑥ Why Did Bathing 'Together' Become Normal — History

This bathing culture wasn't always taken for granted. Koreans, too, were once uncomfortable undressing and washing with strangers.

  • The roots of heat therapy: Korea had its own underfloor heating culture called ondol (온돌), and during the Joseon dynasty there were bulhanjeungmak (불한증막) — sweat huts that used the residual heat from charcoal and pottery kilns. These are considered the prototype of today's jjimjilbang.
  • The start of nude public bathing: Korea's first modern public bathhouse opened in 1905 in Seorin-dong, Seoul, but soon closed due to resistance to the idea of "bathing naked with strangers." Public bathhouses only became established in earnest from the 1920s (Pyongyang in 1924, Seoul around 1925), and even then there was pushback along the lines of "how can you show your bare body to a stranger?"
  • Hygiene education changed the mindset: As generations who received hygiene education in school from a young age grew up, bathing stopped being 'something shameful' and became 'something you simply have to do.' In other words, Korea's nude bathing culture is a modern product of a shift in perception from 'taboo' to 'hygiene.'
  • The birth of the jjimjilbang: In 1993, a modern jjimjilbang combining bathing facilities with heated rooms appeared in Busan, and the traditions of ondol, hanjeungmak (한증막), and the bathhouse evolved into a 24-hour complex space.

In short, the culture of 'bathing naked together' is the result of thermal tradition + a modern hygiene mindset + Korean communal sentiment merging over the course of a century.

⑦ ★ A Practical Guide for Foreigners — How Not to Be Caught Off Guard

Prices and hours are estimated as of 2026 and vary by location — checking before your visit is recommended.

Here are the points where foreigners tend to get caught off guard at a jjimjilbang or bathhouse, in order.

Before you go in

  • Cost: A neighborhood jjimjilbang admission fee runs ₩8,000–20,000 (about $6–15), and premium or late-night entry is ₩20,000–30,000+. Using only a neighborhood bathhouse is cheaper (from around ₩7,000).
  • What to bring: Towels are usually provided (or available for a small fee). Bringing just a change of underwear is enough. Toiletries can be bought at the snack counter.

The bathing area (most important)

  • Men and women separated: Only the same gender enters the bathing area. Check the entrance color/signage (blue for men, red for women is common).
  • Nudity required: Inside the bathing area, you don't wear swimwear or underwear. Nudity is the default. (Sauna clothes are worn afterward, in the common area.)
  • Wash before soaking: You must shower before getting into the tub. This is the single most important rule of etiquette.
  • Eye contact manners: Don't stare at other people. Photography is strictly forbidden (many places even restrict bringing phones in at all).

The common jjimjil area

  • After bathing, change into your sauna clothes before coming out. This area is shared by men and women, so you must be dressed.
  • The routine in the heated rooms is to sweat for 5–15 minutes, come out, and rest, repeating as you like. Staying in too long can make you dizzy, so don't push it.
  • Staying hydrated is essential — drink sikhye or water often.

Staying overnight (if you want to)

  • A 24-hour jjimjilbang has a sleeping room with mats and pillows where you can spend the night. It's not a hotel, but it's popular as a budget-friendly place to stay.
  • Keep valuables in your locker. Many places charge an extra fee for late-night hours.

A note on tattoos: Some traditional bathhouses and jjimjilbang restrict entry if your tattoos are large. If you have a large tattoo, it's safer to choose a foreigner- and tourist-friendly location.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) for Foreigners

  • Do I really have to be naked? Can't I wear a swimsuit? — Nudity is the default in the bathing area. Wearing a swimsuit or underwear is actually seen as awkward. But rest assured, men and women are completely separated and only your own gender is present. You get dressed afterward, in the common jjimjil area.
  • Isn't this a sexual atmosphere? — Not at all. Nudity in Korean bathhouses is for hygiene and relaxation, and it's a completely non-sexual space that families and children go to together. (Sexual service establishments are something entirely separate from regular bathhouses and jjimjilbang.)
  • Do I have to get a sesin (body scrub)? — It's optional. If you want one, soak in the tub for 20–30 minutes first, then ask a sesinsa. It may sting at first from the firm scrubbing, but your skin will feel smooth afterward. If your skin is sensitive, you can skip it.
  • Can I go alone? — Yes. Koreans often go with family or friends, but enjoying it quietly by yourself is completely fine too.
  • What if English doesn't work? — Large jjimjilbang and tourist-area locations tend to have English signage, but a neighborhood bathhouse may be Korean-only. If you know the sequence — entry → locker → wash → soak — you barely need to speak at all.
  • Can I take photos? — Absolutely not in the bathing area. Many places even restrict bringing phones in at all. Even in the dressed common area, be careful not to capture other people in your shots.
