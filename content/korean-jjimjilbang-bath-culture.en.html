Korean Jjimjilbang & Bathhouse Culture — Why Koreans Bathe Naked With Strangers and Spend the Night in a Sauna

> Jjimjilbang (찜질방) · Public Bathhouse (대중목욕탕) · Seshin (세신, body scrub) · Yangmeori (양머리, sheep head) towel · Sikhye (식혜) · Maekbanseok Gyeran (맥반석 계란) — A Korean bathing and sauna culture explainer for foreigners, focusing on "why, what, and how" rather than "it's just a strange place where people bathe naked."

You often see a recurring scene in K-dramas: characters wearing yangmeori sugeon (양머리 수건, sheep head towels) on their heads, sipping sikhye (식혜, sweet rice punch) with straws, and sleeping on the floor. Families go together, couples go on dates, and business travelers even spend the night there instead of a hotel. This place is called a jjimjilbang (찜질방, Korean bathhouse/sauna complex). And in the adjacent bathing area, Koreans bathe naked with complete strangers. To a foreigner, this can be shocking — "Why do they bathe naked in front of strangers?", "Isn't this a bit strange (sexual)?", "Is a jjimjilbang a hotel?" However, for Koreans, this is not strange at all. It's the result of a centuries-old thermal tradition, a hygiene mindset that "bathing is about cleanliness, not shame," and a social culture where being naked together fosters closeness. This article isn't about "which jjimjilbang is good" (for practical guides), but rather delves into the mindset, history, and practical etiquette behind it, tailored for a foreign audience. This is the 15th installment in our Korean Culture explainer series.

★ At a Glance — Jjimjilbang vs. Mokyoktang vs. Sauna

These three terms are often used interchangeably, but in Korea, they have subtle differences.

> Key Point: A mokyoktang (목욕탕) is a place for 'bathing' (naked, gender-segregated), while a jjimjilbang (찜질방) is a 24-hour complex that adds sauna facilities + relaxation areas to a mokyoktang. It's easiest to think of a jjimjilbang as having a mokyoktang inside it. The first modern jjimjilbang in Korea emerged in Busan (부산) in 1993, combining bathing and sauna facilities.

⚠️ 3 Common Misconceptions for Foreigners

1. "Naked in front of strangers = shameful" ❌ — In Korean bathing culture, nudity is simply 'the natural state for thorough cleansing.' Bathing areas are strictly gender-segregated, with only people of the same gender present. No one stares at others' bodies — it's considered good manners not to even glance. It's not about shame, but rather the normalcy of 'everyone is here to wash.'

2. "Naked bathing = sexual" ❌ — This is the biggest misunderstanding. Nudity in Korean bathhouses is strictly non-sexual. It's for hygiene and relaxation, without any sexual context whatsoever. The fact that parents and young children, or grandmothers and granddaughters, go together clearly demonstrates this. (Sexual service establishments are entirely separate from 'mokyoktang' and should not be confused with regular bathhouses or jjimjilbangs.)

3. "Jjimjilbang = just a cheap place to stay" ❌ — While they operate 24 hours and allow overnight stays, the essence of a jjimjilbang is a thermal healing + socializing + relaxation complex. Accommodation is merely a supplementary function; most Koreans visit for the cultural experience of 'washing, sweating, relaxing, and having fun.'

① What is a Jjimjilbang (찜질방)? — A 24-Hour Complex for Washing, Sweating, Relaxing, and Playing

Jjimjilbang (찜질방) literally means 'steaming room,' but in reality, it's a unique Korean complex that combines a mokyoktang (목욕탕, bathhouse) + various temperature sauna facilities + relaxation, entertainment, and dining areas. Most operate 24 hours a day.

A typical jjimjilbang (찜질방) experience follows this flow:

  • Entrance: Take off your shoes and put them in a sinbaljang (신발장, shoe locker). Pay the entrance fee at the counter and receive a jjimjilbok (찜질복, sauna uniform) (T-shirt and shorts) and a samulham (사물함, locker) key.
  • Bathing Area (Gender-Segregated): Take off your clothes and enter naked to shower, use the hot tubs (tang, 탕), and saunas. Here, you can also get a seshin (세신, body scrub) if you wish.
  • Common Sauna Area (Co-ed): After bathing, change into your jjimjilbok (찜질복) and come out into a large, co-ed hall. You'll find various themed saunas like bulgama (불가마, traditional kiln sauna), hanjeungmak (한증막, traditional dome sauna), hwangtobang (황토방, clay room), sogeumbang (소금방, salt room), and eoreumbang (얼음방, ice room), as well as sumyeonsil (수면실, sleeping rooms), restaurants/snack bars, and often even comic book cafes, PC rooms, noraebang (노래방, karaoke rooms), and massage chairs.

Each sauna room (jjimjilbang) has a different temperature. Generally, a bulgama (불가마) is 40-50°C (104-122°F), a dry sauna is 70-100°C (158-212°F), and a hanjeungmak (한증막) can go up to 70-130°C (158-266°F). Check the temperature and efficacy descriptions at the entrance before choosing which one to enter.

② Bathhouse & Naked Culture — Why Bathe 'Naked'?

At the heart of a jjimjilbang (찜질방) is the daejung mokyoktang (대중목욕탕, public bathhouse). This is where foreigners often feel most awkward about naked bathing.

The rules are simple:

  • Men and women are completely separated. There are separate namtang (남탕, men's bath) and yeotang (여탕, women's bath), and only people of the same gender use the same space.
  • Inside, you bathe naked, without swimwear. Wearing swimwear or underwear is considered awkward.
  • Before entering the hot tubs (tang, 탕), you must thoroughly shower and clean your body. Since many people share the same bathwater, entering without washing is a major breach of etiquette.

Why bathe naked, and together? For Koreans, nudity in a bathhouse is a matter of 'hygiene,' not 'exposure.' To clean oneself thoroughly, the entire body must be washed, and everyone of the same gender does so in a segregated space. Therefore, it's not a source of shame. On the contrary, being in the same space without the social facade of clothes can lead to breaking down social barriers — like mothers and daughters, friends, or neighbors from the same area becoming closer in the bathhouse.

③ ★ Seshin (세신) — The Highlight of Korean Bathing

One experience foreigners never forget is seshin (洗身), commonly known as ttaemiri (때밀이), a Korean-style full-body exfoliation. It's a representative Korean bathing culture, even featured by international media (like CNN) as 'Korean scrub.'

What it is — Lying on a waterproof bed in the bathing area, a seshinsa (세신사, bath manager/scrubber) vigorously scrubs your entire body, from head to toe, with a rough, glove-like towel, removing old dead skin cells ('ttae'). Afterward, your skin feels smooth, and you get a sense of improved blood circulation. The seshinsa (세신사) was once simply called 'ttaemiri' (때밀이), but in 2007, the Korean Statistical Office revised its occupational classification to recognize them as a professional occupation: 'mokyokgwallisa (목욕관리사, bath manager).'

The Identity of the Itaeri Towel (이태리타올) — The rough towel used for scrubbing is called 'Itaeri Towel (이태리타올)' in Korea. Despite its name, it is a Korean invention. In 1967, Kim Won-jo (김원조), who ran a textile factory in Busan (부산), created it from viscose rayon fabric. The name 'Itaeri' (Italy) came from the fact that the fabric used was Italian-made. Ironically, people in Italy do not scrub themselves in this manner.

Seshin (세신) Procedure (for foreigners):

1. First, soak your body in the hot tub (tang, 탕) for at least 20-30 minutes to thoroughly soften your skin (this helps the dead skin cells come off easily).

2. When the seshinsa (세신사) calls you, lie down on the waterproof bed.

3. They will scrub your entire body, in order from head, back, arms, legs, to feet. It's quite vigorous, so it might sting a bit at first — this is normal.

4. Afterward, you'll be rinsed with water, and in some places, they might offer a cucumber or milk massage or apply lotion.

5. The cost varies by establishment and course, but a basic seshin (세신) usually ranges from ₩15,000 to 30,000 (~$11-22 USD / ~80-160 RMB / ~1,600-3,200 JPY) (checking before your visit is recommended).

> Tip: Seshin (세신) strongly stimulates the skin, so if you have sensitive skin, wounds, or skin conditions, it's best not to overdo it.

④ Jjimjilbang (찜질방) Specialties — Yangmeori (양머리), Sikhye (식혜), Maekbanseok Gyeran (맥반석 계란)

Jjimjilbangs (찜질방) have 'unwritten rule' (gukrul, 국룰) items that frequently appear in K-dramas. Knowing these will help you enjoy the experience like a local.

  • Yangmeori Sugeon (양머리 수건, sheep head towel) — A towel folded on both sides and worn on the head, resembling a sheep's head (or rabbit ears). This is the jjimjilbang (찜질방) signature style. While it serves practical purposes like absorbing sweat and protecting hair, it is above all a symbol of the jjimjilbang. How to make it: Unfold a towel, roll both ends inward, and then flip the middle part over your head.
  • Sikhye (식혜) — A sweet traditional beverage made from rice. A refreshing sikhye (식혜) after sweating in the sauna is a jjimjilbang (찜질방) classic. It's usually sold cold with ice in a blue-capped container at the snack bar. Besides sikhye (식혜), maesil (매실, plum) juice, sujeonggwa (수정과, cinnamon punch), and green tea drinks are also common.
  • Maekbanseok Gyeran (맥반석 계란) — Smoked eggs baked with the heat of the jjimjilbang (찜질방). They are usually sold with a small packet of salt. Peeling the shell, dipping it lightly in salt, and eating it with sikhye (식혜) is the classic jjimjilbang (찜질방) combination.
  • Miyeokguk (미역국, seaweed soup) · Ramyeon (라면, instant noodles) — In larger jjimjilbangs (찜질방) with restaurants, you can also enjoy hearty meals like miyeokguk (미역국), ramyeon (라면), or bibimbap (비빔밥, mixed rice with vegetables).

⑤ Social & Family Space — A Place to Go 'Together' Rather Than Alone

Another core aspect of jjimjilbangs (찜질방) and mokyoktangs (목욕탕) is socializing. While Western spas are often places for 'quiet, solitary relaxation,' Korean jjimjilbangs (찜질방) are spaces for going together and bonding.

  • Family: A popular family outing where parents and children go together. Some places even have children's play areas, making them great for kids.
  • Friends & Couples: A space for dates and friendships, where people lie together in jjimjilbok (찜질복) in the common hall, chat, and share sikhye (식혜) and eggs.
  • Work: Colleagues might visit together after a hoesik (회식, company dinner) or during a business trip.

As people shed their clothes and bathe together, and then lie on the same floor sweating, social barriers disappear. This connects to Korea's unique sentiments of jeong (情, deep affection/bond) and "uri" (우리, "we/us" communal spirit) — a closeness built through shared physical presence.

⑥ Why Did We Start Bathing 'Together'? — History

This bathing culture wasn't always a given. Koreans initially felt awkward about bathing naked with strangers.

  • Roots of Thermal Culture: Korea had a unique heating culture called ondol (온돌, traditional underfloor heating system), and during the Joseon Dynasty (조선 시대), there were bulhanjeungmak (불한증막, traditional kiln saunas) where people sweated using the residual heat from charcoal and pottery kilns. These are considered the prototypes of today's jjimjilbang (찜질방).
  • Beginning of Naked Public Bathing: Korea's first modern public bathhouse opened in Seorindong (서린동), Seoul (서울) in 1905, but it soon closed due to public reluctance about "bathing naked with strangers." Public bathhouses began to take root in the 1920s (Pyeongyang in 1924, Seoul around 1925), but even then, there was resistance to "how can one show their naked body to strangers?"
  • Hygiene Education Changed Perception: As generations who received hygiene education in school grew up, bathing transformed from a 'shameful act' to 'a natural necessity.' In other words, Korea's naked bathing culture is a product of modern history, where the perception shifted from 'taboo' to 'hygiene.'
  • Birth of the Jjimjilbang (찜질방): In 1993, the modern jjimjilbang (찜질방), combining bathing and sauna facilities, emerged in Busan (부산), evolving the traditions of ondol (온돌), hanjeungmak (한증막), and mokyoktang (목욕탕) into a 24-hour complex.

Thus, the culture of 'bathing naked together' is the result of the thermal tradition + modern hygiene views + Korean communal sentiment converging over 100 years.

⑦ ★ Foreigner's Practical Guide — How Not to Be Awkward

> Prices and operations are estimated based on 2026 and may vary by establishment — checking before your visit is recommended.

Here's a sequence of points where foreigners often feel awkward in a jjimjilbang (찜질방) or mokyoktang (목욕탕).

Before Entering

  • Cost: Neighborhood jjimjilbang (찜질방) entrance fees are typically ₩8,000-20,000 (approx. $6-15 USD / ~40-110 RMB / ~800-2,100 JPY). Premium or late-night entries can be ₩20,000-30,000+ (approx. $15-22+ USD / ~110-160+ RMB / ~2,100-3,200+ JPY). Using only a neighborhood mokyoktang (목욕탕) is cheaper (from around ₩7,000).
  • What to Bring: Towels are usually provided (or available for a small fee). Just bringing a change of underwear is sufficient. Toiletries can be purchased at the snack bar.

Bathing Area (Most Important)

  • Gender Segregation: The bathing area is strictly for the same gender only. Check the entrance color/sign (men = blue, women = red is common).
  • Nudity Required: Inside the bathing area, do not wear swimwear or underwear. Nudity is the norm. (The jjimjilbok (찜질복) is worn in the common area after bathing.)
  • Shower Before Soaking: Always shower and clean your body thoroughly before entering the hot tubs (tang, 탕). This is the most important etiquette.
  • Eye Contact Etiquette: Do not stare at others. Photography is absolutely forbidden (some places even restrict phone entry).

Common Sauna Area

  • After bathing, change into your jjimjilbok (찜질복). This area is co-ed, so you must wear clothes.
  • In the sauna rooms (jjimjilbang), go in for 5-15 minutes to sweat, then come out and rest, repeating the cycle. Do not stay too long, as it can cause dizziness.
  • Hydration is essential — drink sikhye (식혜) or water frequently.

Overnight Stay (If Desired)

  • 24-hour jjimjilbangs (찜질방) have sumyeonsil (수면실, sleeping rooms) with mats and pillows where you can spend the night. While not a hotel, they are popular as budget-friendly accommodation.
  • Keep valuables in your locker. Many places charge an additional fee for late-night stays.

Tattoo Warning: Some traditional mokyoktangs (목욕탕) and jjimjilbangs (찜질방) may restrict entry if you have large tattoos. If you have a large tattoo, it's safer to choose foreigner- and tourist-friendly establishments.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) for Foreigners

  • Do I really have to be naked? Can't I wear a swimsuit? — In the bathing area, nudity is the norm. Wearing swimwear or underwear is actually considered awkward. However, men and women are completely segregated, with only the same gender present, so you can relax. You wear clothes in the common sauna area after bathing.
  • Isn't this a sexual environment? — Absolutely not. Nudity in Korean bathhouses is for hygiene and relaxation, and it's a completely non-sexual space where families and children go together. (Sexual service establishments are entirely separate from regular bathhouses and jjimjilbangs.)
  • Do I have to get a seshin (세신, body scrub)? — It's optional. If you want one, soak your body in the hot tub (tang, 탕) for 20-30 minutes, then ask a seshinsa (세신사). It might sting a bit at first due to the vigorous scrubbing, but your skin will feel smooth afterward. If you have sensitive skin, you can skip it.
  • Can I go alone? — Yes, you can. While Koreans often go with family or friends, it's perfectly fine to enjoy it quietly by yourself.
  • What if I don't speak Korean? — Larger jjimjilbangs (찜질방) and those in tourist areas often have English signage, but neighborhood mokyoktangs (목욕탕) might only have Korean. If you know the sequence of entering → locker → washing → soaking, you'll hardly need to speak.
  • Can I take photos? — Photography is absolutely forbidden in the bathing area. Many places even restrict phone entry. In the common area where you wear jjimjilbok (찜질복), be careful not to include other people in your photos.
