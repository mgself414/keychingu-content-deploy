Korean Ceramics & Crafts — Goryeo Celadon, the Moon Jar & the Beauty Shaped from Earth

Goryeo cheongja (고려청자, Goryeo celadon — bisaek & sanggam) · Joseon baekja (조선백자, white porcelain) · dal-hangari (달항아리, moon jar) · buncheong-sagi (분청사기) · Imjin War potters · najeon-chilgi (나전칠기, mother-of-pearl lacquerware) & maedeup (매듭, knots) · the Icheon (이천) pottery village — a Korean ceramics & crafts explainer for foreigners. Not "which museum holds what," but "why the world loves Korean ceramics even though they aren't flashy, and what aesthetic is shaped into the clay."

There's a line foreigners often say in a Korean museum: "They're pretty… but a bit plain, no?" To eyes used to the intricate patterns of Chinese porcelain or the bold colors of Japanese ware, Korean ceramics can look understated. Yet that very 'plainness' is exactly why the world's museums covet them. The mysterious blue glow of Goryeo celadon (cheongja (청자)), the flawless white of Joseon white porcelain (baekja (백자)), the perfectly imperfect moon jar (dal-hangari (달항아리)) — Korean ceramics boast by not boasting.

This article isn't "which museum is best" (→ that's a place guide). It unpacks the 'why' and the 'aesthetic' held inside Korean ceramics and crafts — the beauty of restraint, nature, and empty space (jeolje (절제, restraint) · jayeon (자연, nature) · yeobaek (여백, empty space)), refined over a thousand years where earth, fire, and hand meet. This is the 69th installment of the Korean Culture explainer series.

⚠️ 3 Things Foreigners Often Misunderstand

1. "Celadon and white porcelain are just old dishes" ❌ — Goryeo celadon's bisaek (비색, the kingfisher-blue color) and its sanggam (상감, inlay) technique were world-leading technology that even contemporary China couldn't match, and Song-dynasty people ranked it first under heaven. It's not simple tableware but the summit of cutting-edge craft.

2. "Korean ceramics must be a knockoff of Chinese or Japanese ware" ❌ — Inlaid celadon (sanggam-cheongja (상감청자), carving the clay and filling it with clay of another color) and the moon jar (dal-hangari (달항아리)) are uniquely Korean inventions found in neither China nor Japan. In fact, the Joseon potters carried off during the Imjin War (Imjin-waeran (임진왜란)) became the very roots of Japanese porcelain (Arita and Satsuma).

3. "With few patterns and only white, it's monotonous" ❌ — The 'emptiness' of Joseon white porcelain isn't a lack of skill but a deliberately emptied aesthetic. It restrained ornament and chose empty space and naturalness (yeobaek (여백) · jayeonseureoum (자연스러움)) — practicing minimalism 600 years ahead.

① What Korean Ceramics Are — A Thousand-Year Beauty Shaped by Earth, Fire & Hand

Ceramics (dojagi (도자기, 陶瓷器)) are vessels shaped from clay and fired in flame. The Korean Peninsula developed fine clay (taeto (태토, body clay)) and kiln technology early, and through the Unified Silla and Goryeo periods produced world-class porcelain (jagi (자기, 磁器)). Porcelain — fired at the high heat of 1,200–1,300°C, hard, and ringing with a clear tone — was a cutting-edge high-tech product for its day.

Korean ceramic history runs in three broad currents.

  • Goryeo celadon (cheongja (청자)) — the aristocratic beauty of blue (Goryeo, to the 14th c.)
  • Buncheong ware (buncheong-sagi (분청사기)) — the rustic charm of the transition from celadon to white porcelain (late Goryeo–early Joseon to 16th c.)
  • Joseon white porcelain (baekja (백자)) — the restrained dignity of white (all of Joseon)

The very flow from ornate celadon to emptied white porcelain mirrors the change in the spirit of the age — from Goryeo (Buddhism, aristocracy) to Joseon (Confucianism, the scholar-official). Look at the ceramics and you see what the people of that time found beautiful.

② Goryeo Celadon — Bisaek & Sanggam, Ranked First Under Heaven by Song China

Goryeo celadon (Goryeo cheongja (고려청자, Goryeo celadon)) is the first peak of Korean ceramics. The celadon technique itself came from China, but Goryeo raised it to a realm all its own.

  • The mystery of bisaek (bisaek (비색, 翡色, kingfisher color)) — The heart of Goryeo celadon is its blue. Firing in a reduction atmosphere that cuts off oxygen in the kiln yields a clear, deep blue-green like jade. In the early 12th century, a perfected bisaek glaze with almost no crackle was achieved, and the Song envoy Xu Jing called this color 'bisaek (비색)' and ranked it first under heaven.
  • Sanggam — a Korean invention (sanggam (상감, 象嵌, inlay)) — In the mid-12th century, Goryeo perfected the original technique of inlaid celadon (sanggam-cheongja (상감청자)). On the dried vessel's surface a design is carved out, the hollows are filled with white and black clay, then glaze is applied and the piece is fired. The result is a picture of cranes, clouds, lotuses, and willows (hak (학, crane) · gureum (구름, cloud)) seeming to float within the clear blue-green glaze. This refined method, almost unknown even in China, is Korean ceramics' pride — 'planting' a picture onto celadon.
  • An iconic piece — The celadon maebyeong with inlaid cranes-and-clouds design (cheongja sanggam unhangmun maebyeong (청자 상감운학문 매병, 梅甁)) is the masterpiece of Goryeo celadon, its broad shoulders flowing into a slender taper, the graceful curve in harmony with bisaek and sanggam.

Meaning: Goryeo celadon is an ornate, aristocratic beauty "that has both color (bisaek) and pattern (sanggam)." It holds the refined taste of a Goryeo where Buddhism flourished and aristocratic culture bloomed.

③ Joseon White Porcelain & the Moon Jar — The Beauty of Emptying, Perfect While Warped

With the rise of Joseon, the ceramic aesthetic turned 180 degrees. In place of ornate celadon, flawlessly white white porcelain (baekja (백자, 白瓷)) became the protagonist.

  • Why white — the spirit of Neo-Confucianism — Joseon's ruling ideology, Neo-Confucianism (seongnihak (성리학, 性理學) · sinyuhak (신유학)), held frugality, restraint, and integrity as the scholar's virtues. White was the very symbol of purity, thrift, and uprightness. So Joseon scholars regarded undecorated pure white porcelain (sunbaekja (순백자, 純白瓷)) as the most dignified vessel. White porcelain's 'emptiness' is not a lack of skill but an aesthetic chosen by thought.
  • The moon jar (dal-hangari (달항아리, moon jar)) — The summit of Joseon white porcelain is the round grand white jar made in the late 17th to mid-18th century, the moon jar. Because it is so large, the upper and lower halves are thrown separately and joined; in firing it warps slightly and becomes asymmetrical. Yet that subtle misalignment gives a natural charm — like a waxing moon rather than a full one — "the symmetry of asymmetry" (bidaeching-ui daeching (비대칭의 대칭)). This imperfection, whose expression shifts with every viewing angle, is itself the completion. Only about 30 Joseon moon jars survive worldwide — a precious treasure.
  • The name is modern — Kim Whanki — The name 'moon jar' isn't ancient. Originally called the baekja daeho (baekja daeho (백자대호, 白瓷大壺, large white jar)), it was the painter Kim Whanki (Kim Hwan-gi (김환기, 1913–1974)) who loved to paint it and, calling it 'dal-hangari (달항아리),' lifted it into the rank of art.
  • The white porcelain the world loves — The moon jar is now held by the British Museum, the V&A, and the Metropolitan Museum of Art, and as RM (Kim Nam-jun (김남준)) of BTS became known as a lover of Joseon white porcelain, it drew the world's fans' attention anew. It is a modern icon traded for millions of dollars at auction.

Meaning: White porcelain and the moon jar are a beauty "that fills by taking away." Choosing natural misalignment over perfect symmetry, pure-white empty space over ornate pattern — this is the essence of Korean aesthetics.

④ Buncheong Ware & the Pottery War — Rustic Charm, and Potters Carried Off to Japan

In the passage from celadon to white porcelain, and amid the tragedy of the Imjin War, Korean ceramics left yet another story.

  • Buncheong ware (buncheong-sagi (분청사기, 粉靑沙器)) — Ceramics made from late Goryeo, branching off from inlaid celadon, down to the 16th century. A grey-blue body is coated with white clay (baekto (백토, white clay)) and decorated over it; neither as refined as celadon nor as neat as white porcelain, its trait is a free-spirited, rustic charm. The abstract motifs of fish, grass, and flowers dashed off without hesitation are the face of Korean ceramics most beloved by modern potters and designers.
  • The Imjin War = the 'Pottery War' — Japan calls the Imjin War of 1592 (Imjin-waeran (임진왜란)) the 'Pottery War' (dojagi jeonjaeng (도자기 전쟁, 陶磁器戦争)), because Toyotomi Hideyoshi even formed special units to carry off Joseon's potters en masse. Japan at the time could not make porcelain, and Joseon potters' skill was wealth and power itself.
  • Joseon potters who became the root of Japanese porcelain — The carried-off potter Yi Sam-pyeong (Yi Sam-pyeong (이삼평)) found a white-clay mine in Arita, Saga, fired Japan's first white porcelain, and is revered as the founding father (dojo (도조, 陶祖)) of Japanese ceramics. Sim Dang-gil (Sim Dang-gil (심당길)) and others carried off from Namwon built up Satsuma ware (Sasseumayaki (사쓰마야키, 薩摩燒)) in Kagoshima. This Japanese porcelain was exported in bulk to 17th-century Europe and even influenced European ceramics (Meissen and the like). Korean ceramic skill spread to the world by way of tragedy.

Meaning: Buncheong ware shows the beauty of "unadorned freedom," and the Pottery War shows that "Korean ceramic skill was a cutting-edge the world coveted."

⑤ Traditional Crafts — Mother-of-Pearl Lacquerware & Knots, the Aesthetic of the Fingertips

There is a Korean craft as deep as ceramics. As much as earth (ceramics), it is an art of the fingertips that handles mother-of-pearl, thread, wood, and paper.

  • Najeon-chilgi (najeon-chilgi (나전칠기, 螺鈿漆器, mother-of-pearl lacquerware)) — Meaning 'a vessel (gi (器)) decorated (jeon (鈿)) with shell (na (螺)) and lacquered (chil (漆)).' Abalone and conch shells are ground as thin as a hair into mother-of-pearl (jagae (자개)), affixed as patterns to a wooden surface, then finished with repeated coats of lacquer (otchil (옻칠)). Depending on the angle of the light, the mother-of-pearl shimmers in rainbow iridescence. Techniques include kkeuneumjil (kkeuneumjil (끊음질)), cutting the shell into thread-like strips for geometric patterns, and jureumjil (jureumjil (줄음질)), sawing out plum-blossom, bamboo, and tortoise motifs. It formed its own style in Goryeo, carried on into Joseon, and Tongyeong (Tongyeong (통영)) in South Gyeongsang is the famed center. In 1966 it was designated and protected as the National Intangible Heritage of najeonjang (najeonjang (나전장), master mother-of-pearl artisan).
  • Maedeup & embroidery (maedeup (매듭, knots) · jasu (자수, embroidery)) — Traditional knots that weave a single cord in left-right symmetry into shapes of chrysanthemum, dragonfly, and butterfly (gukhwa (국화, chrysanthemum) · jamjari (잠자리, dragonfly) · nabi (나비, butterfly)), and embroidery stitched stitch by stitch in colored thread, are delicate inner-chamber crafts that adorned ornaments (norigae (노리개)), wrapping cloths (bojagi (보자기)), and dress.
  • Other crafts — From hanji crafts (hanji (한지), making boxes and small goods from mulberry paper), to lacquering (otchil (옻칠), protecting and decorating vessels and furniture with natural coating), to woodcraft (mokgongye (목공예), joinery furniture assembled without nails) — Korean crafts consistently share an aesthetic of restraint that brings out natural materials and finishes them without excess.

Meaning: Traditional crafts are made "slowly, by hand, bringing the natural material to life." In an age of fast mass production, the value only grows more precious.

⑥ The Icheon Pottery Village & Hands-On Experience — Shape the Clay Yourself

There is a place where you can go beyond 'seeing' ceramics and shape them yourself.

  • Icheon — the city of ceramics (Icheon (이천, 利川)) — Icheon in Gyeonggi Province is Korea's representative pottery village with some 300 kilns and workshops (gama (가마, kiln) · gongbang (공방, workshop)), designated a UNESCO Creative City (in crafts and folk art). Workshops cluster densely in Yespark (Yeseupakeu (예스파크, the ceramic art village)) and the Sagimakgol pottery village (Sagimakgol (사기막골)) around Sindun-myeon.
  • The Icheon Ceramics Festival — Korea's largest ceramics festival, held each year in spring (late April–early May). Wheel-throwing experiences (a professional potter guides you alongside, with the finished piece received after firing), direct ceramic sales (deepest discounts), and performances and exhibitions all come together. With free admission and parking, it's great for foreigners too.
  • Pottery experience — Beyond Icheon, one-day classes like wheel-throwing, hand-building (haendeubilding (핸드빌딩, shaping by hand)), and painting are common at workshops in Seoul, Gyeongju, and elsewhere. The feel of the clay turning and taking shape in your hands is another charm of Korean ceramics that you can't get from behind museum glass.

In practice: A ceramics experience usually takes 2–4 weeks from shaping → drying → kiln firing, so receiving the finished piece by delivery is recommended, or only when your schedule allows. There are also simple courses where you glaze and fire on the spot, so ask the workshop in advance.

⑦ The Korean Aesthetic Within Ceramics — Restraint, Nature & Empty Space

The aesthetic running through Korean ceramics and crafts is, in a word, the 'beauty of taking away.' It stands out clearly against the ceramics of other countries.

  • Restraint (jeolje (절제, 節制, restraint)) — It deliberately reduces ornament. Joseon white porcelain emptied its patterns not for want of skill but because of the scholar's spirit (seonbi jeongsin (선비 정신)) that saw frugality as a virtue. The courage to leave empty a place that could be filled — that is the beauty of restraint.
  • Nature (jayeon (자연, 自然, nature)) — It prizes natural misalignment over artificial perfection. Like the warped moon jar or the unrestrained brushwork of buncheong, it keeps as-is the expression born where the human hand meets the chance of earth and fire. This is called "the beauty of non-doing" (muwi-ui mi (무위의 미, 無爲)) — a naturalness with no forced ornament.
  • Empty space (yeobaek (여백, 餘白, empty space)) — The emptied white ground isn't merely blank but a space of 'possibility waiting to be filled.' The empty space of white porcelain is the empty space of the mind — a place left for the viewer to fill with imagination.

This aesthetic of restraint, nature, and empty space lives not only in ceramics. From the gentle palette of the five cardinal colors (obangsaek (오방색), #1147), to the flowing lines and planes of hanbok (hanbok (한복), #1189), to the emptied courtyard and eaves of the hanok (hanok (한옥), #1197) — Korean beauty consistently connects into one philosophy: "emptying rather than filling, bringing out nature rather than refining it." Ceramics are that philosophy crystallized in clay.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • What's the difference between celadon and white porcelain? — Celadon (cheongja (청자)) is blue (bisaek) ware representing Goryeo's ornate, aristocratic beauty; white porcelain (baekja (백자)) is white ware representing Joseon's restrained, frugal beauty. Celadon's key is its inlaid pattern (sanggam (상감)), white porcelain's the emptiness of pure white.
  • Why is the moon jar so famous? — Its round pure white with a slight warp — the 'symmetry of asymmetry' — is counted as the essence of Korean aesthetics, and the painter Kim Whanki elevated it into art. With only about 30 surviving, it's rare, and modern celebrities like BTS's RM love it, making it a global icon.
  • Where can I see Korean ceramics? — The ceramics gallery of the National Museum of Korea (Gungnip Jungang Bangmulgwan (국립중앙박물관)) in Seoul is the best place to see celadon, white porcelain, and buncheong in one spot. To shape some yourself, the Icheon pottery village and the Ceramics Festival (spring) are recommended.
  • What is najeon-chilgi? — A Korean traditional craft that affixes shell mother-of-pearl (jagae (자개)) as patterns and finishes it with lacquer (otchil (옻칠)). It shimmers iridescently with the light, with Tongyeong (Tongyeong (통영)) as the famed center. Small jewelry boxes and mirrors are popular gifts for foreigners.
  • What does the Imjin War have to do with ceramics? — Japan calls the Imjin War the 'Pottery War' because it carried off Joseon potters en masse, and they became the founders of Japanese porcelain (Arita and Satsuma). It's proof of how far ahead Korean ceramic skill stood.
