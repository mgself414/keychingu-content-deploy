Korean Hiking Culture — Why Korea's Middle-Aged Are So Serious About Mountains (The Sociology of Hiking Gear & Bukhansan)

> 70% of the land is mountains · Bukhansan (북한산) · hiking clubs · makgeolli (막걸리, rice wine) at the summit · hiking gear as everyday wear — a Korean hiking-culture explainer for foreigners. Not "which trail to climb," but "why, what, and how."

One of the first scenes that surprises a foreigner arriving in Seoul (서울): get on the subway and, even on a weekday morning, a crowd of people in their 60s and 70s decked out head to toe in expensive hiking gear pours out. Trekking poles in hand, backpacks on, wrapped in colorful technical jackets — and yet their destination isn't Everest but a downtown hill reachable by subway. In Korea, hiking isn't a niche hobby for enthusiasts. It's a national activity that a majority of adults do at least once a month, and for the middle-aged and older it's especially a way of life where exercise, socializing, and pride are all tangled together. Lately the MZ generation has joined in leggings, and there's even an official Seoul city hiking-tourism center for foreign tourists (they lend out gear in English). This article isn't about "which mountain to climb" (→ cross-link) but about why Koreans are this serious about mountains — the social picture and the practical side, at a foreigner's eye level. ⚠️ 3 Things Foreigners Often Get Wrong

1. "Isn't hiking just an old people's hobby?" ❌ — It's true the middle-aged-and-older share is high, but since COVID 2030 MZ-generation hikers flooded in, and with "san-stagram" (산스타그램, mountain + Instagram), hiking crews, and "100 famous mountains" challenges, the scene has gone completely young. The mountains are now a cross-generational activity in Korea.

2. "Why wear full gear just to climb a neighborhood hill?" ❌ — Korea is almost the only country where hiking gear became everyday clothing. Middle-aged people wear hiking gear and backpacks not just on the mountain but on walks, errands, and commutes. It's not an exaggeration — it's a cultural phenomenon (see ④ below).

3. "The mountains must be far and treacherous" ❌ — Over 70% of Korea's land is mountainous, so cities are ringed by mountains. From Seoul you can reach the entrance of a national-park-grade mountain in 30 minutes to an hour by subway. 'Nature inside the city' is the core appeal of Korean hiking.

① What Is Hiking Culture — Starting From '70% of the Land Is Mountains'

The first key to understanding Korean hiking culture is terrain. Over 70% of the Korean peninsula is mountainous. Even big cities are surrounded on all sides by mountains, so to a Korean a mountain isn't 'something distant to conquer' but a living space that's always right near home.

That gives Korean hiking two faces.

  • Everyday hiking: A majority of the hiking population regularly climbs low hills near home, usually under 500m. It's less a grand expedition than neighborhood exercise.
  • National scale: According to one survey, a majority of adults visit a mountain or forest trail at least once a month (a reported figure of about 78%, tens of millions of people). The numbers differ by survey and year, so focus on the fact itself that this is a 'national activity.'

In other words, if hiking abroad is a 'special weekend activity,' in Korea hiking is a culture embedded in daily life like breathing. This 'everydayness' is the foundation of everything that follows (hiking fashion, hiking clubs, makgeolli).

> ※ Statistics source: Korea Forest Service · Statistics Korea reporting. Exact figures shift by year of release — verifying the original data when citing is recommended.

② A Mountain Inside the City — Bukhansan, a Global Anomaly

The icon of Korean hiking is, without question, Bukhansan (북한산, 北漢山, approx. 836m). The point that surprises foreigners most is that this is a real national park stuck right next to a ten-million-person megacity.

  • World-class density: Bukhansan is a nature park adjoining the city center, known as one of the world's most-visited national parks per unit area (its past Guinness-related mention is often brought up). Reports put annual visitors in the 7-million range, consistently ranking #1 in visitor count among all of Korea's national parks.
  • The reversal of scenery: Granite peaks, the Joseon-era fortress wall (Bukhansanseong, 북한산성), and a panorama of downtown Seoul (서울) from the top all fit into one course. The experience of "taking the subway, lacing up your boots, and having the city laid out beneath your feet in 30 minutes" is unique to Korean hiking.
  • Neighboring mountains: From Dobongsan (도봉산, 740m), ridge-linked to Bukhansan, to Gwanaksan (관악산, 632m) in southwestern Seoul, the low Inwangsan (인왕산, 338m) beside Gyeongbokgung (경복궁), and Namsan (남산, 262m) in the heart of downtown — Seoul is itself a giant hiking city.

This 'downtown accessibility' is the very physical condition that lets Korea's middle-aged be so serious about mountains. With no need to travel far — one bus or subway ride and you're at a mountain — hiking becomes not a special event but a habit.

③ Why the Middle-Aged Are So Serious — The Sociology of Clubs, Summits, and Makgeolli

Now the core question. Why are Korea's middle-aged and older, in particular, so serious about mountains? It isn't simply about health. For Korean middle-agers, the mountain is an athletic field, a social club, and a stage for identity all at once.

  • Hiking clubs = the middle-aged social network: Korea is densely woven with sanakhoe (산악회, hiking clubs) organized by workplace, region, alumni, or hobby group. Every university has a mountaineering circle, larger companies often have in-house clubs, and every neighborhood has a residents' club. For middle-agers whose social ties shrink around retirement, a sanakhoe (산악회, hiking club) is a key channel for regularly meeting people and gaining a sense of belonging. (→ This 'relationship-centered' sentiment connects to #1135 jeong (정, 情) culture.)
  • Summit verification = a shared narrative of conquering together: Clubs take a group photo in front of the summit marker. It's a ritual for sharing the small achievement of "we conquered this mountain." They build out hiking schedules systematically and tackle the "100 famous mountains" together.
  • Makgeolli after the descent = the real main event: An indispensable scene in Korean hiking is a bowl of makgeolli (막걸리, rice wine) at the summit or after coming down. Pairing makgeolli (막걸리) with pajeon (파전, savory scallion pancake) after a sweat is a kind of reward ritual. In a sanakhoe (산악회), the post-descent meal-and-drink bonding is effectively half the point of the hike. It's not unusual for a Korean stranger to offer a foreign hiker a cup of makgeolli (막걸리) at the summit — an "only in Korea" moment people love to share.

In short, for Korea's middle-aged the mountain is not a solitary workout but a community activity. Health, nature, human ties, and a sense of achievement all fit into a single hike — no wonder they're serious.

> ⚠️ Note: In some areas, including national parks, drinking and cooking are restricted. The makgeolli (막걸리) culture is mostly a scene of private gatherings or permitted zones, and following park rules is the rule.

④ Hiking Gear as Everyday Wear — Korea's Unique 'Outdoor Fashion' Phenomenon

The facet of Korean hiking culture that foreigners find most curious: wearing expensive hiking gear like everyday clothes.

  • What's the phenomenon: Korean middle-agers wear hiking gear, technical jackets, and hiking backpacks not only on the mountain but on walks, grocery runs, commutes, and trips. At the local mart, in the park, even at the airport heading abroad, you'll often see Koreans in colorful hiking jackets. It's no exaggeration to say Korea is almost the only country where "hiking gear = casual wear" holds.
  • Why is that: ① Since mountains are everyday, hiking gear became everyday too; ② its functionality (light, warm, waterproof) is excellent and practical; and ③ during the 2000s outdoor boom, "being properly outfitted in real hiking gear" became a kind of etiquette and point of pride socially, spreading high-end brand consumption.
  • The brand landscape: Korea's outdoor market is fiercely contested between homegrown and overseas brands. Depending on the survey, domestic brands like Black Yak (블랙야크)·K2 (케이투)·Kolon Sport (코오롱스포츠)·Eider (아이더) and labels like The North Face (노스페이스)·Discovery (디스커버리) are strong. Interestingly, The North Face is an American brand but holds a near-national-brand status especially in Korea. (Brand rankings and market share differ by survey and year — reference only.)

This normalization of hiking gear isn't mere fashion but a cultural indicator of how deeply the mountain is embedded in life. The number of hiking outfits in your closet is, in effect, a measure of your intimacy with the mountains.

⑤ The MZ Generation Joins In — From 'Dad Hobby' to 'Hip Hobby'

The era when hiking was the exclusive domain of the middle-aged is over. Since COVID-19, the 2030 generation has rushed to the mountains en masse, and hiking culture itself has gotten younger.

  • What changed: Younger people call it a hiking crew or hiking club instead of a sanakhoe (산악회), and post verification shots on social media under #san-stagram (산스타그램, mountain + Instagram). They climb in leggings and crop tops instead of hiking gear, and leaving a 'hip' photo at the summit has become a kind of play. The perception of hiking as a 'dad hobby' flipped into a symbol of gat-saeng (갓생, a diligent, productive life).
  • Why the appeal: ① a preference for outdoor over indoor exercise; ② a low-barrier sport you can easily enjoy solo; ③ a good fit for the urge to post on social media; and ④ a tie-in with lifestyle trends like "owanwan" (오운완, "today's workout done") and hiking dates. Trekking-style hiking — strolling lightly along low city mountains and dulle-gil (둘레길, perimeter trails) — is especially popular.
  • Dating · socializing: Hiking crews have become a new arena for meeting people. Press reports keep noting that the 2030 share of new club sign-ups has grown sharply. Often the goal is people more than the summit.

In short, Korea's mountains today are a fascinating generational crossroads where a sanakhoe (산악회) of 70-somethings and a crew of 20-somethings pass each other on the same trail.

⑥ A Practical Guide for Foreign Hikers — Courses, Gear, Access (K-Hiking Guide)

Good news. K-hiking has now risen as a must-do for inbound tourism, and Seoul city officially supports it.

> Operations and fees can change — verifying official channels before visiting is recommended.

  • Seoul Downtown Hiking Tourism Center (highly recommended): A foreigner-focused facility run by the Seoul Tourism Organization. Located at the trailheads of Bukhansan (북한산, via Ui Station)·Gwanaksan (관악산)·Ansan (안산), it guides courses in Korean, English, Chinese, and Japanese. The key is gear rental — hiking boots, hiking gear, poles, gloves, etc. (for foreigners, by reservation) — so you can come empty-handed and still hike. It also runs accompanied hiking programs for foreigners. The Bukhansan branch is a 5-minute walk from Bukhansan Ui Station (북한산우이역).
  • Course difficulty guide:
  • Entry (a foreigner's first hike): Inwangsan (인왕산, 338m) — fortress-wall path + Seoul view, short and gentle. Namsan (남산, 262m) — basically a city stroll.
  • Intermediate: Bukhansan (북한산) dulle-gil / low-difficulty courses, parts of Dobongsan (도봉산)·Gwanaksan (관악산) — but the summit areas have rugged rock ridges (암릉, amneung), so hiking boots and gloves are a must.
  • Caution: The summit areas of Dobongsan (도봉산)·Gwanaksan (관악산) and Bukhansan's Baegundae (백운대) have many rocky sections that are taxing for beginners. Don't overreach — pick a course to match your fitness and the weather.
  • Gear · clothing: Korean mountains may be low, but with many rocks and stairs, non-slip hiking boots matter. Prepare for UV and humidity in summer, ice in winter (crampons needed). Water and snacks are essential. If you have no gear, use the rental at the hiking-tourism center above.
  • Etiquette: Koreans are surprisingly friendly on the trail — a stranger may greet you or offer snacks or makgeolli (막걸리), so don't be flustered (it's goodwill). Carry your trash back out, stick to designated trails, and observe no-smoking and no-cooking zones.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • I have zero hiking gear — is it still possible? — Yes. The Seoul Downtown Hiking Tourism Center lends hiking boots, jackets, poles, etc. to foreigners (by reservation). Coming empty-handed is OK. Sneakers alone are plenty for Inwangsan (인왕산)·Namsan (남산).
  • Can I get help in English? — Yes. The hiking-tourism center provides guidance in four languages including English, and runs accompanied hiking programs for foreigners. The Bukhansan Ui Station (북한산우이역) branch is the main one.
  • I'm a beginner — which mountain first? — Start with Inwangsan (인왕산, fortress wall + view) or Namsan (남산, stroll level). Once you're confident, move to a low-difficulty Bukhansan (북한산) course. The summit areas of Dobongsan (도봉산)·Gwanaksan (관악산) are rocky and rough, so don't overdo it.
  • If someone offers makgeolli at the summit, can I drink it? — It's goodwill, so a light yes is fine, but drinking and cooking may be restricted in places like national parks. Read the situation, and to be safe avoid overdrinking and cooking.
  • Why does everyone wear pricey hiking gear? — Because of Korea's distinctive culture of hiking gear as everyday wear (see ④). Foreigners don't need to buy along — comfortable workout clothes + hiking boots are enough, and gear can be sorted by rental.
