Korean safety and security culture — Why is it okay to leave your laptop in a cafe and go to the bathroom?

Perceived safety·Low violent crime·CCTV·Lost and found·Citizenship awareness — Korean safety culture explainer for foreigners. Not “Korea is safe” but “Why is it so safe? What made it? Even its shadows and limitations.”

There is one 'culture shock' that foreigners who come to Korea experience almost without exception. At a cafe, the Korean sitting next to me leaves his MacBook and wallet on the table and goes to the bathroom or even walks to the counter to buy a drink. It is "taking charge" of your seat and leaving your luggage behind. In many countries this is more of a “take it” signal. However, in Korea, laptops, bags, and cell phones usually stay right where they are. A woman walks alone in an alley late at night, and her wallet, left on the subway, returns a few days later. Foreigners post this on social media saying, “Korea continues to surprise me.” This article is not intended to repeat the praise that “Korea is safe.” We unravel the background of why this kind of perceived safety is possible, and at the same time, we look at the shadows (surveillance society debate) and limitations ("Still, we must not let our guard down") of that safety in a balanced manner. Key: Safety in Korea is created by the overlap of systems such as CCTV and police and citizen awareness such as returning lost items. It cannot be explained by just one of the two (③④ below).

⚠️ 3 things foreigners often misunderstand

1. "Korea is 100% safe, so you don't have to worry about your luggage" ❌ — The perceived safety is very high, but there is no absolute safety. In places where tourists flock, such as Myeongdong, Hongdae, and Itaewon, pickpocketing, price gouging, and bar scams also exist (⑤ below). Let's remember the difference: "Koreans leave luggage, but I am a tourist."

2. "Korea is safe simply because the people are nice" ❌ — Citizen awareness is a big part, but behind it, there is a structural background, such as world-class CCTV, card payment tracking, and a virtual ban on guns. Rather than being “nice,” it is a combination of “niceness + a thorough system” (③ below).

3. "More surveillance cameras are always a good thing" ❌ — CCTV greatly contributes to safety, but it is also a subject of debate over privacy/surveillance society. Even in Korea, opinions are divided over “how far the photo should be taken” (⑥ below). Safety and privacy are a trade-off.

① Perceived safety — both statistics and ‘feel’ are high

According to foreigners, safety in Korea has two levels. Statistical Safety and Perceived Safety.

  • Violent crime rate: Korea's murder rate is reported to be about 0.5 per 100,000 people, which is far below the OECD average (about 2.6). Large cities such as Seoul, Busan, and Incheon are also compared to have lower violent crime rates than Western cities such as New York and London. (Figures vary by source/year — SOURCES below, recommended.)
  • Perceived safety (체감안전): What's more impressive than the numbers is the 'feeling.' Heading to the convenience store in slippers at 11 PM, a woman walking home alone late at night, leaving your belongings at a cafe table and stepping away — it's this everyday unguardedness that shocks foreigners most.

In other words, Korean safety is defined by the fact that the statistic — "dangerous things happen less often" — and the perception — "people live without thinking much about danger" — are both high together.

② 3 symbolic scenes — laptops, night streets, and lost property

The three scenes that most compactly capture Korean safety.

  • ☕ Reserving a cafe seat: People leave laptops, tablets, wallets, and designer bags on the table while they go to the bathroom or step up to order a drink. This is called jari matgi (자리 맡기) — the belongings themselves become the marker: "this seat is taken." It's an everyday sight in Korea, but for a foreigner it's "wait, they're just leaving that there?"
  • 🌃 Night streets, walking home alone: Walking home alone late at night or taking public transit alone is relatively normal, especially downtown. "Is it okay to go out alone at night?" is one of the most common questions foreigners ask — and most who try it say it was "better than expected."
  • 📦 Lost items come back: Stories of wallets and phones left on the subway, in a taxi, or at a restaurant being returned are shared often on social media. When someone finds a lost item, the culture is to return it directly or hand it in at a station office or to the police. (That said, recovery isn't guaranteed 100% of the time — see ④ and ⑥ below.)

③ Why is it safe, part 1 — the 'structure' of CCTV, payment tracking, and a gun ban

"People are just nice" isn't a complete answer. One pillar of Korean safety is structural infrastructure that's easy to overlook.

  • Dense CCTV: Korea, and Seoul in particular, is ranked among cities with the world's highest CCTV density (near the top in global city comparisons). Cameras blanket alleys, parking lots, elevators, buses, and subways. Police themselves say "a large share of violent crimes are solved using CCTV footage." (Camera counts and rankings vary by research body and year — check recommended.)
  • Traceable payments: In Korea, card, simple pay, and transit-card use overwhelmingly outpaces cash. It's easy to leave an electronic trail of what was bought where, and that raises the 'cost' of crime, producing a deterrent effect.
  • A virtual gun ban: Private gun ownership is strictly restricted. The structurally low risk of gun-related violent crime is a major factor behind Korea's perceived safety.

Together, these three create a deterrent environment where "if you do something wrong, you'll almost certainly be caught on camera, traced, and caught."

④ Why is it safe, part 2 — the social norm that 'touching someone else's things is a big deal'

Structure alone isn't enough either. The other pillar is civic awareness and social norms.

  • A culture of returning lost property: Picking up something someone else dropped and returning it to the owner or handing it in is treated as simply 'the obvious thing to do.' The habit of pitching in to help a stranger in trouble on the street works the same way. It's a voluntary safety net that functions in daily life even without any formal system behind it.
  • The lost-and-found system: On top of that sit the official yusilmul centers (유실물 센터, lost-and-found centers) for the subway, buses, and taxis. On the Seoul subway, a large share of reported lost items are returned to their owners or handed over to police (per 2025 figures, roughly half of reports are returned and about a third are handed to police — see SOURCES). It's a structure where the system processes citizens' goodwill.
  • "You don't touch what isn't yours": This is a norm drilled in strongly from childhood. The seat-reserving culture survives partly because of a shared understanding that taking someone else's belongings is something society condemns heavily.

In short, Korean safety is produced by the "surveillance system" (③) and "shared norms" (④) reinforcing each other.

⑤ ★ Balance 1 — "Still, don't let your guard down": the limits of safety

This is the most important balance to strike. Korea being safe ≠ it's fine to be careless. Tourists in particular need to know this difference.

  • Pickpockets (소매치기) at tourist spots: In crowded places like Myeongdong, Dongdaemun, Namdaemun, Hongdae, and Itaewon — especially at night — pickpockets and petty thieves do exist. Distraction tactics like "bumping into you" or "striking up conversation" have been reported. Koreans may leave their bags unattended, but tourists are easier targets.
  • Bar and nightlife scams: In areas like Itaewon and Hongdae, there are reports of price gouging (바가지), as well as "teaming" scams — where a friendly stranger approaches you on the street, leads you to a specific bar, and then hits you with a massive bill. Treat "free, overly friendly hospitality" with suspicion first.
  • In short, the perceived safety is real, but it isn't 'zero risk.' Keep valuables in a crossbody bag or inner pocket, and stay alert as you normally would in crowds or late at night. Enjoy Korea's safety, but keep up a traveler's basic guard.

The key point: this article isn't trying to scare you into thinking "Korea is dangerous." It's that overconfidence — "it's safe, so anything goes" — is what actually invites accidents. The wisest approach is to enjoy the safety without letting your guard down.

⑥ ★ Balance 2 — the shadow side of safety: the 'surveillance society' debate

It shouldn't be overlooked that the very CCTV that creates safety is, at the same time, a subject of debate within Korean society itself.

  • Privacy concerns: Dense CCTV reduces crime, but being "filmed everywhere" also touches on privacy and surveillance-society (감시사회) concerns. Even within Korea, opinion splits between "install even more for safety" and "it's already excessive." The debate has only grown as AI-analyzed CCTV (automatically detecting dangerous situations in footage) expands.
  • Surveillance isn't just CCTV: Some analyses estimate that Korea's rate of communications-data access and wiretapping runs higher than in Western democracies (estimates and methodology vary — check recommended). Safety and the state's tracking capability are two sides of the same coin.
  • Light and shadow come as a set: The "country where it's safe to leave your laptop unattended" that foreigners admire is, flipped around, also "a country that films and tracks just as much." Understanding Korean safety means seeing this trade-off too.

⑦ Safety tips for foreigners — Enjoy, but keep the basics

The safety level is high, but the below is basic that works in any country — Korea is no exception.

  • Valuables: Passport, cards, and cash dispersed. When in crowds, late at night, or in clubs, put your crossbag forward** and do not place your cell phone on the edge of the table.
  • In case of loss: For public transportation, call Official Lost and Found Center (subway station station office, Korea Transportation Corporation Lost and Found Center, license plate number on taxi receipt). For general loss, call 112 (police) or the nearest district office. The sooner you report, the higher the chance of recovery.
  • Night out: The city center is relatively safe, but first refuse offers to drink, overly friendly strangers, or to out-of-the-way bars. Be careful with your belongings after drinking.
  • Emergency numbers: 112 (crime/police), 119 (fire/emergency). For foreign tourist information and interpretation, call 1330 (Korea Tourism Organization 24 hours a day, multilingual).
  • Mindset: Don’t use “Korea is safe” as an excuse to get the normal attention of travelers**. Enjoy your safety, but remain vigilant as usual.

❓ Foreigners Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • Is it really okay to go alone at night? — Many reviews say that downtown and downtown areas are relatively safe. However, as everywhere, be careful in remote alleys, late-night club streets, and excessive drinking situations. There are many reviews saying it is “okay” ≠ it is not “100% safe”.
  • Can I leave my luggage at the cafe and go to the bathroom? — Koreans often do that. However, tourists are advised to take expensive and important items such as laptops and passports with them. Enjoy the safety culture, but manage your own risks.
  • Are there really pickpockets? — It's rare, but they exist in tourist areas, crowds, and late at night. Put it on the front of your bag in Myeongdong, Hongdae, Itaewon, etc. “Korea = 0 pickpockets” is an exaggeration.
  • Can you find lost items? — There are many cases of recovery and the lost and found system is well established, but it is not a 100% guarantee (there are also reports that the recovery rate is on the decline). Report it quickly and use the Lost & Found Center/112.
  • Is privacy okay if there are so many CCTVs? — Although it contributes to safety, it is a topic of privacy controversy even in Korea. Although there is no major inconvenience to travelers, it is good to know that this is the other side of “why is it so safe?”
