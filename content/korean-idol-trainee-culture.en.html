K-pop Idol & Trainee Culture — How K-pop Stars Are Actually Made

> Trainees, Agencies, Auditions, Lightsticks, Survival — An explainer of the K-pop production system for foreigners. It's not about 'who made it big,' but 'how they are made, and what are the lights and shadows of that process.'

One fact often missed by foreigners who love K-pop is that stars like BTS and BLACKPINK (블랙핑크) didn't just get discovered by chance and debut one day. The members who perfectly sing and perform synchronized choreography (칼군무) on stage are all former trainees (연습생) who joined an entertainment agency (기획사) years before their debut, training for over 10 hours daily in singing, dancing, foreign languages, and media skills. In other words, K-pop is less about an individual's burst of talent and more a product systematically designed and nurtured by Korean entertainment agencies. This system was first established by SM Entertainment (SM엔터테인먼트) in the mid-1990s, becoming the standard for the Korean music industry and now a model followed worldwide. This article, rather than focusing on "who's cool," explains the structure and culture behind how these stars are made — and the shadows behind the glamour — from a foreigner's perspective. This is the 30th installment of the Korean Culture explainer series.

> Note on Timeliness: K-pop is a rapidly changing field. The group, agency, and generation classifications in this article are general observations as of 2026, and the situations of specific individuals, groups, or agencies may change over time, so checking for the latest information is recommended.

> Key Takeaway: K-pop is not about 'discovered geniuses' but the 'result of a designed system.' This level of perfection is the secret to global success, and at the same time, the root of controversies surrounding trainee labor and pressure (see ⑥ below).

⚠️ 3 Common Misconceptions Foreigners Often Have

1. "K-pop stars are simply scouted for their talent" ❌ — Almost all K-pop idols undergo several years of trainee life (연습생 생활) before their debut. On average, this is 2-5 years, and sometimes even over 10 years of training. The perfection seen on stage is not by chance but the result of long-term investment and training.

2. "Agencies (기획사) = Just management companies" ❌ — Major agencies like HYBE, SM, JYP, and YG are not merely management companies but production systems that entirely design and operate everything from auditions (오디션) → training → debut → album production → marketing. The company even plans the group's concept, lore (세계관), and member lineup.

3. "K-pop is only glamorous" ❌ — Behind the light of global success and perfection, there are definitely shadows such as long-term unpaid trainee (연습생) labor, past controversies over long-term contracts (the so-called 'slave contracts' (노예계약)), appearance/weight pressure, and mental health issues. A balanced perspective is essential for true understanding (see ⑥ below).

① What is a K-pop Idol? — More Than Just a 'Singer'

In K-pop, an idol (아이돌) is not just a singer. They are closer to a comprehensive entertainer nurtured by an agency (기획사), performing a combination of singing, dancing, visuals, variety show appearances (예능), and fan communication.

  • They usually operate as a group (though solo artists exist, groups are dominant). Each member has a distinct role, such as main vocalist, lead dancer, rapper, visual, or leader.
  • The first full-fledged K-pop idol group in Korea is considered to be H.O.T., who debuted in 1996. The pre-training system introduced then became the standard for all subsequent agencies.
  • It's not just about music; stage performance, music videos, variety show appearances (예능), social media, and fan meetings are all part of a single package. The reason foreigners fall for K-pop is precisely this total sum of music + performance + fan experience.

In short, the starting point is that a K-pop idol is not just "someone who sings well," but a multi-performer nurtured by a system.

② The Trainee System — Audition, Training, Debut

The core of K-pop is its trainee (연습생) system. The path to becoming a star typically goes through three stages.

Audition & Casting — The Entry Point

  • There are broadly three paths to joining an agency (기획사): open auditions (공개 오디션), street casting (길거리 캐스팅), and online/global auditions.
  • Major agencies receive tens to hundreds of thousands of applications each year, but only a tiny fraction are accepted as trainees (연습생). (According to reports, SM is said to receive applications from around 300,000 people across 9 countries annually — numbers vary by time and media, checking is recommended.)
  • Selection criteria consider not only basic talents like singing and dancing but also a combination of diligence, attitude, image, and growth potential.

Training — Years Before Debut

Selected trainees (연습생) undergo systematic training at the agency's training centers (primarily in the Gangnam (강남) area of Seoul (서울)).

  • Vocal & Dance Lessons: K-pop choreography (안무) is technically challenging even among commercial popular music, often taking years to master.
  • Foreign Language Education: Targeting the global market, English, Japanese, and Chinese are virtually mandatory at major agencies (기획사).
  • Media & Variety Show (예능) Training: They are taught 'on-camera skills' such as interview techniques, broadcasting responses, facial expressions, and manners.
  • Intensity: Trainees (연습생) are often reported to train for 10-12 hours a day, 6 days a week, alongside their studies. Dormitory life is common, and strict rules apply regarding curfews, mobile phones, and dating.

Debut — Appearing as a Complete Package

If they pass evaluations and join the debut lineup, they enter the market as a complete group with a concept, songs, choreography (안무), visuals, and even a lore (세계관). What foreigners see as 'debut' is actually the finish line of years of unseen preparation.

Survival Auditions (서바이벌 오디션) — Public Competition

Beyond internal agency (기획사) evaluations, the culture of selecting debut teams through TV survival audition programs (서바이벌 오디션 프로그램) is also a characteristic of K-pop.

  • A prime example, 〈Produce 101 (프로듀스 101)〉 (2016~), gathered 101 trainees (연습생) from various agencies (기획사) to compete on stage weekly, with the final debut members chosen by viewer votes. Groups like I.O.I, Wanna One (워너원), and IZ*ONE (아이즈원) were formed through its seasons.
  • While such survival programs are interesting for turning the debut process into content and fan participation, they also face criticism for publicly amplifying competition and pressure (there have been instances of vote manipulation scandals — checking cases and outcomes is recommended).

The key is that K-pop's 'selection' involves various layers of competition, from private monthly evaluations to nationwide public voting.

④ Fandom Culture — Lightsticks (응원봉), Fan Clubs, Concerts

Another pillar that makes K-pop truly K-pop is its fandom (팬덤) culture. It's a community with names, colors, tools, and rituals that goes beyond just 'fans.'

  • Fan Club Names: Each group has an official fandom name. Just like BTS fans are ARMY and BLACKPINK (블랙핑크) fans are BLINK, these names are designated by the group or agency (기획사) at the time of debut. The names give fans a sense of identity and belonging.
  • Lightsticks (응원봉 or 'bong'): Each group has its uniquely designed lightstick (응원봉). Like BTS's 'ARMY Bomb (아미밤)' and BLACKPINK's 'Bl-Ping-Bong (블핑봉),' they incorporate the group's colors, logos, and symbols. A 'lightstick ocean (응원봉 바다)', where the concert venue is filled with a wave of lightsticks of the same color, is an iconic scene at K-pop concerts. (Some lightsticks can even synchronize colors via Bluetooth.)
  • Concerts & Fan Chants (응원법): Fans learn fan chants (응원법), which involve shouting member names or specific slogans together during certain parts of a song, to participate in performances. The appeal of K-pop concerts is that they are not just performances to 'watch,' but performances 'created together.'

This fandom culture leads to album sales, streaming, voting, and merchandise, and is also a real driving force supporting the K-pop industry. (Note: The strong bond and collective cheering shown by fandoms are also connected to Korea's unique concept of 'jeong (정)' → see "Deeper Dive" below.)

⑤ Global Success — How K-pop Went Global

K-pop's global success is the result of its system, content, and fandom interlocking.

  • Breakthrough: Around 2017-2018, BTS and BLACKPINK (블랙핑크) made significant inroads into the mainstream US charts, becoming a stepping stone that elevated K-pop onto the 'world music' stage.
  • Expansion: Subsequently, various groups like TXT (투모로우바이투게더) and Stray Kids (스트레이키즈) gained recognition on US charts like Billboard, expanding K-pop's reach. In the 2020s, fresh concepts breaking existing molds, such as NewJeans (뉴진스), also emerged (the latest activity status of specific groups may change — checking is recommended).
  • Cited Reasons for Success: ① The high performance perfection created by the trainee (연습생) system, ② The content package combining music videos, lore (세계관), and social media, ③ The spontaneous global spread of fandoms (fans directly handle translation, promotion, and streaming).

In other words, K-pop's globalization is accurately seen not as 'luck' but as the result of designed perfection × fandom power. And it is precisely that 'design for perfection' that gives rise to the shadows discussed in the next section.

⑥ ★ Light and Shadow — The Shadows as Clear as the Light

To truly understand K-pop, one must view it with a balanced perspective, acknowledging both its glamour and the darker aspects inherent in the system. Only praising it is only half an understanding.

Long-Term Unpaid Trainee (연습생) Labor

  • The trainee (연습생) period typically involves no salary. It's common for agencies (기획사) to initially cover lodging, meals, lessons, and costume costs, then settle (정산) these expenses from future earnings after debut.
  • The problem lies with the majority who do not debut. If they train for years but fail to make the debut lineup, that time becomes labor with little to no pay, leaving financial and emotional burdens on them and their families. One report stated that it can cost millions of dollars to train a single girl group member until debut (figures vary by media and time — checking is recommended).
