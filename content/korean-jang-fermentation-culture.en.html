Korean fermentation and fermentation culture — Why does the taste of Korean food come from ‘time’?

Soybean paste, soy sauce, red pepper paste, cheonggukjang, meju, soybean paste — Explainer of Korean fermentation culture for foreigners. Not ‘introduction to types of kimchi’ but ‘why, what, and how’.

There is a question that foreigners most often ask after eating a spoonful of soybean paste stew at a Korean restaurant. "Where on earth does this deep flavor come from? There's no meat in it." The answer is not spice, but time. Korean food is almost always covered in soybean paste (soybean paste, soy sauce, red pepper paste, and cheonggukjang) made by fermenting soybeans, and the paste is the result of fermentation that takes place over several months or years, and in some families, hundreds of years. When foreigners think of Korean fermentation, only kimchi comes to mind, but kimchi is just the tip of the iceberg. The real root is making soy sauce. The tradition is so deep that in December 2024, UNESCO registered ‘Korean soybean paste-making culture’ as an intangible cultural heritage of humanity (the ‘kimjang culture’ of making kimchi was already listed in 2013). This article is not about “Where to buy it” (→ cross-link), but explains why Korean food came to focus on fermentation, and the way of thinking, history, and philosophy contained in it from a foreigner’s perspective. Key: Soybean paste and soy sauce are brothers derived from the same ingredient (meju). Gochujang is a fermented red pepper paste found only in Korea, and Cheonggukjang has the shortest fermentation time. These four types are collectively called Jang(醬).

⚠️ 3 things foreigners often misunderstand

1. "Korean fermentation = Kimchi" ❌ — Kimchi is just vegetable fermentation. The real spine of Korean fermentation culture is soybean fermentation, or soybean paste. Almost all Korean soups, stews, and condiments have soybean paste, soy sauce, and red pepper paste at the bottom. UNESCO also added Kimjang (2013) and Jang-making (2024) to the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity separately — they are two huge, separate traditions.

2. "Fermentation = just something that has been left for a long time or something that has gone bad" ❌ — Fermentation is not neglect, but precise control that tames microorganisms. Umami is created when microorganisms (fungi and bacteria) break down soy protein into amino acids. This is why it has a deep flavor even without adding MSG.

3. "Miso = Same thing as Japanese miso" ❌ — Similar but different. Miso is usually fine and sweet because it uses rice malt (koji), while soybean paste is coarser, richer, and more savory because it is made by soaking whole soybeans in salt water. Above all, soybean paste has a unique manufacturing method separated from soy sauce in the same jar (③ below).

① Why fermentation? A ‘way to save time’ discovered by the cold peninsula.

Before fermentation was a taste for Koreans, it was a survival technique. In the era when there were no refrigerators in the Korean Peninsula, where winters are long, storage was essential to eating proteins and vegetables all year round, and fermentation was the answer.

  • Salt + Time = Preservation: Boiling beans and fermenting them with salt prevents spoilage and deepens the taste and nutrition (same principle for kimchi).
  • Without throwing anything away: The method of obtaining soybean paste and soy sauce from the same pot of beans (③) is a wisdom that uses resources to the end.
  • 'Time is an ingredient': The core philosophy of Korean fermentation. A good chapter cannot be made quickly. Maturation equals taste, and there is a saying in Korea that "food tastes good when the sauce is good" — because the starting point of all dishes is intestines.

In other words, Korean fermentation is not an “exotic delicacy,” but the basic infrastructure of Korean food refined to survive the long winter.

② 4 types of sauces — soybean paste, soy sauce, red pepper paste, cheonggukjang

Doenjang (된장) — The Base of Deep Flavor

A brown soybean paste made by fermenting meju — soybeans boiled, mashed, and cultured into bricks — in salt water for anywhere from several months to several years. It forms the bottom layer of doenjang stew and doenjang soup, and it's where most of Korean food's "savory depth" comes from. The more it simmers, the richer it gets; the longer it ages, the more valuable it becomes.

Ganjang (간장) — The Sibling Split from the Same Jar

Ganjang comes from the very same jar used to make doenjang: the salty liquid that seeps out of the fermenting meju is ladled off and aged separately. In short, doenjang and ganjang share one root (this separation step is called jang-gareugi — see ③). It's further divided into types such as guk-ganjang and jin-ganjang; the longer it ages, the darker it turns, and umami overtakes saltiness.

Gochujang (고추장) — A Fermented Red Pepper Paste Found Nowhere Else

A spicy, sweet, umami-rich red paste made by fermenting meju powder + glutinous rice (or barley) + red pepper powder + malt syrup. It's the red in bibimbap, tteokbokki, and grilled-fish glazes. The decisive difference from a simple "hot sauce" is that it's a fermented, compound seasoning — so the heat is followed by sweetness and depth. Sunchang, in North Jeolla Province, is famous as gochujang's home region.

Cheonggukjang (청국장) — The Fastest Ferment

Unlike the other three, cheonggukjang skips meju entirely: boiled soybeans are fermented for just two to three days in a warm spot (around 40°C). It's known for its sticky strands and strong aroma (a cousin of Japan's natto). The smell can be a hurdle for first-time foreigners, but it's prized among fermented foods as a health food.

③ Meju (메주) and Jangdokdae (장독대) — Fermentation's "Engine" and "Home"

Meju — Where Every Jang Begins

Doenjang, ganjang, and gochujang nearly all start from meju. Boiled soybeans are mashed, shaped into bricks, and hung in a warm spot until mold grows and cultures the surface — that's meju. This is when natural microorganisms (molds such as Aspergillus, bacteria such as Bacillus) take hold in the beans; those microbes later break down the soy protein into umami and aroma. Hence the saying "meju decides the taste of the jang" — meju is the engine of fermentation.

Jang-gareugi: Well-cultured meju is submerged in salt water for about two months, then separates into the clear liquid that rises to the top — ganjang — and the solids that settle at the bottom — doenjang. Ladling off and dividing the two is called jang-gareugi, "sauce-splitting." Getting two different sauces from one pot of beans is the most elegant moment in Korean fermentation.

Jangdokdae — Where the Jars Breathe

Fermented jang is aged in onggi (옹기) — earthenware jars — gathered in a jangdokdae, a sunny spot set aside in the yard for the jars. Onggi are not just containers.

  • A breathing vessel: Onggi walls are porous clay, letting air pass in and out. That subtle air exchange creates an ideal environment for fermentation microbes — something plastic or glass containers simply can't replicate.
  • Sun and daily care: Opening the lids to catch the sun on clear days, covering them when it rains, checking in on them — that everyday attentiveness is what finishes the flavor. The jangdokdae wasn't just storage; it was the center of the household, traditionally the domain of mothers and grandmothers.

④ UNESCO and History — When the Act of Making Becomes Heritage

Korea's jang-making goes beyond a mere recipe — it has been recognized as a culture of community.

  • On December 3, 2024, at the 19th session of the Intergovernmental Committee for the Safeguarding of Intangible Cultural Heritage in Asunción, Paraguay, "Knowledge, beliefs and practices related to jang-making in the Republic of Korea" was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
  • This is the second Korean food tradition to be listed, following kimjang culture (the making of kimchi) in 2013. That the two traditions were recognized separately, on their own, shows how broad and deep Korean fermentation culture runs.
  • What UNESCO singled out wasn't a "finished flavor" but the act of making and sharing it together. The assessment credits the process itself — families gathering to steam and shape meju, split the jang, and pass the skill down from mother to daughter or daughter-in-law — with nurturing peace and bonds within the community.

Note: soybean fermentation itself is a tradition shared across East Asia, but the method of culturing whole meju and splitting doenjang and ganjang from a single jar, plus gochujang — a fermented red pepper paste — are considered uniquely Korean.

⑤ "The Taste of Jang Is Family Identity" — Ssi-ganjang, a Living Family Tree

The most striking concept in Korean fermentation culture is ssi-ganjang (씨간장), literally "seed soy sauce."

  • Ssi-ganjang is the finest batch of well-aged ganjang, set aside like a seed and handed down through generations. Every year, when a new batch of ganjang is made, a spoonful of the old ssi-ganjang is stirred in (a step called deot-jang), carrying its beneficial microbes and flavor into the new batch — a way of passing the taste on, the way you'd keep an ember from dying out.
  • That's why the ssi-ganjang of well-known families can run anywhere from a century to several hundred years (reported cases include a head-family sauce aged 350 to 370 years — exact years are family claims, worth verifying). The microbial makeup differs from household to household, and so does the taste — making ssi-ganjang, in effect, a family tree written in flavor.
  • Ganjang is even named for how long it has aged — freshly made haet-ganjang, several years old jung-ganjang, and jin-ganjang, aged five years or more until it turns dark and its umami outweighs its saltiness. Time itself becomes the grade.

The philosophy behind this concept is clear. The taste of jang isn't one person's skill — it's a microbial ecosystem, and an identity, that a family has cultivated across generations.

⑥ Jang Today — Store-Bought vs. Traditional, and How to Enjoy It

Most Koreans today buy factory-made jang at the supermarket. It's a landscape where handmade tradition and industrial production coexist.

  • Store-bought jang: Standardized, cheap, and convenient, it accounts for most everyday cooking. Because fermentation periods are shorter or brewing methods differ, the depth of flavor can fall short of traditional jang.
  • Traditional (traditional) soybean paste: Soybean paste made from meju and aged in a pottery for a long time. It is passed down to masters, head families, temples, and farms and receives premium treatment. For foreigners, tasting this ‘traditional sauce’ is a real fermentation experience.
  • How ​​to enjoy: Soybean paste stew, strong soybean paste (soybean paste), bibimbap, tteokbokki (red pepper paste), and seasoned and stewed foods (soy sauce) are found in almost all Korean dishes. At a restaurant, you can hear the owner's proud explanation by simply asking "Is this sauce you made yourself?".

⑦ Foreigner’s Perspective — Vegetarian/Healthy Food

Jang fits surprisingly well at a foreigner's table. In particular, it has great strengths for vegetarian and vegan travelers.

  • Natural umami (without MSG): Fermentation converts soy protein into amino acids and naturally creates umami. This is the secret to a deep flavor without artificial seasoning, and it is attractive to foreigners looking for a clean flavor.
  • Basically plant-based: Traditional soybean paste and soy sauce are made with only soybeans, water, and salt and are originally vegan** (gochujang is also mostly plant-based). It is a key weapon in vegetarian dishes that want to create a deep flavor without animal ingredients.
  • The foundation of temple food: Korean temples need to have a deep flavor without using garlic or green onions, so the fermented flavor of the intestines is crucial**. This is why soybean paste, soy sauce, and red pepper paste are made directly at the temple (→ #800 Temple Food).
  • Intestinal Health: As a fermented food, it contains beneficial bacteria, so it also appeals to those interested in healthy eating and intestinal health (specific figures for efficacy without exaggeration, avoiding medical conclusions).

Recommended first step for foreigners: Soybean paste stew (the mildest and most common) → Gochujang for bibimbap (a spicy and sweet introduction) → Once you get used to the scent, Cheonggukjang. If you are a vegetarian traveler, a plate of temple food will best show you the true value of the temple.

❓ Foreigners Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • How is soybean paste different from Japanese miso? — Miso is made from rice malt (koji) to make it fine and sweet, while soybean paste is made from whole meju (meju) soaked in salt water to make it coarser and richer. Above all, the soybean paste is unique in that it is made separated from the soy sauce in the same jar.
  • Isn’t red pepper paste just a spicy sauce? — No. Because it is a fermented complex seasoning, the spiciness is followed by sweetness and umami. There is no fermented red pepper paste like it anywhere in the world.
  • Why does Cheonggukjang have a strong smell? — Because the soybeans are fermented for a short period of time at high temperature for 2-3 days without meju. Natto's cousin has a strong flavor, but is loved as a healthy food. If this is your first attempt, I recommend starting with soybean paste.
  • I am vegetarian, but can I eat Korean fermented sauce? — Traditional soybean paste and soy sauce are usually vegan as they only contain soybeans, water, and salt. However, anchovies and kelp may be added to commercial products, so checking the label is recommended. You can enjoy temple restaurants with confidence.
