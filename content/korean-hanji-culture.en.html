Hanji — Korea's Thousand-Year Paper Behind Window Screens, Calligraphy & Craft

> Daknamu (닥나무, paper mulberry) bark · Oebal-tteugi (외발뜨기, single-sheet papermaking) · Thousand-year preservation · Mugujeonggwang Dasdarani-gyeong (무구정광대다라니경) · Changhoji (창호지, window/door paper)·calligraphy·hanji craft · Restoration paper for the Louvre & Vatican — a hanji (한지, traditional Korean paper) culture explainer for foreigners. Not "Korean paper is pretty," but why it was made to last a thousand years, and where and how it was used.

Look at old Korean objects and you'll notice paper everywhere. The doors and windows of a hanok (한옥, traditional Korean house) were sealed not with glass but with paper; calligraphers laid ink on paper; and everyday items like lanterns, chests, and brush holders were layered together from paper. That paper is hanji (한지) — paper made by scooping up daknamu (닥나무, paper mulberry) bark fiber in the traditional Korean way.

Western paper yellows and crumbles within 200–300 years, but hanji lasts a thousand years. In fact, hanji printed in the 8th century survives intact to this day. This article isn't "which hanji workshop is pretty" (→ a place guide), but rather unpacks what hanji is, how it's made, why it lasts so long, and where Koreans used it. This is installment #70 of the Korean Culture explainer series.

⚠️ 3 Things Foreigners Often Get Wrong

1. "Isn't hanji just handmade paper?" ❌ — Hand-scooped paper exists everywhere in the world, but in hanji the raw material (daknamu / 닥나무), the method (oebal-tteugi / 외발뜨기), and the chemistry (neutral) all interlock to achieve thousand-year preservation. It's not "pretty handmade paper" but paper engineered to preserve records permanently.

2. "Aren't Korean, Chinese, and Japanese papers all pretty much the same?" ❌ — All three are mulberry-family papers, but their methods and uses differ. Hanji in particular evolved into a tough, double-sided paper by crossing the fibers horizontally and vertically through oebal-tteugi (외발뜨기). In Korea it's also called dakjongi (닥종이, mulberry paper).

3. "A door made of paper must have been weak and cold." ❌ — A hanok's changhoji (창호지) is no ordinary paper. It lets light in softly and blocks wind while letting air and humidity pass through, regulating the indoor environment. That's why it was called a "breathing window" — not because it was weak, but because it was designed to work that way (→ Hanok #1197).

① What Is Hanji — the Weight of the Name "Dakjongi"

Hanji (한지) literally means 'Korean paper.' The key is the raw material. Hanji is made from the long fibers drawn out of the inner bark of the daknamu (닥나무, 楮, paper mulberry). That's why in Korea hanji is also called dakjongi (닥종이, mulberry paper).

Why does this matter? Today's common paper is made from pulp — wood ground fine — and acidic substances introduced during manufacturing cause it over time to yellow and crumble (the reason old Western books decay). Daknamu (닥나무) inner-bark fiber, by contrast, is long and tough, and the traditional hanji method uses no acid, so the paper stays neutral to slightly alkaline. That's why hanji barely decays even after centuries.

To old Koreans, paper was no mere disposable. It was a core material for keeping records, building houses (window screens and floor paper), and making everyday goods. Making good paper was therefore a state-managed craft, and the artisan who scooped the paper was called a hanjijang (한지장, hanji master artisan).

② How It's Made — Oebal-tteugi (외발뜨기), Where a Hundred Hands Touch the Paper

Half the secret of hanji's thousand-year life lies in how it's made. Traditional hanji is done almost entirely by hand, often described as "a hundred hands touch a single sheet." The broad flow goes like this.

  • ① Harvest (winter): One-year daknamu (닥나무) is cut in late autumn to winter (roughly Nov–Feb). Fiber from the cold season is considered superior.
  • ② Steaming and stripping: The daknamu is steamed, the outer bark peeled away, leaving only the white inner bark (baekdak / 백닥).
  • ③ Boiling in lye: The inner bark is boiled for hours in jaetmul (잿물, natural alkaline lye) to remove impurities and soften the fibers. This step is the key to making the paper neutral.
  • ④ Rinsing, sorting, beating: The boiled bark is rinsed in clear water, every speck picked out by hand, then beaten with a mallet to loosen the fibers finely.
  • ⑤ Mixing in dakpul: The sticky mucilage drawn from the root of hwangchokgyu (황촉규, sunset hibiscus / dakpul / 닥풀) is mixed into the water. It's a natural dispersant that keeps the fibers floating evenly in the water.
  • ⑥ Oebal-tteugi (외발뜨기): The artisan dips a bal (발, bamboo screen frame) into the water and rocks it rhythmically front-to-back and side-to-side to gather the fibers. This rocking makes the long fibers interlock crosswise, producing hanji's signature tough, double-usable paper. Scooping with a single handle is called oebal-tteugi (외발뜨기), and it's held to yield better-quality paper than the easier ssangbal-tteugi (쌍발뜨기, two-handle scooping).
  • ⑦ Drying and finishing: Each sheet is stacked to drain water, dried against a hot plate, then smoothed.

> Key: Hanji is expensive not because it's labor-intensive, but because that very process — crossing fibers through oebal-tteugi (외발뜨기) and achieving neutrality through lye — creates thousand-year preservation. Hanji's durability isn't "a well-made result" but a designed property.

③ The Paper That Lasts a Thousand Years — the Proof of the Mugujeonggwang Dasdarani-gyeong

"Hanji lasts a thousand years" is no exaggeration — it's proven by a physical object. That decisive proof is the Mugujeonggwang Dasdarani-gyeong (무구정광대다라니경, 無垢淨光大陀羅尼經).

This printed Buddhist scripture, found in the Seokgatap (석가탑, Seokga Pagoda) at Bulguksa (불국사, Bulguk Temple) in Gyeongju (경주), dates to the 8th century, the Unified Silla (통일신라) period (estimated made before 751) and is regarded as both the world's oldest surviving woodblock print and a print on some of the oldest surviving paper as well. In other words, hanji made about 1,200 years ago survives to this day with its form intact.

This is possible for the two reasons seen above — the toughness of daknamu's (닥나무) long fibers and the chemical stability of being neutral to slightly alkaline. Acidic paper decays by breaking itself down, but in hanji that breakdown reaction barely occurs. That's why Korea's countless old documents, old books, and Buddhist scriptures are unearthed in good condition.

> A foreigner's view: Compared with European parchment or acidic pulp paper crumbling within a few centuries, hanji's preservation is remarkable even in materials-science terms. This is what leads to its adoption as restoration paper for world cultural heritage, described later.

④ Where It Was Used — Changhoji (창호지), Calligraphy, Floor Paper: the Daily Life Hanji Held Up

Before it was a museum relic, hanji was life itself. Koreans used hanji in three broad ways.

  • Changhoji (창호지) — doors and windows made of paper: A hanok's (한옥) doors and windows are made by pasting hanji onto a wooden lattice (changhoji). Unlike glass, it filters light softly, blocks wind yet lets air and humidity pass, keeping the interior comfortable. Winter sunlight passing through changhoji and filling a room with a warm, milky glow is part of the charm of a hanok (→ Hanok #1197).
  • Seohwaji (서화지) — the ground for calligraphy and painting: Hanji holds ink well and bleeds beautifully. Old calligraphers and painters favored it, and the writing and painting endured for centuries on hanji. (Today Chinese-style hwaseonji (화선지, xuan paper) is also widely used for calligraphy, but hanji is still prized as a tough, well-preserving ground.)
  • Jangpanji (장판지)·dobaeji (도배지) — the paper that wraps a house: Layered several times on the floor and soaked in soybean oil to make jangpan (장판, oiled floor paper), or pasted on walls as wallpaper. The warm floor of an ondol (온돌, traditional underfloor heating) and a hanji jangpan went together as a set.

Beyond these, hanji was used for everyday goods like fans, kites (yeon / 연), lanterns (deung / 등), and umbrellas — even as armor reinforcement or a cloth substitute. It's said that "Koreans wore their houses in paper and lived in it."

⑤ Hanji Craft — Layering Paper into Bowls, Chests, and Lanterns

Hanji's other face is craft. Hanji craft — making three-dimensional objects by layering, twisting, or cutting hanji — is a distinctly Korean craft genre with several branches by technique.

  • Jiseung craft (지승공예, 紙繩工藝): Twisting hanji thin like cord and weaving it into baskets, bowls, and cushions. It has the texture of a fabric woven from paper.
  • Jiho craft (지호공예, 紙糊工藝): Dissolving scrap hanji into a paste in water, then mixing it with glue and shaping it like clay into bowls and chests. A kind of 'paper pottery.'
  • Jihwa craft (지화공예, 紙花工藝): Dyeing hanji to make flowers for rituals and decoration.
  • Jeonji craft (전지공예, 剪紙工藝): Pasting hanji in several layers onto a wood or paper frame to make chests, sewing boxes, and brush holders, then decorating by cutting and applying patterns in colored paper.

The chests, brush holders, lanterns, and sewing boxes made this way are firm yet light, with lovely colors and patterns from the colored paper, beloved as practical goods and craft pieces alike. Adding ot-chil (옻칠, lacquer) to hanji raises its water resistance and durability for longer use. Today hanji craft is expanding into modern design products as well — lighting (lanterns), interior accents, accessories, and eco-bags.

⑥ Jeonju Hanji and the Globalization of Hanji — the Paper the Louvre and Vatican Chose

The city that comes to mind for hanji is Jeonju (전주, 全州). Daknamu (닥나무) grows well there and the water is good, making it a paper town from old; in the Goryeo (고려) and Joseon (조선) dynasties, Jeonju hanji (전주한지) was a royal tribute item, ranked highest in both output and quality. Even today Jeonju is the hub of the hanji industry and the Hanji Culture Festival.

Entering the 20th century, the hanji industry shrank sharply for a time, pushed out by cheap machine-made paper and imported hwaseonji (화선지). Yet hanji's thousand-year preservation drew attention again on an unexpected stage — the restoration of world cultural heritage.

  • Around 2016, after hanji passed international certification tests (by document- and book-restoration institutions in Italy, among others), the Vatican Museums were reported to have used hanji to restore important relics such as St. Francis's handwritten prayer 'Chartula' (estimated 1224).
  • France's Louvre Museum was also reported to have adopted hanji to restore furniture in its collection (such as a 19th-century desk of Maximilian II).

Since restoration paper must not decay along with the object when it ages, hanji's neutral, durable nature, stable even after centuries, was highly valued as a restoration material. Korea went further, nominating the traditional knowledge and techniques of hanji-making to the UNESCO Representative List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2024, and the decision is to be made at a later meeting.

> ※ Restoration adoption cases, dates, and the UNESCO inscription's progress may change over time and are best verified. This text is based on public reporting and institutional sources.

⑦ A Foreigner's Hanji Experience — See It, Make It, Take It Home

Hanji isn't a culture trapped behind museum glass — it's something you can experience directly. There are three broad ways a foreign traveler can enjoy hanji.

  • See: Looking directly at changhoji (창호지) doors and windows in a hanok (palaces, hanok villages) lets you feel how the paper filters light. Some hanji- and paper-related exhibitions and workshops also let you watch oebal-tteugi (외발뜨기) demonstrations.
  • Make: Hanji workshops and experience centers run paper-scooping or hands-on programs making lanterns, fans, and small chests from hanji. Rock the bal (발) yourself to scoop a sheet and you'll understand "why a hundred hands touch it."
  • Take home: Hanji notebooks, postcards, fans, lanterns, bokjumeoni (복주머니, lucky pouches), and hanji dolls are light and distinctly Korean, popular as souvenirs and gifts. The texture of daknamu (닥나무) fiber and its subtle color are clearly different from mass-produced paper (→ Gift Culture #1193).

> Tip: Even among "hanji products," handmade hanji and machine-imitated mass versions differ in texture and durability. It's good to touch it slowly with your hands before choosing.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • Is hanji the same as Japanese washi or Chinese paper? — All three are mulberry-family traditional papers with similar roots, but their methods and uses differ. Hanji crosses fibers through oebal-tteugi (외발뜨기) to be tough and double-sided, and evolved toward very high preservation.
  • Does it really last a thousand years? — With the 8th-century Unified Silla (통일신라) Mugujeonggwang Dasdarani-gyeong (무구정광대다라니경) surviving as a physical object, centuries-to-millennium preservation has been demonstrated thanks to its neutral, slightly alkaline nature. But that's the story when it's well stored.
  • Why is it so expensive? — From harvesting daknamu (닥나무) to oebal-tteugi (외발뜨기), nearly every step is done by hand, so it's labor-intensive. Think of it not as "expensive paper" but as paper closer to a craft piece.
  • Which hanji souvenir is good to buy? — Light, low-bulk items like notebooks, postcards, fans, bokjumeoni (복주머니), and hanji lanterns (등) are safe choices. The more handmade the hanji, the more alive the texture.
  • Can I try making hanji? — Some workshops and experience centers in hanji-producing areas like Jeonju (전주) and in cities run paper-scooping and hanji craft experiences. Checking reservations and operating hours before visiting is recommended.
