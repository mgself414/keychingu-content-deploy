Same Korean, different texture — stories of Busan, Jeolla, and Jeju dialects

Dialect, Dialect, Gyeongsang-do, Jeolla-do, Chungcheong-do, Jeju-do — Korean regional language explainer for foreigners. Not ‘I don’t understand Korean,’ but ‘Why, how, and why is it attractive?’

Foreigners who learned “Hello” from Korean textbooks are embarrassed when they go to Busan. It's clearly the same Korean, but the accent is fast and strong, so it sounds like they're fighting. In Jeolla-do, "~ing" and "~dangke" are added to the end of sentences, and in Chungcheong-do, "~yu" is added at the end of sentences. When you go to Jeju Island, you will meet a greeting that you can't even guess in standard language, such as "Honjeobseoye". This is dialect. Although Korea is a small country, the texture of the language in each region is clearly different due to the terrain being divided by mountains and the sea. Foreigners usually only learn standard language (Seoul dialect), but an increasing number of people are fascinated by characters with dialect in K-dramas and movies and are wondering "What kind of accent is this?". This article is not about “Where do I go to hear dialect?” (→ cross-link), but explains what dialect is, how it is different, and why Koreans love it from a foreigner’s perspective. ⚠️ 3 things foreigners often misunderstand

1. "Dialect = wrong Korean, unlearned language" ❌ — Dialect is not a wrong word, but official Korean that has been refined over a long period of time in that region. The standard language is merely a promise set as the standard for "modern Seoul language widely used by educated people" for administration and broadcasting (National Institute of the Korean Language), and is not a language that is 'superior' to dialect. Each dialect is close to a cultural heritage that contains the history and emotions of the region.

2. "People in Gyeongsang-do are angry" ❌ — Gyeongsang-do (Busan) The reason why people speak fast and loudly is because of the tone (pitch), not that they are angry. Many foreigners say that the Busan dialect in the drama sounds "like shouting," but in reality, it's normal everyday conversation. The only difference is the texture of the intonation.

3. "Jeju language is just one of the dialects" ❌ — Jeju language is so different that even mainland Korean speakers cannot understand it, so UNESCO listed it as critically endangered in 2010. It is not “one dialect,” but is actually close to another language in Korea (④ below).

① Standard language vs. dialect — What became the ‘standard’?

In Korean, there are standard language and dialect.

  • Standard Language is the official definition set by the National Institute of the Korean Language and is "modern Seoul language widely used by educated people". Broadcasting, news, textbooks, and Korean language teaching materials are all used in this standard language. This is the Korean language that foreigners learn.
  • Dialect is the language actually used in each region. The further away from Seoul and the more it is cut off by the mountains and the sea, the greater the difference with the standard language becomes.

The important point here — Standard language is not “more correct” than dialect. Standard language is just a promise to pick one as the standard for communication across the country. People in Seoul and Gyeonggi technically also speak the ‘Central dialect’. The dialect is not “old-fashioned language,” but a living language that retains traces of the old Korean language and regional sentiments that the standard language has lost.

② Gyeongsang-do (Southeast) dialect — fast and strong, so attractive

Southeastern dialect used in Busan, Daegu, Ulsan, and Gyeongsangnam-do. It is the dialect that foreigners feel first and most strongly.

  • Texture: This is among the dialects with the biggest difference in intonation in Korean. It has tone — seongjo (성조) — where pitch changes the meaning of a word, much like tone works in Chinese. That's why it sounds fast and forceful, giving an impression of being "blunt" or "like they're fighting" — but Gyeongsang people take pride in the fact that there's deep warmth underneath it.
  • A famous example: "Gaga gagaga?" (가가 가가가?) — rendered in standard Korean, this means "Is that kid the one whose surname is Ga?" It's often cited as the classic example of a tone-based dialect, where the same sound 'ga' becomes a whole sentence just through pitch (a fun example, not to be taken too literally).
  • A vocabulary example: Chives are called jeongguji (정구지). An expression like "meorakano" (머라카노) (what did you say?) is also known as a symbol of Busan speech.

Tip for foreigners: Don't be scared even if Busan speech sounds intimidating. The texture is just strong — people are friendly in most cases.

③ Jeolla-do (Southwest) and Chungcheong-do — Textures of Warmth and Ease

Even though they're both in the south, Jeolla-do and Chungcheong-do have a texture all their own, different from Gyeongsang-do.

Jeolla-do (Southwestern dialect) — warm and affectionate

  • The intonation differs noticeably from standard Korean, and sentence endings are distinctive. Endings like "~ing" (geureochi-ing), "~dangkke" (~ranikka), and "~beo/haebeo" (~haebeorida) create that warm, homey feel.
  • "Geosigi" (거시기) is the symbol of Jeolla speech. It's an all-purpose word slotted into a noun or verb position whenever the exact word won't come to mind — there's even a joke that "you can make Jeolla speech just by adding geosigi." Interjections like "atta" and "ome" also carry a strong Jeolla flavor.

Chungcheong-do — laid-back and unhurried

  • The biggest feature is dragging out the end of a sentence. A classic example is using "~yu" where standard Korean would use "~yo" (e.g., "geuraeyu~"). It sounds unhurried less because people actually talk slowly, and more because the sentence ending gets pulled out and stretched.
  • The roundabout, unhurried way of speaking gives Chungcheong-do an image of being "gentle" and "easygoing."

④ Jeju Language — Another Language Within Korea (Endangered)

The speech of Jeju Island, Jeju language (the Jeju dialect), is on a completely different level from other dialects.

  • Even mainlanders can't understand it. Because Jeju Island sits cut off by the sea, its language changed more slowly, leaving behind many traces of Middle Korean. It's different enough from standard Korean that mutual communication with standard speakers is almost impossible.
  • A UNESCO endangered language. In December 2010, UNESCO listed Jeju language on its Atlas of the World's Languages in Danger as 'critically endangered' — the highest risk category. Fewer than 10,000 people speak it fluently, and most of them are estimated to be elderly, 70 and older. That's why academics and Jeju Island keep up preservation efforts such as compiling dictionaries and education programs.
  • A taste of Jeju language: The most famous greeting is honjeoopseoyo (혼저옵서예). It does not mean 'come alone' — it means "welcome, come on in" ('honjeo' means 'quickly'). Chives are called seuri (세우리) on Jeju — the same vegetable going by jeongguji in Gyeongsang, sol in Jeolla, and seuri in Jeju depending on the region is exactly the charm of dialects.

Jeju language is more than an 'interesting dialect' — it's a piece of humanity's linguistic heritage that can never be recovered if it disappears. Next time you see a "Honjeoopseoyo" sign on a trip to Jeju, remember the weight behind it.

⑤ Dialect in K-Content — How Foreigners Fall for Dialect

These days, the way foreigners first encounter dialect isn't a textbook — it's dramas, movies, and variety shows.

  • Reply 1997 (2012) is set in Busan in 1997, so Gyeongsang-do dialect runs through it from start to finish. Because the lead actors are actually from Gyeongsang-do, the show has the real vitality of genuine Busan speech. By contrast, Reply 1988 is set in Ssangmun-dong, Seoul, so it has a completely different texture — comparing regional color across the same series is part of the fun.
  • In Korean crime and noir films set in Busan, Gyeongsang-do dialect also builds intense characters. A character who speaks in dialect is often loved as the most human and memorable one.
  • For foreign fans who've only learned standard Korean, dialect is a refreshing shock — "Wait, this is the same Korean language and it sounds this different?" — and becomes the next level of deokjil (덕질) — hardcore fandom — pulling them one layer deeper.

If standard Korean is the 'textbook version' of the language, dialect is Koreans' 'real, everyday version.' Part of why K-content is loved so much comes from this vivid texture.

⑥ What Dialect Carries — Speech Is Regional Identity

Dialect isn't just a difference in pronunciation — it's the identity and pride of the people from that region.

  • Koreans can guess someone's hometown just from the way they talk, and quickly feel close to someone who shares their dialect. Hearing your hometown dialect far from home is a small joy — dialect is 'a mark of belonging.'
  • Dialects carry an image of that region's temperament, too: Gyeongsang-do as blunt but loyal — uiri (의리) — Jeolla-do as warm and full of affection, Chungcheong-do as laid-back and easygoing... Of course, these are just images that may not match the actual person, so generalizing should be done carefully.
  • In the past, a standard-language-centered society sometimes tried to 'correct' dialects, but today the view that dialect is a cultural asset worth protecting is far stronger. The Jeju-language preservation movement is a prime example.

⑦ When Foreigners Hear Dialect — How to Enjoy It

For foreigners learning standard Korean, dialect isn't a burden — it's bonus content.

  • You don't need to understand all of it. Even Koreans don't fully understand an unfamiliar regional dialect the first time they hear it. Knowing the key words plus standard Korean is enough for travel.
  • Try one phrase and you'll be welcomed. Say "meorakano~" in Busan or "honjeoopseoyo" in Jeju, and locals will smile and warm up right away. Even a clumsy attempt earns goodwill — just make sure it comes from respect and curiosity, not mockery.
  • Don't let the intonation scare you. Even when Gyeongsang-do speech sounds forceful, people are friendly in most cases, and Jeolla-do and Chungcheong-do speech sounds warm right from the start.
  • If you know the dialect, you can see hidden details in K-dramas and movies. You begin to understand why the character uses that way of speaking and where he or she is from.

❓ Foreigners Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • Do I have to learn dialect separately? — No. For travel and life, standard language is sufficient. Knowing the dialect is just a 'plus' that enhances fun and understanding, and is not essential.
  • I think people in Busan are angry. Should I be scared? — Not at all. The Gyeongsang-do language only sounds strong because of the tone (pitch), but it is not angry. In most cases, they are friendly.
  • What if I can’t communicate on Jeju Island? — Don’t worry. Jeju people also use Standard language.** Pure Jeju language is mainly heard in the elderly and in traditional expressions, and Standard language is sufficient for everyday communication.
  • Is it rude to imitate dialect? — Trying out a word or two out of respect and curiosity is actually welcomed. However, it is rude to ridicule or imitate a specific area. It is good to have the attitude of ‘I want to learn’.
  • Is dialect more ‘real Korean’ than standard language? — Both are real. The standard language is ‘common standard’, and the dialect is ‘regional everyday language’. It is not a matter of superiority or inferiority, but Korean languages ​​with different roles.
