Korea Travel Health Guide — Where to Go When It's Not an Emergency: Out of Prescription Meds, Toothache, Lost Contacts, Morning-After Pill, Struggling Mentally — Numbers by Counter and Links to Detailed Guides

Subtitle: Prescription meds are re-prescribed at a clinic (search the active ingredient on the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (식약처) Drug Safety Nara (의약품안전나라)) · Dentists and pharmacies open at night and on holidays: E-Gen, the emergency medical portal (응급의료포털) run by the National Emergency Medical Center (중앙응급의료센터) · Glasses and contact lenses are sold only at optical shops, and online sales are banned by law (Medical Technicians Act, Article 12) · The morning-after pill is a prescription-only drug, so you need a prescription (Ministry of Food and Drug Safety) · If you are struggling mentally, call 109 (24 hours) or the mental health crisis line 1577-0199; if you don't speak Korean, call 1330 (8 languages; Korean, English, Japanese, Chinese 07:00~24:00; Russian, Vietnamese, Thai, Malay/Indonesian 08:00~19:00) · For suspected infectious disease, call 1339 (counseling 24 hours; foreign-language interpretation by three-way call with 1330 or 1345 within their operating hours) · 1345 Immigration Contact Center, 20 languages, weekdays 09:00~18:00 (Korean, English, Chinese until 22:00, fee charged) · Checked 2026-11-04

🎯 How to Use This Guide

You take your last blood pressure pill at your lodging and only then realize you have five days of travel left. It's not a 119 situation, and a pharmacy won't hand over prescription drugs. This guide is a hub that sorts these non-emergency health problems by situation and tells you only "which counter to go to and which number to call." How to buy medicine at a pharmacy, the clinic steps from check-in to payment, finding an emergency room, and insurance claim documents each have a dedicated guide, so pick your counter here and move on to that guide.

The numbers and rules written directly in this guide are only the values seen on the official pages of the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (식약처), statutes, the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (질병관리청), the Ministry of Justice (법무부), the National Emergency Medical Center (중앙응급의료센터), and the Seoul city government on 2026-11-04. Medical fees and drug prices differ by hospital and pharmacy, so no figures are given here; see the detailed guides. The food allergen labeling list is based on the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety press release of 2021-07-14.

💊 Out of Prescription Meds — Get a New Prescription at a Clinic

  • A pharmacy can't sell prescription-only drugs on the spot, such as blood pressure pills, diabetes pills, and antibiotics. See a doctor at a clinic (의원) near your lodging (internal medicine, etc.), get a prescription, and take it to any pharmacy. The steps from check-in to payment and the validity period of a "prescription" (처방전) are in #1307 ③·④.
  • Bring the active ingredient name, not the brand name. The same ingredient has different brand names in different countries. The drug search on the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety's Drug Safety Nara (의약품안전나라) (nedrug.mfds.go.kr) has an English ingredient name field; enter the ingredient of your drug (e.g., amlodipine) and Korean products with the same ingredient appear (Ministry of Food and Drug Safety). The screen is in Korean and combination drugs show up too, so pick a product with only one ingredient.
  • A traveler without national health insurance pays the full amount, and it varies by hospital; a rough range is in #1307 ⑥. Tests raise the cost. If you have travel insurance, keep the receipt, the itemized bill, and the prescription; the 5 documents are listed in #251.
  • The easiest approach is to get extra medicine for the remaining days before you leave. For prescription drugs, bring an English prescription or a doctor's letter; for narcotic or psychotropic ingredients such as sleeping pills and ADHD meds, get prior approval from the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety — #1352.

🦷 Toothache — Call First to Confirm Same-Day Treatment, and Search E-Gen at Night or on Holidays

  • If the pain is urgent, search "치과" (dentist) in a map app and call first to ask whether they can treat you today (some places are by appointment only). Show your passport at check-in; the process is the same as at a clinic (#1307).
  • For nights and public holidays, choose dental clinics (치과의원) in the hospital search of the National Emergency Medical Center's E-Gen emergency medical portal (응급의료포털 E-Gen) (e-gen.or.kr). It shows "현재 진료가능" (currently open) and "공휴일운영" (open on holidays) labels, along with a notice that hours can change, so call before going (National Emergency Medical Center). The site is in Korean, so enter the area and "치과" in Hangul. The site's pharmacy search shows open pharmacies on a map (hours are provided by the Korean Pharmaceutical Association and can change, so confirm by phone), and you can also download a list of pharmacies open after 22:00.
  • Costs vary by treatment and clinic, so ask "비용이 얼마인가요?" (How much does it cost?) at check-in. Without national health insurance, you pay in full. Planned treatments such as implants or braces are a different matter from pain treatment while traveling, and quotes differ by dental clinic.
  • To get through one night with painkillers, use convenience-store safety OTC drugs (4 categories, 13 items: fever/pain relievers, cold medicine, digestive aids, pain patches; Ministry of Food and Drug Safety) or a pharmacy — #433 💊.

👁 Contacts and Glasses — Sold Only at Optical Shops, and Online Orders Are Banned by Law

  • In Korea, glasses and contact lenses can be sold only at optical shops (안경업소) with a licensed optician (안경사), and e-commerce, mail-order sales, and purchasing or delivery agency services are prohibited (Medical Technicians Act, Article 12, version in force 2026-09-11). If you lost a lens, don't plan on ordering through an app; go to an optical shop (안경원).
  • The statute has no requirement for a prescription. You can have your eyes tested and fitted at the optical shop, and when selling contact lenses, the optician must explain how to use them, the expiration date, and side effects (same Act, Article 12). Take a photo of your current lens power and brand to speed things up.
  • For dry eyes, buy artificial tears at a pharmacy. The 13 convenience-store safety OTC items include no eye drops (Ministry of Food and Drug Safety item list).

🌸 Women's Health — Period Products Are Everywhere; the Morning-After Pill Needs a Prescription

  • Sanitary pads are sold at convenience stores, drugstores, and supermarkets everywhere. Tampons and menstrual cups may have little variety at convenience stores, so look at drugstores like Olive Young (올리브영). Buy period pain medicine (painkillers) at a pharmacy or from convenience-store OTC drugs. Write "생리통약 주세요" (Period pain medicine, please) and show it to the pharmacist (for the symptom card method, see #1278 ②).
  • The morning-after pill (emergency contraceptive) is a prescription-only drug — on the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety's Drug Safety Nara (의약품안전나라), single-ingredient levonorgestrel emergency contraceptives (Norevo One Tab (노레보원정), Nogess One Tab (노제스원정), etc.) are registered as prescription-only drugs (Ministry of Food and Drug Safety). You can't buy it directly at a pharmacy; you need a prescription from a clinic (OB-GYN, internal medicine, etc.). For check-in and prescription steps, see #1307; at night, use E-Gen to find a clinic that is open.
  • Some oral contraceptives you already take are sold at pharmacies as over-the-counter drugs (per Drug Safety Nara registration, Ministry of Food and Drug Safety). Show the pharmacist the box or the ingredient name, and if it is a prescription-only drug, get a prescription at a clinic (same method as 💊).

💬 When You're Struggling Mentally — 109 and 1577-0199; If You Don't Speak Korean, Start with 1330

  • 109 (Ministry of Health and Welfare suicide prevention hotline) and 1577-0199 (mental health crisis hotline) are open 24 hours (Korea Foundation for Suicide Prevention). The foundation's website guidance is in Korean, with no foreign-language counseling information.
  • If you don't speak Korean, call 1330 (tourist interpretation hotline, 8 languages by phone; Korean, English, Japanese, Chinese 07:00~24:00 (Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency table); Russian, Vietnamese, Thai, Malay/Indonesian 08:00~19:00 (Korea Tourism Organization)) to get interpretation — how to call is in #1299. 1330 is not guaranteed to connect you to 109, so tell 1330 only your situation and ask for interpretation, and call 109 or 1577-0199 yourself.
  • If it is the middle of the night, or you are in danger right now, such as a panic attack with shortness of breath and shaking or an urge to self-harm, call 119 (#433). Sleeping pills and tranquilizers are prescription drugs, so get them at a clinic (including psychiatry) using the method in 💊.

💧 Water, Air, and Food Allergies

  • Tap water: Seoul presents its tap water, Seoul Arisu (아리수), as drinking water. The official "how to drink" guidance says to run the water for 2~3 minutes the first time you turn it on each day, and that the chlorine smell disappears after the water sits for 20~30 minutes (Seoul Arisu Headquarters (서울아리수본부)). Most restaurants give water for free. Bottled water is easy to buy at convenience stores.
  • Fine dust and yellow dust: Check grades on the Ministry of Environment's AirKorea (에어코리아) (airkorea.or.kr) app or site. For spring yellow dust and choosing KF masks, see #1194 Four Seasons Culture and #1318 Surviving the Cold Snap.
  • Food allergies: Allergen labeling is mandatory on packaged food — eggs (알류), milk (우유), buckwheat (메밀), peanuts (땅콩), soybeans (대두), wheat (밀), mackerel (고등어), crab (게), shrimp (새우), pork (돼지고기), peaches (복숭아), tomatoes (토마토), sulfites (아황산류), walnuts (호두), chicken (닭고기), beef (쇠고기), squid (오징어), shellfish (조개류) (oysters, abalone, mussels), and pine nuts (잣) (Ministry of Food and Drug Safety press release 2021-07-14; eggs means poultry eggs, and sulfites apply at 10mg/kg or more). The list may grow with revisions. Restaurant menus often have no labeling, so write "저는 ○○ 알레르기가 있어요" (I'm allergic to ○○) in Hangul and show it — peanuts (땅콩), shrimp (새우), crab (게), eggs (계란 / 달걀), milk (우유).

🤒 When You Suspect an Infectious Disease — 1339, with a Three-Way Call for Foreigners

  • If you have a severe fever, cough, or diarrhea and are worried about an infectious disease, ask the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency's 1339 (365 days, 24 hours, free). Foreigners are counseled by three-way call with 1330 (Korea Tourism Organization) or 1345 (Ministry of Justice), so foreign-language interpretation is added only within the operating hours of 1330 (hours differ by language) or 1345 (weekdays 09:00~18:00, Korean, English, Chinese until 22:00) (Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency, Ministry of Justice). 1339 is a counseling line, not an ambulance — if you collapse or are short of breath, call 119.
  • If your child has a fever at night, find a Moonlight Children's Hospital (달빛어린이병원), which offers pediatric night care, through the Moonlight Children's Hospital menu on E-Gen, and call before going because closing times differ by hospital (#433 🏥).

⚠ 4 Common Mistakes

  1. "A pharmacy will give me prescription meds if I beg" — They won't. The order is clinic visit → prescription → pharmacy (💊).
  2. "I'll just order lenses through an app" — Online sales of glasses and lenses are banned by law in Korea. Go to an optical shop (👁).
  3. "I can buy the morning-after pill at a pharmacy" — It is a prescription-only drug, so you need a prescription. Time matters, so find the nearest clinic first (🌸).
  4. "If I'm sick, call 1339" — 1339 is for infectious disease counseling. For emergencies, call 119; if you can't communicate, call 1330 (🤒 · #433).

⚙ FAQ 4

Q1. How do I find a clinic where English is spoken?

The sure bet is the international clinic at a large hospital — contacts, languages, and hours for 6 international clinics and 2 emergency rooms are in #405. At a neighborhood clinic, it is faster to write your symptoms in one Korean sentence and show it (#1307 ⑤), and if you need interpretation, call 1330 and use speakerphone for interpretation (#1299).

Q2. How do I explain my medicine's name in Korea?

Enter the ingredient name (in English) from the back of the package into the English ingredient name field in the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety's Drug Safety Nara search to find Korean products, and show that screen (in Korean) together with your original medicine package. Saying only the brand name often isn't understood.

Q3. Are dentists and pharmacies open at 11 p.m.?

On the emergency medical portal E-Gen, look at a map of dental clinics labeled "현재 진료가능" (currently open) or "공휴일운영" (open on holidays) and of open pharmacies, and call to confirm before going (National Emergency Medical Center). For the standard for getting through a night on convenience-store OTC drugs, see #1278 ④.

Q4. Can travelers call 109, 1339, and 1345?

Anyone can call these numbers. 1339 is free, and 1345 (20 languages, weekdays 09:00~18:00, Korean, English, Chinese until 22:00) is listed as a paid call by the Ministry of Justice. The guidance on 109 and 1577-0199 is in Korean, and for foreigners, 1339 arranges a three-way call with 1330 or 1345 (within the operating hours of those numbers). If you don't speak Korean, 1330 (Korean, English, Japanese, Chinese 07:00~24:00; the other 4 languages 08:00~19:00) is the starting point, and for a late-night emergency, call 119.
