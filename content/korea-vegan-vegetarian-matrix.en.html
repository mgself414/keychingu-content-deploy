Why Korean Vegetarian/Vegan Matters

Korea's vegetarian and vegan scene offers foreign visitors more options than they typically expect.

Temple food (사찰 음식), distinctive to Korea's Buddhist culture, builds flavor entirely from seasonal vegetables and fermented foods — no meat, no fish, and none of the "five pungent vegetables" (osinchae: 오신채 — green onion, garlic, chives, wild chives, and asafoetida). This tradition is now being reinterpreted in contemporary vegan restaurants across downtown Seoul. At the same time, Itaewon (이태원), Hongdae (홍대), and Gangnam have grown a cluster of internationally minded vegan specialty spots, many with English menus and HappyCow listings.

For foreign visitors, vegetarian/vegan Korea really comes down to two threads: one runs through temples, tradition, and UNESCO-linked heritage; the other runs through the international-style vegan clusters of Itaewon, Hongdae, and Gangnam. Understanding these two threads solves 80% of vegan travel in Korea.

3 Common Mistakes Foreigners Make

Mistake 1: Giving up because "Korean food is all meat"

Traditional Korean food actually has plenty of vegetarian bases. Bibimbap (계란 제거 요청 가능, egg can be requested off), namul (seasoned vegetable) side dishes, and doenjang-jjigae (soybean paste stew — watch for anchovy broth) are naturally vegetable-centered. Even outside a dedicated temple-food restaurant, asking a regular Korean restaurant for a vegetarian option often works.

Mistake 2: Assuming doenjang-jjigae and kimchi are automatically vegan

Standard doenjang-jjigae uses anchovy or shellfish broth. Standard kimchi contains jeotgal (fermented fish sauce). Vegan kimchi is a separate product made without jeotgal, and is reliably available only at vegetarian specialty restaurants or temple-food restaurants. When ordering, specify "No fish sauce, no anchovy broth."

Mistake 3: Opening HappyCow for the first time only once you're already there

Vegan restaurants in Itaewon and Hongdae often have a break time (2:00-5:00 PM) or run reservation-only. Check operating hours, whether reservations are needed, and whether the place is currently open on HappyCow the day before your visit. Cross-checking with recent Google Maps reviews is a safe way to confirm it's actually still running.

Axis 1: Temple Food Deep Dive

1. Tongdosa, Haeinsa, Songgwangsa Temple Food

Background: These three temples are Korea's "Three Jewel Temples." Each runs its own temple-food restaurant (a monks' dining hall, or an affiliated restaurant) or has specialty restaurants clustered nearby.

  • Tongdosa (통도사, Yangsan, Gyeongnam): Restaurants near Tongdosa serving "mountain-vegetable bibimbap" operate in temple-cuisine style. Tongdosa's own monks' dining hall is for resident monks, but opens to outside visitors during events.
  • Haeinsa (해인사, Hapcheon, Gyeongnam): A restaurant row at the Haeinsa entrance specializes in mountain-vegetable bibimbap and mushroom hot pot, with meat-free menu options. Signature dish, mountain-vegetable bibimbap, runs ₩12,000-15,000.
  • Songgwangsa (송광사, Suncheon, Jeonnam): Connects to a Jogyesan hiking course. The temple's own dining hall serves barugongyang (발우공양, a formal monastic communal meal, fully vegan, one set portion per person) to temple-stay participants.

Practical info:

  • All three temples are 3-4 hours from Seoul by express bus.
  • English: temple websites have English pages, but restaurant English menus are limited. Pointing at a photo menu is the common way to order.
  • Vegan certification: none. That said, the temple-food tradition itself excludes osinchae and meat by definition.
  • Halal: not certified.

2. Jogyesa, Bongeunsa (Seoul) Temple Food

Background: Downtown Seoul temples. High accessibility for foreigners makes these a good entry point to temple food.

  • Near Jogyesa (조계사, Ujeongguk-ro, Jongno-gu): Barugongyang, a temple-food specialty restaurant run by the Korean Buddhist Cultural Business Group next to Jogyesa — Seoul's flagship temple-food restaurant. Lunch course ₩25,000-45,000, dinner course ₩45,000-80,000. English menu available. Reservation recommended. Listed in the Michelin Guide Seoul Bib Gourmand in 2024.
  • Near Bongeunsa (봉은사, Samseong-dong, Gangnam-gu): Bongeunsa's own monks' dining hall opens during Buddhist events. The COEX commercial district right next to Bongeunsa hosts vegetarian restaurants influenced by temple cuisine.

Practical info:

  • Transport: Jogyesa → Jonggak Station Exit 2, Line 1, 5 minutes. Bongeunsa → Bongeunsa Station Exit 1, Line 9, 5 minutes.
  • English available: Barugongyang has an English menu and English-speaking staff. Bongeunsa's dining hall is Korean-only.
  • Vegan suitability: Barugongyang ★★★★★ (osinchae adjustments can be requested). Bongeunsa's dining hall ★★★★.

3. Temple Food Cooking Class (English)

Background: English-language temple-food cooking classes for foreigners have become established in Seoul. These include a hands-on cook-and-eat experience.

  • Korean Temple Food Culture Experience Center (run by the Korean Buddhist Cultural Business Group): Jongno-gu, Seoul. A regular English-language program. ₩70,000-120,000 per person. 2-3 hours. Participants cook 2-3 temple-food dishes themselves, then eat them as a formal meal. Advance reservation required.
  • Klook/KKday-listed temple-food classes: Searching "temple food cooking class Seoul" turns up several listed operators. Check reviews before choosing. Roughly ₩60,000-100,000.

Practical info:

  • Transport: Korean Temple Food Culture Experience Center → Anguk Station Exit 6, Line 3, 5 minutes on foot.
  • Duration: 2-3 hours. Morning slot at 10:00 AM or afternoon slot at 2:00 PM.
  • Vegan suitability: ★★★★★ (the entire class is vegetarian/vegan-based).

Axis 2: Downtown Vegan Cluster Deep Dive

4. Gangnam/Cheongdam Vegan Cluster

Background: Centered on Gangnam-gu and Seocho-gu. A dense concentration of luxury vegan restaurants and premium vegan cafes. The top-priority Seoul area for foreign vegan enthusiasts.

Cluster character:

  • Cheongdam-dong/Apgujeong-dong: fine-dining-style vegan restaurants using truffle and organic ingredients. ₩50,000-100,000+ per person.
  • Gangnam Station/Sinsa-dong: casual vegan cafes and vegan bakeries. ₩15,000-35,000 per person.
  • Search HappyCow for "Gangnam" or "Gangnam vegan" to check the list of currently operating businesses (turnover is fast, so search by app rather than relying on a fixed business name).

Access:

  • Gangnam Station, Line 2; Apgujeong Station, Line 3; Cheongdam Station, Line 7.
  • Card payment 100%.
  • English menus: most are bilingual with Japanese as well.

5. Hongdae/Hapjeong Vegan Cafes

Background: Hongdae and Hapjeong have the highest density of MZ-generation foreign visitors in Seoul. Several small, independent vegan/vegetarian cafes operate here. A high share of English-speaking staff and active Instagram presence are typical.

Cluster character:

  • Hongdae main gate to Sangsu Station: dense with vegan dessert and vegan brunch cafes.
  • Hapjeong Station to Mangwon Station: a wider genre mix — local vegan restaurants, vegetarian Japanese food, vegan Mexican.
  • Menu prices: vegan brunch set ₩15,000-28,000 / vegan cake-plus-drink set ₩12,000-20,000.
  • Search HappyCow "Hongdae vegan" for the latest list of operating businesses.

Access:

  • Hongik University Station, Line 2; Sangsu Station or Hapjeong Station, Line 6.
  • Crowded on weekend afternoons. Weekday mornings recommended.
  • English: mostly available, or an English menu is on hand.

6. Itaewon Vegan Restaurants

Background: Itaewon has the highest foreign-resident ratio of any Seoul commercial district. Vegan specialty restaurants took root here early, and many spots offer 100% English service. The mix of nationalities is matched by a wide range of vegan cuisine genres — Indian, Mexican, American-style vegan burgers, Korean-style vegan fusion.

Cluster character:

  • Itaewon-ro main street to Noksapyeong Station: vegan Indian food (curry, dal) and vegan American-style burgers.
  • Toward Hannam-dong: premium vegan restaurants. ₩30,000-55,000 per person.
  • The alley behind Itaewon Station (Sobangseo-gil): casual vegan cafes and vegan bakeries. ₩10,000-20,000 per person.
  • Search HappyCow "Itaewon vegan" for real-time confirmation of currently operating businesses.

Access:

  • Itaewon Station Exit 1, Line 6.
  • English available: nearly 100%.
  • Some spots also serve halal-certified vegan menus (check the halal marking).
  • Vegan certification: confirm at each individual restaurant (use HappyCow's category filter).

Axis 3: K-food Vegan Deep Dive

7. Vegetarian Versions of Bibimbap, Porridge, and Pancakes

Bibimbap (비빔밥):

  • Standard structure: rice + namul (spinach, bean sprouts, bellflower root, bracken fern) + gochujang + egg + meat.
  • Vegan request: "no egg, no meat" or asking for "vegetarian bibimbap" gets you namul only, no meat or egg. The namul itself is vegan. Some gochujang products contain shrimp paste, so gochujang at a temple-food restaurant is the safer choice.
  • Price: ₩8,000-15,000.

Porridge (juk):

  • Vegetable porridge, mushroom porridge, and pumpkin porridge are vegan-friendly. Avoid abalone porridge and chicken porridge.
  • Chain porridge restaurants like Bonjuk have English menus. Vegetable porridge ₩7,000-12,000.

Jeon (Korean pancake):

  • Scallion pancake and kimchi pancake can be made without egg in the batter. However, kimchi pancake carries the jeotgal-kimchi issue.
  • Scallion pancake and shiitake-mushroom pancake at temple-food restaurants are vegan. ₩8,000-16,000.

8. Kimbap and Vegetarian Kimchi-Jjigae

Kimbap:

  • Standard kimbap includes egg, ham, imitation crab, and tuna.
  • Ask for vegetarian kimbap, or choose vegetable kimbap: cucumber, pickled radish, carrot, and spinach only. ₩2,500-4,000 per roll.
  • Convenience stores (CU, GS25, 7-Eleven) also sell vegetable kimbap.

Vegetarian kimchi-jjigae:

  • Standard kimchi-jjigae: pork plus jeotgal-based kimchi.
  • Vegan version: tofu plus vegan kimchi (no jeotgal). Reliably vegan only at temple-food restaurants and vegetarian specialty restaurants.
  • Asking a regular Korean restaurant often removes the meat but leaves the jeotgal-kimchi or anchovy broth in place.

Practical tip: A convenience-store vegetable kimbap plus soy milk (plant-based) is a realistic budget vegan meal — available 24 hours, with English labeling.

9. Temple Food × K-food Fusion

Background: Some Seoul restaurants have recently begun blending temple-food techniques (no osinchae, fermentation-centered) into contemporary Korean menus. Awareness is rising among discerning foreign vegan foodies.

Characteristics:

  • Seasonal-vegetable course: 3-5 namul dishes + vegan doenjang soup + multigrain rice. ₩25,000-50,000.
  • Fermentation-centered: cooking built on soy sauce, doenjang, and gochujang fermented sauces. Deep flavor. Vegan-suitable.
  • Find these restaurants with a combined HappyCow "vegan" + "Korean" filter, or by searching Google Maps reviews for the keyword "temple food."

12. Season — Buddha's Birthday (May), the Best Time

Buddha's Birthday (부처님오신날, the 8th day of the 4th lunar month, falling in April-May on the solar calendar):

  • Temples nationwide hold lantern festivals alongside free or low-cost temple-food sharing.
  • Major Seoul temples like Jogyesa and Bongeunsa run public communal-meal events open to foreign visitors.
  • Tied to the Lotus Lantern Festival (연등회, UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage since 2020) — a lantern procession runs through Cheonggyecheon and Jongno.

Other seasons:

  • Fall (Sep-Nov): temple foliage season. Peak time to visit Tongdosa and Haeinsa.
  • Spring (Mar-Apr): heavy competition for temple-food class reservations.
  • Year-round: the Itaewon, Hongdae, and Gangnam vegan clusters run regardless of season.

FAQ

Q1. I heard vegetarian travel in Korea is hard — what's it really like?

Basing yourself in Itaewon, Hongdae, or Gangnam makes it easier than expected. HappyCow lists 300+ vegan/vegetarian-friendly businesses in Seoul. Regional areas are harder, but sticking to temple-food restaurants solves most of it.

Q2. Is Korean kimchi vegan?

Standard kimchi contains salted shrimp or anchovy sauce, so it's not vegan. Vegan kimchi (no jeotgal) is available at vegetarian specialty restaurants, temple-food restaurants, and some supermarkets (check for a vegan label).

Q3. What's the difference between temple food and general vegetarian food?

Temple food avoids osinchae (green onion, garlic, chives, wild chives, asafoetida). General vegetarian cooking often still uses these five ingredients. If you need to avoid osinchae for an allergy or religious reason, choose a temple-food restaurant specifically.

Q4. Do halal restaurants and vegan restaurants overlap?

Some restaurants in Itaewon offer halal-certified vegan menu items together. Search HappyCow combined with "Halal," or ask the restaurant directly about halal status. If you need full certainty, visually confirm the halal certification mark.

Q5. What kind of budget should I plan for a vegan trip?

Budget: ₩20,000-40,000/day (convenience-store kimbap, vegetarian kimbap, one vegan cafe visit). Mid-range: ₩60,000-100,000/day (two vegan restaurant meals). Premium: ₩150,000+/day (one fine-dining vegan meal plus a cooking class).

5 Tips

1. Time it around Buddha's Birthday in May: Jogyesa and Bongeunsa offer free temple-food meals open to the public. Combined with the Lotus Lantern Festival, it's one of Korea's best culture-plus-vegan experiences.

2. Pre-list on HappyCow: The night before visiting an area, save 3-5 vegan restaurants. Check break times and whether reservations are required.

3. Learn how to convert K-food to vegan: Bibimbap (no egg, no meat), vegetable kimbap, and vegetable porridge can all be ordered near-vegan even at a regular restaurant.

4. Use the 1330 tourist interpretation line actively: If communication breaks down, call ☎ 1330 to have staff speak directly with the restaurant and confirm vegan status on the spot.

5. Pair temple food with a tea ceremony (#735, #759): After barugongyang, connect to a traditional tea café in Insa-dong or Bukchon. A one-day route through traditional Korean culture.
