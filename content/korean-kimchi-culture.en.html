Korean Kimchi Culture — Why Koreans Eat Kimchi at Every Meal

Cabbage kimchi · kkakdugi · chonggak kimchi · water kimchi · fermentation · gimjang · origin dispute — a Korean kimchi-culture explainer for foreigners. Not a single plate of "spicy pickled cabbage," but the "why · what · how."

Sit down at a Korean restaurant and something arrives before you even order: a dish of red kimchi (김치). At a pork-belly grill, at a snack-bar (bunsik), even in airline in-flight meals — a Korean table almost always comes with kimchi. Foreigners usually file it away as "spicy fermented cabbage," but to Koreans kimchi is far more than a single side dish. It is a whole food universe with more than 200 varieties, a fermentation science thousands of years old, and a family-shared gimjang (김장, winter kimchi-making) tradition that earned a spot on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list — a symbol of Korea. Recently it even landed at the center of an international argument over "which country does kimchi belong to?" This piece is not about "where to go buy and eat kimchi" (→ cross-link); it explains, at a foreigner's eye level, the meaning, the types, the fermentation, and the history behind it. The point: kimchi is not "one kind of cabbage kimchi." Depending on vegetable, season, and region there are more than 200 varieties. There are also non-spicy ones — baek-kimchi (백김치, white kimchi) and mul-kimchi (물김치, water kimchi) — that make great entry points for foreigners.

⚠️ 3 things foreigners often get wrong

1. "Kimchi = one kind of spicy cabbage" ❌ — Kimchi is a huge category of over 200 varieties that splits by vegetable, season, and region. Kkakdugi (깍두기, radish kimchi) made from radish, oi-sobagi (오이소박이, cucumber kimchi) made from cucumber, mul-kimchi (물김치, water kimchi) eaten with its broth, and even baek-kimchi (백김치, white kimchi) made with no chili powder at all — these are all "kimchi." Plenty of kimchi isn't spicy.

2. "Kimchi = just pickled vegetables" ❌ — Kimchi is different from a Western vinegar pickle. It is made by lacto-fermentation. The lactic-acid bacteria (Lactobacillus) naturally present on the vegetables convert sugars into lactic acid, and the kimchi ripens over time — it is a living, fermented food.

3. "Kimchi is just an optional side" ❌ — To Koreans, kimchi is not a "take it or leave it" banchan (반찬, side dish). Many Koreans feel a meal isn't complete without it. A figure of roughly 37 kg per person per year shows just how central it is.

① What kimchi is — not "pickling" but "fermentation"

In one sentence, kimchi (김치) is a traditional Korean fermented food: vegetables salted, seasoned, and then lacto-fermented.

  • Step 1 — Salting: Vegetables such as napa cabbage and radish are soaked in brine to draw out water and improve keeping quality.
  • Step 2 — Seasoning: A seasoning paste (sok) of chili powder, garlic, ginger, jeotgal (젓갈, salted seafood), shredded radish, and more is mixed in.
  • Step 3 — Fermentation: Over time, lactic-acid bacteria turn sugars into lactic acid. That lactic acid gives kimchi its signature tang while also preventing spoilage and adding shelf life.

So the essence of kimchi is "salt + time = preservation + deep flavor." That's also why a just-made geotjeori (겉절이, fresh kimchi) tastes completely different from a fully ripened shin-kimchi (신김치, sour kimchi): kimchi is alive and constantly changing.

Note: kimchi is vegetable fermentation. The other great pillar of Korean fermentation — soybean fermentation (doenjang, soy sauce, gochujang) — is a separate, huge tradition → #1134 fermentation & jang (醬) culture.

② Types — a world of 200+ kimchi

Kimchi branches endlessly depending on which vegetable, in which season, in which region it is made. It is commonly counted at more than 200 kinds.

  • Baechu-kimchi (배추김치, napa-cabbage kimchi): The most iconic. Whole cabbage is salted and the seasoning is packed in. When a Korean says "kimchi," this is usually it.
  • Kkakdugi (깍두기, radish kimchi): Radish cut into cubes. An indispensable partner to seolleongtang, gomtang, and haejangguk (해장국, hangover soup).
  • Chonggak-kimchi (총각김치, ponytail-radish kimchi): Small radishes (chonggak-mu) pickled whole, greens and all. The pleasure of crunching into one.
  • Yeolmu-kimchi (열무김치, young-radish kimchi): A summer kimchi made with young radish (yeolmu). Refreshing, and pairs well with noodles and bibimbap.
  • Mul-kimchi / Dongchimi (물김치·동치미, water kimchi): Brothy kimchi. Not spicy; you drink the cool, tangy liquid too. Dongchimi is the classic water kimchi made with winter radish.
  • Baek-kimchi (백김치, white kimchi): White kimchi made without chili powder. Not spicy, so it suits foreigners and children.
  • Oi-sobagi (오이소박이, stuffed-cucumber kimchi): A summer kimchi with seasoning stuffed into slits in a cucumber. Crisp and fresh.
  • Gat-kimchi (갓김치, mustard-leaf kimchi): A strongly aromatic kimchi made with Dolsan mustard leaf from Yeosu in Jeolla. A regional specialty.

Foreigner tip: if heat worries you, start with baek-kimchi (백김치, white kimchi) and mul-kimchi / dongchimi (물김치·동치미, water kimchi). You get the cool, tangy side of fermentation without the spice.

③ The science of fermentation — lactic-acid bacteria and health

The reason kimchi draws global attention is the health effects fermentation creates.

  • Lacto-fermentation: The lactic-acid bacteria (Lactobacillus, etc.) naturally present on the vegetables break down sugars to make lactic acid. In this process kimchi turns tangy and gains keeping quality.
  • Probiotics (beneficial bacteria): Well-ripened kimchi is very rich in lactic-acid bacteria. The count is said to peak around 2–3 weeks after it's made, rather than at the very start of fermentation — meaning slightly ripened kimchi is especially good for gut health.
  • Health effects: Kimchi is a fermented food continually studied in relation to gut health, digestion, and immunity. The U.S. magazine Health even named it one of the "world's 5 healthiest foods" (alongside olive oil, yogurt, and others).

Caution: because kimchi is salted, it also comes with sodium. Being a "healthy food" does not mean you can eat it without limit; moderation is key — and Koreans, too, increasingly make it less salty.

④ Gimjang — a UNESCO heritage the whole family makes together

The pinnacle of kimchi culture is gimjang (김장, winter kimchi-making). Gimjang is Korea's annual event of making a large batch of kimchi all at once in late autumn to early winter to eat through the cold season.

  • Why gimjang: To get through cold winters when fresh vegetables were scarce, families and neighbors gathered when late-autumn cabbage was at its best and made dozens of heads at once. It's usually done in November to early December, as temperatures drop.
  • A communal event: Gimjang is not a solo job. Family and neighbors come together to salt the cabbage and mix the filling. It's shared out, and the know-how is passed to the next generation — both a food and a communal ritual.
  • ★ UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage (2013): This 'Kimjang, making and sharing kimchi' was inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2013. What was recognized was not merely a recipe, but the Korean communal spirit of sharing, solidarity, and transmission across generations.

Note: in Korean fermentation culture, UNESCO inscribed gimjang (2013) and jang-making (2024) separately. It shows that vegetable fermentation (kimchi) and soybean fermentation (jang) are two distinct, huge traditions → #1134.

⑤ The kimchi refrigerator — a fridge dedicated to kimchi

Many Korean homes have, on top of the regular fridge, a separate kimchi refrigerator (김치냉장고). Because kimchi is a living fermented food, it keeps its best flavor longest at a steady, low temperature — this appliance carries over the principle of the old practice of burying kimchi jars (gimchidok) in the ground. Dimchae (딤채), launched by Winia Mando in 1995, effectively opened this market. The very fact that people keep a separate refrigerator "just for kimchi" tells you how everyday and important this food is to Koreans.

⑥ Why at every meal — history and food culture

The question foreigners are most curious about: "Do Koreans really eat kimchi at every meal?" Largely, yes. Behind it lie history and food culture.

  • The base of the table: A traditional Korean table setting (bansang) is built on rice, soup, and kimchi. Kimchi isn't optional — it's the basic frame of the meal.
  • The wisdom of preservation: On a peninsula with four distinct seasons and long winters, kimchi — which ferments and stores vegetables to eat year-round — was survival wisdom. Even in seasons without fresh vegetables, kimchi supplied vitamins and fiber.
  • The arrival of chili: Today's red kimchi is actually a relatively recent look. Chili, a New World crop, reached Korea only around the 16th–17th century — before that, kimchi was closer to a white kimchi made with salt and garlic. Kimchi is a food that has kept changing over thousands of years.
  • A flavor base: Kimchi isn't just a side dish. It is the main ingredient in countless dishes — kimchi-jjigae, kimchi fried rice, kimchi-jeon, kimchi dumplings — and well-ripened sour kimchi is actually preferred for cooking.

⑦ ★ The origin dispute — which country does kimchi belong to (an objective summary)

In recent years kimchi has popped up in international news over a Korea–China "origin (which-came-first) dispute." Because it's an emotionally charged topic, here we lay out only the facts, neutrally.

  • The trigger (2020): In November 2020, reports said 'pao cai (泡菜, pao cai)', a pickled vegetable from Sichuan, China, had gained an international standard related to the International Organization for Standardization (ISO). When some Chinese outlets reported this as "the international standard for pickled vegetables," there was a backlash in Korea that "this makes it look as if it's the standard for kimchi."
  • The core point — kimchi ≠ pao cai: Objectively, kimchi and Sichuan pao cai are different foods, made differently and tasting different. Pao cai is mainly vegetables soaked in brine, while kimchi is seasoned with chili powder and jeotgal (젓갈, salted seafood) and then lacto-fermented. That said, Chinese had no separate word for "kimchi," so kimchi too was conventionally translated as 'pao cai' (han-guo pao cai = Korean pickled vegetables) — this overlap in translation was one cause of the confusion.
  • Korea's response (2021): In July 2021, Korea's Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism announced it would set the official Chinese-language name of kimchi as 'sinchi (신치, official Chinese name for kimchi; 辛奇 / xinqi)', distinct from the existing 'pao cai' (it said it reviewed some 4,000 Chinese characters and picked ones that sound close to "kimchi"). It was a move to clearly separate kimchi from pao cai. That said, this 'sinchi' name had been attempted once before in 2013, but failed to take hold because it didn't catch on locally in China.
  • A neutral summary: This dispute is cleanest to understand by accepting that both are true: that "the broad category of fermented/pickled vegetables is shared across several East Asian countries," and that "within it, kimchi stands out clearly as a uniquely Korean fermentation culture." UNESCO inscribing Korea's gimjang culture as Intangible Cultural Heritage was not an 'origin verdict' on the food itself, but recognition of Korea's social and cultural tradition of making and sharing kimchi together.

A safe stance for a foreigner: it's enough to understand it as "kimchi is Korean, pao cai is Chinese — different fermented/pickled foods." There's no need to take a side; you can respect both as their own cultures.

⑧ Kimchi in practice for foreigners — entry, manners, expressions

Practical tips for foreigners not yet used to heat and fermentation aromas.

  • Entry order: If spice worries you, go baek-kimchi (백김치, white kimchi) → mul-kimchi / dongchimi (물김치·동치미, water kimchi) → baechu-kimchi (배추김치, cabbage kimchi). And rather than fully ripened sour kimchi from the start, freshly made kimchi has a milder aroma.
  • If kimchi feels like a lot: If raw kimchi is tough, start with cooked kimchi dishes. In kimchi-jjigae, kimchi fried rice, and kimchi-jeon, the heat softens and the savory depth grows — very popular with foreigners.
  • How to eat it: Kimchi shines when eaten together with rice and meat. Paired with fatty meats like samgyeopsal (pork belly) or bossam, it cuts the richness.
  • Smell manners: The fermentation aroma (especially jeotgal, 젓갈, salted seafood) can be strong. It's a natural part of fermentation, so don't be thrown. At restaurants, kimchi is usually a free refill (a banchan), so feel free to ask for more.
  • Vegetarian check: Many kimchi contain jeotgal (젓갈, salted seafood; aekjeot fish sauce, saeu-jeot salted shrimp) and may not suit strict vegans. If you're vegan, look for baek-kimchi (백김치, white kimchi) or temple kimchi (사찰김치, made without salted seafood), or check in advance.

❓ Frequently asked questions from foreigners (FAQ)

  • Is all kimchi spicy? — No. Baek-kimchi (백김치, white kimchi) and mul-kimchi / dongchimi (물김치·동치미, water kimchi) use little or no chili powder and aren't spicy. If heat worries you, start here.
  • The kimchi smell is strong — is that okay? — It's the natural aroma of a fermented food. The riper it gets, the more sour the smell — a sign the lactic-acid fermentation went well. It's not the same as spoilage.
  • Do people still make kimchi at home? — Store-bought has grown, but many homes still make their own in the late-autumn gimjang (김장, winter kimchi-making). Gimjang is an annual event done with family and neighbors.
  • Is kimchi vegetarian? / Is it Korean? — Most kimchi contains jeotgal (젓갈, salted seafood), so it isn't fully vegan (look for baek-kimchi or temple kimchi made without it). For the origin dispute see ⑦ — kimchi is a uniquely Korean food, different from Chinese pao cai in method and taste.
