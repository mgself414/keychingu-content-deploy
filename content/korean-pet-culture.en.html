Korean pet culture — In an era of 15 million pet owners, how did pets become ‘family’ in Korea?

Pets, pet families, petconomy, dog cafes — Korean pet culture explainer for foreigners. Not ‘Korea also raises dogs and cats,’ but ‘Why and how did they become a family, and what are the lights and shadows?’

There is a view you see when you sit in a cafe in Seoul. A person pushing a stroller approaches, but when you look inside, you see that it is not a baby but a puppy. Some Starbucks stores have a 100-pyeong “dog playground,” and department stores have a floor dedicated to pets. There are approximately 15.46 million people in Korea who own pets – approximately 30% of the total population (KB Financial estimate, confirmation recommended). But what's more interesting isn't the numbers, but the words. Koreans don't often say "pet" anymore. "Companion animal" — In other words, it is called a 'companion who walks together through life.' In the eyes of foreigners, it is just 'pet culture', but in Korea, there is a rapid and deep social change in which animals were upgraded from 'toys' to 'family' in just one generation. This article is not about “Which dog cafe is better?” (→ cross-link), but explains the changes and meaning behind it, and ★light and shadow from a foreigner’s perspective. ⚠️ 3 things foreigners often misunderstand

1. "Isn't Korea a country that doesn't like (or eat) dogs?" ❌ — It's a misunderstanding based on past images. Today, Korea has quickly become one of the most pet-friendly societies in Asia, with approximately 30% of the population being pet owners. (Note: The breeding, slaughter, and distribution of dog meat will be gradually banned in 2024 under the 'Special Act on Dog Meat Breeds' - Check recommended.)

2. "Isn't 'pet' just a Korean translation of pet?" ❌ — It's not a simple translation, but intentional word replacement. The very act of abandoning 'pet' (loved as a plaything) and choosing 'companion (companion)' is close to a social declaration showing the status elevation of animals (② below).

3. "If you raise them like family, everyone must be happy?" ❌ — That's half the truth. Familyization and welfare have clearly improved, but at the same time, the shadows of over 100,000 abandoned animals per year, pet shop/breeding farm controversy, and noise/defecation conflict have also grown (⑥ below). This is why this article deals with light and dark together.

① What is pet culture — a social phenomenon that goes beyond ‘raising’ pets?

In Korea, 'pet culture' is not simply the act of raising animals, but refers to a social phenomenon that has completely reorganized consumption, space, relationships, and industry.

  • Size: Approximately 5.91 million pet households, approximately 15.46 million pet owners. Close to 30% of the total population (KB Financial estimate, confirmation recommended). In particular, more than half of pet households are concentrated in the metropolitan area (Seoul, Gyeonggi, Incheon).
  • Change in title: Pet owners call themselves 'steward' (cat) or 'dog owner/mom dad', and refer to the animal as 'our child'. The sentiment of raising animals as if raising children is embedded in the language.
  • 'Pet+Family': People who consider their pets as family members. This sentiment is the engine of the huge petconomy that we will see below.

In other words, understanding Korea's pet culture means understanding the social question 'Why did Koreans make animals part of their family?'

② ‘Pet → Companion’ — The status of animals changed by words

The core of Korean pet culture is condensed in a swap of words.

  • Aewan-dongmul (애완동물, 愛玩動物, ‘pet’): An animal you ‘love (愛) and play with (玩).’ In other words, it carries the nuance of being an object for human enjoyment.
  • Ballyeo-dongmul (반려동물, 伴侶動物, ‘companion animal’): ‘Ban-lyeo (伴侶) = a companion who walks through life together.’ It implies a horizontal relationship and emotional family — the same spirit as the English companion animal.
  • When did it change?: Animal welfare groups proposed ‘ballyeo’ as a replacement for ‘aewan,’ and since the 2000s it has become everyday vocabulary through inclusion in the Standard Korean Dictionary, adoption in legislation, and use in the media. In other words, this wasn’t a change imposed from above — it’s a change society itself accepted.

It’s just one small word, but abandoning ‘plaything (玩)’ for ‘companion (侶)’ signals that the way Korean society sees animals has itself changed. It’s the single most useful code for foreigners to know first about Korean pet culture.

③ The Petconomy — An Economy That Opens Its Wallet for ‘Our Child’

Once animals became family, an entire industry for that family — the petconomy (펫코노미), Pet + Economy — exploded.

  • Market size: Korea’s pet market surpassed roughly ₩8 trillion in 2022 and is projected to grow to around ₩21 trillion by 2032 (KPMG and others, confirmation recommended) — double-digit growth every year.
  • Beyond food and treats: It started out centered on food and supplies, but has now expanded into veterinary hospitals, pet insurance, grooming, pet hotels, daycare, pet funerals (pet loss), premium appliances, and even pet strollers.
  • ’Our child’ spending: The core driver is the sentiment of spending on animals as freely as you would on a child. Average monthly pet-care cost per household is around ₩190,000 (confirmation recommended), and it rises every year.
  • A retail battleground: Department stores, big-box marts, and e-commerce platforms are racing to open dedicated pet floors to compete for ‘pet-fam’ shoppers.

To foreign tourists, sights like ’animal department stores,’ ‘pet hotels,’ and ‘dog daycare’ are unfamiliar and fascinating.

④ Why Did It Happen 'Now' — The Backdrop of Low Birth Rates and Single-Person Households

The deepest question here: why exactly now, in Korea, did animals become family? The answer lies in Korea's changing demographic structure. (This sociological backdrop shares the same roots as #1159 Solo Dining Culture.)

  • The world's lowest birth rate: Korea's total fertility rate is around 0.7 (confirmation recommended), among the lowest in the world. As more people choose not to have children, the object of emotional caregiving has shifted toward animals. Some people call their pets 'the child born from the heart.'
  • A surge in single-person households: Over 40% of all households are single-person households (confirmation recommended). For people living alone, a pet is a companion that eases loneliness and a reason to come home. → If solo dining (#1159) is 'the normalization of being alone,' a pet is 'the device that turns alone into together.'
  • Aging population: For middle-aged and older Koreans whose children have moved out, a pet becomes family that fills the emotional void.
  • 'Pet Humanization': The academic name for all of this. It's a global trend of treating animals like human family members, but in Korea, low birth rates, single-person households, and an aging population all act at once, making the pace unusually fast.

In short, Korea's pet boom isn't simply 'love of animals' — it's one facet of a massive social shift in what a family looks like. It's one of the deepest angles for explaining Korea to a foreigner.

⑤ Pet Cafes and Pet-Friendly Spaces — A City to Enjoy With Animals

Animals that became family come out of the house together. That's what gave rise to pet-friendly spaces.

  • Dog cafes: There are two kinds. ① Cafes you go to in order to meet animals (bonding with the resident dogs or cats), and ② pet-friendly cafes where you bring your own pet. The one popular with foreign tourists is usually ① (a low-pressure way to try it out even if you're nervous around animals).
  • Pet-friendly chains spreading: It used to be only independent dog cafes, but now large franchises like Starbucks and Hollys also open 'pet-friendly' stores. Some even have a dog playground or a 'dog parking' space.
  • Scale: Some counts put the number of pet-friendly restaurants, cafes, and spaces nationwide at around 15,000 (based on app data, confirmation recommended).
  • A rule worth knowing: Bringing a dog indoors at an ordinary licensed food-service restaurant is, in principle, not allowed — only separately partitioned and registered spaces qualify. Policies differ store by store, so checking before you visit is essential.

Foreigner tip: Even without a pet, you can bond with dogs or cats at an 'animal cafe.' That said, be cautious with some exotic-animal cafes (raccoons, meerkats, etc.) that carry animal-welfare controversy. See a separate guide for locations and reservations (to avoid duplication).

⑥ ★ Light and Shadow — The Dark Side of Familization: Abandonment, Pet Shops, and Conflict

This is the most important section in this article. Familization and improved welfare are clearly the light side, but leaving out the shadow behind it means seeing only half of Korean pet culture.

(1) Abandoned animals — the most painful paradox of familization

  • Roughly 107,000 animals are abandoned or lost each year (Animal and Plant Quarantine Agency, 2024 estimate, confirmation recommended). It has declined for five straight years, but the number remains large.
  • Of the animals that enter shelters, only about 23.5% are adopted into a new family, while natural death plus euthanasia reaches around 46% (confirmation recommended). These are the moments when the promise of 'family' breaks down.
  • Nationwide, animal shelter operating costs run around ₩46.4 billion a year and keep rising (funded by taxes).

(2) Pet shop and breeding-farm ('puppy mill') controversy

  • In Korea, many pets are still 'purchased' through pet shops. But upstream in that supply chain sits the problem of breeding farms — the so-called 'puppy mills.'
  • Animal-welfare groups (such as KARA) claim there are several thousand breeding farms nationwide (confirmation recommended), and criticize the harsh conditions in which mother dogs are caged in wire-floor cages (tteun-jang, 뜬장) and forced into repeated pregnancy and birth.
  • This is why the 'Adopt, Don't Shop' campaign is active. The contradiction between a culture of familization and a 'production and sale' structure is the central tension in Korean pet culture.

(3) Pet owners vs. non-owners (pet-iquette)

  • As 30% of the population became pet owners, friction with the remaining 70% also grew. The top complaint from non-pet households is 'leaving waste uncleaned' (about 61%), followed by 'not using a leash' (about 48%) and 'ignoring barking noise' (about 35%) (per surveys, confirmation recommended).
  • Apartment noise between floors (barking), complaints over park access, and 'No-Pet Zones' are all social issues. This gave rise to the coined term pet-iquette (펫티켓) — Pet + Etiquette — the public manners pet owners are expected to follow.

(4) High cost

  • Treating a pet like family comes at a cost. On top of monthly care costs, surgery and treatment for an aging or sick animal can run into the millions of won. The pressure of 'being responsible to the end because they're family' can, paradoxically, lead right back to the abandonment described in (1).

The key point: Korean pet culture has the light of 'familization' and the shadow of 'abandonment, pet shops, and conflict' in one body. Looking only at the light glamorizes it; looking only at the shadow unfairly diminishes it — you have to see both to get the real picture.

⑦ Korean Pet Culture Through Foreign Eyes — What Stands Out

Features of Korean pet culture as seen through the eyes of travelers and foreign residents.

  • The intensity of raising them 'like a child': pet strollers, pet hanbok, dog birthday parties, pet daycare and hotels — the sheer density of care given to animals as if they were children is strikingly high to foreign eyes.
  • Cat cafes and small dogs dominate: Because housing is apartment-centered, small dogs and cats make up a large share, so pet culture is 'urban' in shape (unlike a large-dog-and-yard culture).
  • Fast-moving infrastructure: Pet-friendly cafes, hotels, hospitals, and groomers are densely packed across the city. Access to animal-related services is very good.
  • Where to experience it: If you like animals, a cat or dog cafe is a light, enjoyable Korean-style experience. That said, if you're a foreigner sensitive to animal welfare, it's a more mature perspective to also know the light-and-shadow issues in ⑥ above (abandonment, exotic-cafe controversy).

❓ Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) for Foreigners

  • Is dog meat still eaten in Korea? — With the 2024 Special Act on Ending Dog Meat Consumption, breeding, slaughter, and distribution are being phased out step by step (confirmation recommended). Among the younger urban generation, the practice has already nearly disappeared, and pet culture is the mainstream instead.
  • Can I visit a pet cafe while traveling? — Yes. You can enjoy a bonding experience at a cat or dog cafe even without your own pet. Just check the store's policy and animal-welfare reputation, and be cautious with exotic-animal (raccoon, meerkat, etc.) cafes.
  • Are 'ballyeo-dongmul' and 'pet' different words? — They mean the same thing, but the nuance differs. The fact that Koreans deliberately dropped 'aewan' for 'ballyeo (companion)' carries the social implication that the animal's status has risen to family level.
  • Why is there a dog riding in that stroller? — It's a Korean scene combining protection for elderly or small dogs, convenience when moving around with a pet, and the 'our child' sentiment. It's common, so no need to be surprised.
  • Does Korean pet culture have a dark side too? — Yes. Over 100,000 abandoned animals a year, pet-shop and breeding-farm controversy, and pet-iquette conflicts are the main examples (⑥ above). Seeing both the light and the shadow together is what gives an accurate understanding.
