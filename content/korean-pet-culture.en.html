Korea's Pet Culture — How Pets Became "Family" in a Nation of 15 Million Pet Parents

> Banryeo-dongmul (반려동물, companion animals) · Petfamjok (펫팸족, pet+family tribe) · Petconomy · Aegyeon-kape (애견카페, dog cafes) — A Korean pet culture explainer for foreigners. It's not about 'Korea also raises dogs and cats,' but 'why and how they became family, and what are the light and shadow aspects.'

When you sit in a cafe in Seoul (서울), you'll often see a particular sight. Someone approaches pushing a stroller, and when you peek inside, it's not a baby, but a dog. Some Starbucks (스타벅스) stores even have a 100-pyeong (약 330 sqm) 'dog playground' attached, and department stores are creating floors exclusively for pets. In Korea, there are approximately 15.46 million pet parents — about 30% of the entire population (estimated by KB Financial Group (KB금융), verification recommended). But what's even more interesting than the numbers is the word itself. Koreans no longer commonly say "aewan-dongmul (애완동물, "pet" in the sense of "toy for affection")". Instead, they use "banryeo-dongmul (반려동물, "companion animal")" — meaning 'a companion who walks with you through life.' To a foreigner, it might just seem like 'pet culture,' but in Korea, within just one generation, animals have been elevated from 'playthings (玩)' to 'family,' reflecting a rapid and profound social change. This article doesn't focus on "which dog cafe is good" (→ cross-link), but rather explains the changes, meaning, and ★light and shadow behind it from a foreigner's perspective. This is the 33rd installment in the Korean Culture Explainer Series.

⚠️ 3 Common Misconceptions Foreigners Often Have

1. "Isn't Korea a country that dislikes (or eats) dogs?" ❌ — This is a misunderstanding based on past images. Today, Korea has rapidly transformed into one of Asia's leading pet-friendly societies, with approximately 30% of its population being pet parents. (Note: Dog meat consumption was gradually banned for breeding, slaughter, and distribution by the Special Act on the End of Dog Meat Consumption (개식용 종식 특별법) in 2024 — verification recommended.)

2. "'Banryeo-dongmul (반려동물)' is just a Korean translation of 'pet,' isn't it?" ❌ — It's not a simple translation but an intentional word replacement. The choice to abandon 'aewan (애완, "loving as a plaything")' and adopt 'banryeo (반려, "companion who walks together")' itself is a social declaration that demonstrates the elevated status of animals (see ② below).

3. "If they raise them like family, everyone must be happy, right?" ❌ — That's only half the truth. While familialization and welfare have certainly improved, simultaneously, the shadow of over 100,000 abandoned animals annually, controversies surrounding pet shops and breeding farms, and conflicts over noise and waste disposal has also grown (see ⑥ below). This is why this article covers both the light and shadow.

① What is Pet Culture? — A Social Phenomenon Beyond Just 'Raising' Animals

In Korea, 'pet culture' refers not merely to the act of raising animals, but to a social phenomenon where consumption, spaces, relationships, and industries have been entirely reorganized.

  • Scale: Approximately 5.91 million pet households and 15.46 million pet parents. This approaches 30% of the total population (KB Financial Group (KB금융) estimate, verification recommended). Notably, over half of pet households are concentrated in the Seoul Metropolitan Area (수도권, Seoul (서울), Gyeonggi (경기), Incheon (인천)).
  • Change in Titles: Pet parents refer to themselves as 'jipsa (집사, "butler")' (for cats) or 'gyeonju (견주, "dog owner") / eomppa (엄빠, "mom and dad")' (for dogs), and call their animals 'uri-ai (우리 아이, "our child")'. The sentiment of raising animals as if they were children is directly embedded in the language.
  • 'Petfamjok (펫팸족, Pet+Family)': People who consider their companion animals as family members. This sentiment is the engine behind the massive Petconomy we'll explore below.

In other words, understanding Korean pet culture means understanding the social question of 'why Koreans have made animals into family.'

② 'Aewan (애완) → Banryeo (반려)' — How a Word Changed the Status of Animals

The essence of Korean pet culture is condensed in the replacement of a word.

  • Aewan-dongmul (애완동물): An animal 'loved (愛) and played with (玩).' This carries the nuance of being an object for human enjoyment.
  • Banryeo-dongmul (반려동물): 'Companion (伴侶) = a partner who walks together through life.' This signifies a horizontal relationship and an emotional family member. It's akin to the English term companion animal.
  • When did it change?: Animal protection organizations and others proposed 'banryeo (반려)' as an alternative to 'aewan (애완),' and after the 2000s, it became common language through its inclusion in the Standard Korean Dictionary (표준국어대사전), adoption in legislation, and use in media. This means it was a societal change that was embraced, not imposed from above.

> Though just a small word, choosing 'companion (侶)' over 'plaything (玩)' is a signal that the Korean society's perspective on animals itself has changed. It's the most important code for foreigners to understand first in Korean pet culture.

③ Petconomy — An Economy Where Wallets Open for 'Our Child'

As animals became family, the industry for these family members (Petconomy, Pet+Economy) exploded.

  • Market Size: The domestic pet market in Korea exceeded ₩8 trillion in 2022 and is projected to grow to approximately ₩21 trillion by 2032 (KPMG, etc., verification recommended). It boasts a double-digit annual growth rate.
  • Beyond Food and Snacks: While initially centered on food and supplies, it has now expanded to animal hospitals, pet insurance, grooming (trimming), hotels, kindergartens (daycare), funerals (pet loss), premium home appliances, and even pet strollers.
  • 'Our Child' Consumption: The core driving force is the sentiment of spending generously on animals, just as one would on children. The average monthly cost of raising a pet is about ₩190,000 (verification recommended), increasing every year.
  • Retail Battleground: Department stores (백화점), large supermarkets (대형마트), and e-commerce (이커머스) platforms are all vying to open pet-exclusive sections and attract 'Petfamjok (펫팸족).'

To foreign tourists, sights like 'animal department stores,' 'pet hotels,' and 'dog kindergartens' are unfamiliar and intriguing.

④ Why Did They Become Family 'Now'? — The Background of Low Birth Rates and Single-Person Households

The deepest question: Why did animals become family in Korea, and why now? The answer lies in Korea's changing demographic structure. (※ This sociological background shares roots with [#1159 Solo Dining Culture](1159_korean_solo_dining_culture.md).)

  • World's Lowest Birth Rate: Korea's total fertility rate is approximately 0.7 children (verification recommended), the lowest in the world. As the choice 'not to have children' increases, there's a shift in the object of emotional care towards animals. Some even call pets "children born from the heart."
  • Rapid Increase in Single-Person Households: Over 40% of all households are single-person households (verification recommended). For those living alone, pets are companions that alleviate loneliness and a reason to return home. → While honbap (혼밥, solo dining) is the 'normalization of being alone,' pets are a 'device that transforms being alone into being together.'
  • Aging Population: For middle-aged and elderly people whose children have become independent, pets fill an emotional void as family members.
  • 'Pet Humanization': The academic term for all these changes. It's a global trend of treating animals like humans (family), but in Korea, the combination of low birth rates + single-person households + an aging population has made its pace uniquely fast.

> In essence, Korea's pet boom is not merely 'animal love,' but a facet of a massive social change where the form of family is transforming. This is the most profound angle when explaining Korea to foreigners.

⑤ Pet Cafes and Companion Spaces — Enjoying the City with Animals

As animals became family, they started coming out of the house. This led to the creation of pet-friendly spaces.

  • Aegyeon-kape (애견카페, Dog Cafes): There are two types. ① Cafes where you go to meet animals (interact with dogs/cats belonging to the cafe), and ② pet-friendly cafes where you bring your own pet. Type ① is usually popular with foreign tourists (easy to experience even if you're afraid of animals).
  • Spread of Pet-Friendly Establishments: In the past, only individual dog cafes existed, but now large franchises like Starbucks (스타벅스) and Hollys Coffee (할리스) are also opening 'pet-friendly' stores. Some even have dog playgrounds and 'dog parking' spaces.
  • Scale: There are estimates of over 15,000 pet-friendly restaurants, cafes, and spaces nationwide (app-based, verification recommended).
  • Regulations to Know: In general, companion dogs are not allowed inside regular 'food service businesses (식품접객업)' restaurants; only separately designated and reported spaces are permitted. Therefore, policies vary by establishment, so checking before visiting is essential.

> Foreigner Tip: Even without your own pet, you can experience interacting with dogs and cats at 'animal cafes.' However, be cautious with some exotic animal cafes (raccoons, meerkats, etc.) that have animal welfare controversies. For location and reservation information, refer to a separate guide (to avoid duplication).

⑥ ★ Light and Shadow — The Dark Side of Familialization: Abandonment, Pet Shops, and Conflicts

This is the most important section in this article. While familialization and improved welfare are certainly the light, excluding the shadow behind them means seeing only half of Korean pet culture.

(1) Abandoned Animals — The Most Painful Paradox of Familialization

  • Approximately 107,000 abandoned or lost animals occur annually (Animal and Plant Quarantine Agency (농림축산검역본부), estimated 2024, verification recommended). While it's been decreasing for five consecutive years, it's still a large number.
  • Among animals brought into shelters, the adoption rate by new families is only about 23.5%, while natural death + euthanasia accounts for about 46% (verification recommended). These are the moments when the promise of 'family' is broken.
  • The operating costs for animal shelters nationwide are around ₩46.4 billion annually, increasing every year (tax-funded).

(2) Pet Shop and Breeding Farm ("Puppy Mill") Controversies

  • In Korea, many pets are still 'purchased' through pet shops (aegyeon maejang, 애견 매장). However, above this supply chain lies the problem of breeding farms (euphemistically called "puppy mills").
  • Animal welfare organizations (such as KARA (카라)) claim there are thousands of breeding farms nationwide (verification recommended), raising concerns about the deplorable conditions where mother dogs are confined in 'tteunjang (뜬장, raised wire cages)' and subjected to repeated pregnancies and births.
  • Therefore, the "Adopt, Don't Shop" campaign is active. The contradiction between the culture of familialization and the structure of 'production and sale' is a core tension in Korean pet culture.

(3) Pet Parent vs. Non-Pet Parent Conflicts (Pettiquette)

  • As 30% of the population became pet parents, friction with the remaining 70% also grew. The number one inconvenience experienced by non-pet households is 'leaving pet waste unattended' (approx. 61%), followed by 'not using leashes' (approx. 48%), and 'leaving barking noise unattended' (approx. 35%) (based on survey, verification recommended).
  • Apartment noise between floors (층간소음, dog barking), complaints about park access, and 'No-Pet Zones (노펫존)' are social issues. This led to the emergence of the neologism 'Pettiquette (펫티켓, Pet+Etiquette)' — public manners that pet parents should observe.

(4) High Costs

  • The cost of treating pets as family is financial. In addition to monthly care expenses, surgery and treatment costs can reach millions of won for elderly or sick pets. The burden of 'taking responsibility until the end as family' paradoxically sometimes leads to abandonment (as in (1)).

> Key point: Korean pet culture is a single entity with the light of 'familialization' and the shadow of 'abandonment, pet shops, and conflicts.' Seeing only the light beautifies it, and only the shadow disparages it — one must see both to understand its true nature.

⑦ Korean Pet Culture as Seen by Foreigners — What's Impressive?

Characteristics of Korean pet culture as observed by travelers and foreign residents.

  • Intensity of Raising 'Like a Child': Pet strollers, pet hanbok (한복, traditional Korean clothing), dog birthday parties, pet kindergartens and hotels — the density of care and devotion in treating animals like children is surprisingly high to foreign eyes.
  • Focus on Cat Cafes and Small Dogs: Due to apartment-centric housing, small dogs and cats are prevalent, making pet culture more 'urban' (different from large dog/yard culture).
  • Rapid Infrastructure: Pet-friendly cafes, hotels, hospitals, and grooming services are densely available in cities. Access to animal-related services is generally very good.
  • Experience Points: If you like animals, cat/dog cafes offer a light and enjoyable Korean experience. However, if you are a foreigner sensitive to animal welfare, it's a more mature perspective to be aware of the light and shadow (abandonment, exotic animal cafe controversies) mentioned in ⑥.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions for Foreigners (FAQ)

  • Do people still eat dog meat in Korea? — The Special Act on the End of Dog Meat Consumption (개식용 종식 특별법) was enacted in 2024, moving towards a phased ban on breeding, slaughter, and distribution (verification recommended). Among young urban generations, it's already an almost vanished custom, and pet culture is the mainstream.
  • Can I visit a pet cafe during my trip? — Yes. Even if you don't have your own pet, you can experience interaction at cat/dog cafes. However, check the store's policy and animal welfare reputation, and be cautious with exotic animal (raccoon, meerkat, etc.) cafes.
  • Are 'banryeo-dongmul (반려동물)' and 'pet' different terms? — The meaning is similar, but the nuance is different. The fact that Koreans intentionally abandoned 'aewan (애완)' and adopted 'banryeo (반려, companion)' implies a social significance: the status of animals has been elevated to that of family.
  • Why are dogs riding in strollers? — This is a uniquely Korean sight, combining the protection of elderly/small dogs, convenience for pet-accompanied travel, and the 'our child' sentiment. It's a common occurrence, so no need to be surprised.
  • Are there any dark sides to Korean pet culture? — Yes. Over 100,000 abandoned animals annually, pet shop and breeding farm controversies, and Pettiquette (펫티켓) conflicts are representative (see ⑥ above). Seeing both the light and shadow provides an accurate understanding.
