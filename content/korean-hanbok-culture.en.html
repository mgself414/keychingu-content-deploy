Korean Hanbok Culture — Why go to the palace wearing Hanbok, the meaning and revival of traditional clothing

Free admission to hanbok, jeogori, skirt, five cardinal colors, palace — Korean traditional clothing explainer for foreigners. Not ‘pretty costumes’ but ‘why, what, and how.’

If you have ever walked in front of Gyeongbokgung Palace, there is a scene you would have seen. People wearing colorful hanbok take pictures with the palace stone wall in the background, and about half of them are foreigners. In fact, in 2025 alone, the number of visitors to Gyeongbokgung Palace, Changdeokgung Palace, Deoksugung Palace, Changgyeonggung Palace, and Jongmyo Shrine wearing hanbok exceeded 2.07 million — a number that has increased by more than 260% in three years from about 540,000 in 2022. Court costumes in K-dramas, modern hanbok worn on stage by Blackpink and BTS, Bukchon Hanok Village, which has become a popular photo spot — hanbok is currently the hottest 'wearing experience' in Korean travel. However, most foreigners view Hanbok as just a “pretty traditional costume.” In fact, each curve, color, and wearing time of Hanbok contains hundreds of years of meaning. This article is not about “where to borrow it” (→ location guide), but explains the structure, aesthetics, why you wear it, and how it was revived from a foreigner’s perspective. ⚠️ 3 things foreigners often misunderstand

1. "Hanbok = costume/cosplay" ❌ — Hanbok is not a costume for an event, but Korea's official traditional costume (national dress). Even now, it is a living piece of clothing that is actually worn at important moments in life such as holidays, weddings, and first birthday parties. Rental clothing for photography is just an entrance to lightly experience the culture.

2. "Hanbok is just a fancy dress" ❌ — The color and shape of Hanbok have meaning. The five colors (Five Colors) come from the idea of ​​Yin-Yang and the Five Elements, and the curvy beauty created by straight fabric touching the body is the core of Hanbok aesthetics. It is not clothes that are flashy but moderate and harmonious.

3. "Hanbok is old clothes in a museum" ❌ — Hanbok is not a stuffed past. Hanbok for everyday life that is comfortable to wear like jeans, fusion hanbok performed on stage by BTS and Blackpink, and even the Milan runway — hanbok is an ongoing fashion that is still evolving.

① What is Hanbok — Korean clothing?

Hanbok(韓服) literally means 'Korean clothing'. It is Korea's unique traditional clothing that has been passed down since the Three Kingdoms period, and the form we think of today (jeogori + skirt, jeogori + pants) was mainly established during the Joseon Dynasty (1392-1910).

The most fundamental characteristic of Hanbok is that the top and bottom are separate. Above, both men and women wear jeogori, and below, women wear skirt and men wear pants. Unlike Western suits that are cut along the lines of the body, Hanbok covers the body generously and creates natural lines according to the wearer's movements. That's why they say that Hanbok is not "clothes that fit the body," but "clothes that embrace the body."

② Structure of Hanbok — Jeogori, skirt, pants, pus

The most unfamiliar thing when a foreigner tries on Hanbok is that it has no buttons. Hanbok is fastened with strings. Let’s point out the key parts.

  • Jeogori (Jeogori): A short upper garment worn on top. Basic clothing for both men and women. It consists of several parts.
  • Collar: A collar that wraps around the neck.
  • Dongjeong (동정): a white strip of fabric layered over the collar. It's kept perfectly white at all times as a symbol of neatness and cleanliness.
  • Seop (섶): the left and right overlapping panels where the front closes.
  • Sleeve and baerae (배래): the curve along the underside of the sleeve (baerae) is one axis of hanbok's curved aesthetic.
  • Otgoreum (고름): the long tie that closes the front of the jeogori. A long strip and a short strip are tied together on one side into a half-bow (oegoreum) shape. Instead of buttons, this tie fastens the hanbok, and the shape of the knot and the trailing lines become decoration in themselves — the most iconic detail of hanbok.
  • Chima (치마): the woman's bottom garment. Several panels of fabric are joined and pleated at the top, then tied with a strap above the chest (below the armpits) so it falls full and voluminous. The wide width creates a rippling silhouette when sitting or walking.
  • Baji (바지): the man's bottom garment. Very roomy in the legs, comfortable for sitting cross-legged. The ankles are tied and tidied with a daenim (string).
  • Durumagi (두루마기): a coat-type outer garment worn over the jeogori when going out. It means "to wrap all the way around," and in men's hanbok in particular, it's considered proper etiquette for formal dress.

Add a vest-type overcoat, baeja (배자), and a decorative knotted ornament worn at the waist or tie, norigae (노리개), and a full outfit is complete.

③ Color and Curve — The Two Axes of Hanbok Aesthetics

The reason hanbok isn't simply a "flashy garment" lies in the thinking behind its color and lines.

(1) Obangsaek (오방색) — Wearing the Order of the Universe

Hanbok's traditional color palette is rooted in obangsaek (오방색), the five basic colors that come from yin-yang and the five elements.

  • Blue — east, spring, life / Red — south, summer, passion / Yellow — center, earth, authority / White — west, autumn, purity / Black — north, winter, wisdom.

These five colors weren't just colors — they symbolized the order and harmony connecting nature, humanity, and the cosmos. For example, a new bride's yellow jeogori with a crimson chima, and the saekdong (multicolored striped) sleeves on a baby's first-birthday outfit, both carried wishes for good fortune and health. (Obangsaek runs through more than just hanbok — dancheong, food, and tradition throughout Korea → #1147)

(2) Curved Beauty — Lines Made by Straight Fabric

Hanbok fabric, laid flat, is simply straight (rectangular). But once wrapped and worn on the body, the curve of the jeogori's baerae, the pleats of the chima, and the flow of the otgoreum meet to bring out fluid curves. This "flow of line," where straight and curved reverse into and weave with each other, is the essence of hanbok aesthetics. Traditional hanbok also used natural materials like ramie, hemp, and silk, dyed with natural dyes such as indigo and safflower, producing subtle, deep tones.

④ When Is It Worn — At Every Knot in Life

Hanbok is less an everyday garment than clothing that marks important moments in life. Following a Korean's life course shows where hanbok appears.

  • Holidays — on Seollal (Lunar New Year) and Chuseok (harvest festival), people wear hanbok to perform sebae (a formal bow) to elders or hold ancestral rites. A child in saekdong hanbok is the iconic image of the holidays. (→ #1136)
  • Doljanchi (first birthday) — on a baby's first birthday, a saekdong hanbok and a small hat (bokgeon for boys, jobawi for girls) are worn to wish for health and good fortune.
  • Weddings and pyebaek — in a traditional wedding, the bride wears a hwarot or wonsam with a jokduri headpiece, and the groom wears a samo-gwandae. Today, after a Western-style wedding, the bride and groom change into hanbok for the pyebaek ceremony, bowing to the groom's parents and elders.
  • Coming-of-age ceremonies and hwangap (60th birthday), etc. — hanbok is also worn as formal dress for coming-of-age rites or an elder's major birthday.

In this way, hanbok has served to visually express the meaning of the moment at every rite of passage — birth, coming of age, marriage. (Rites of passage overall → #1144)

⑤ ★Modern Revival — Free Palace Admission, the Rental Boom, and K-pop Hanbok

Hanbok, which nearly ended up locked away in museums, is now back at the center of foreign travel in Korea. Its revival has a few clear driving forces.

(1) Free admission to the five major palaces while wearing hanbok — the decisive factor

Under a policy in effect since 2013, wearing hanbok grants free admission to Joseon's five major palaces (Gyeongbokgung, Changdeokgung, Changgyeonggung, Deoksugung, and Gyeonghuigung). It's an official guideline from the Korea Heritage Service (formerly the Cultural Heritage Administration), and since its purpose is "popularizing and globalizing hanbok," it applies regardless of whether you're Korean or a foreigner. This simple benefit — "wear hanbok, enter the palace for free" — sparked a boom in hanbok rental shops in front of the palaces.

(2) The hanbok rental boom

Around Gyeongbokgung, Bukchon, Insadong, and the Jeonju Hanok Village, hanbok rental shops are packed close together. They stock everything from traditional hanbok to king and queen costumes, fusion hanbok, and hair ornaments and norigae, and the standard route has become: rent an outfit, then head out to take "photos of a lifetime" in the palace and hanok alleys. It started aimed at foreigners, but now Korean teens and twenty-somethings have joined in, and business is booming. The result is the number seen earlier — 2.07 million palace hanbok visitors in 2025 — a symbol of K-tourism shifting from "sightseeing" to "wearing travel."

(3) Everyday hanbok and fusion hanbok

To ease the discomforts of traditional hanbok, saenghwal hanbok (everyday/modern hanbok) — reinterpreted as comfortable, washable daily wear in denim and cotton — has taken hold. It keeps the lines of the jeogori and chima but swaps silk and starching for denim, lace, and pastels. More people now wear it like everyday clothes on university campuses and in Seochon cafes.

(4) K-pop Hallyu hanbok

The global engine of hanbok's revival is K-pop. BTS has reworked the dopo and gat into modern looks in music videos and performances, and reports emerged that sales at a modern hanbok brand surged right after Blackpink's "How You Like That" (2020) stage performance. Fans overseas began buying modern hanbok in the US, Europe, and Southeast Asia, and the recognition that "that outfit is hanbok" quickly took hold. Efforts to promote hanbok, like Hanbok Day (October 21), established in 1996, are now spreading worldwide riding on K-pop.

❓ Foreigners Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • Can foreigners wear hanbok and enter the palace for free? — Yes. The purpose of the system is ‘globalization of hanbok’, so it is free for all Hanbok wearers regardless of whether they are Koreans or foreigners. In general, not only traditional hanbok but also hanbok from rental stores are accepted (recommended to check detailed guidelines for each palace).
  • Can men also rent hanbok? — Of course. For men, jeogori + pants + durumagi (or king/mover costume), couple/family sets are also popular.
  • Wouldn’t Hanbok look like cosplay? — Not at all. Wearing Hanbok in front of the palace is a very natural and welcomed cultural experience in Korea. In fact, Koreans sometimes ask to take pictures with them.
  • Is fusion hanbok free admission? — Traditional hanbok is generally accepted, but excessively modified hanbok may vary depending on palace guidelines. It is safe to ask the rental store for “free entrance to the palace.”
  • Which season is best for Hanbok? — Available in all four seasons. The background of the palace during spring flowers and fall foliage season is especially pretty, and in winter, wearing a durumagi creates a warm and cozy atmosphere.
