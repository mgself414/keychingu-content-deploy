Korean superstitions and taboo culture — Activities that are done inadvertently in Korea and then end up making you feel bad

Number 4·Red Name·Fan Death Theory·Exam Seaweed Soup·Shoe Gift·Two Hands Etiquette — Korean superstitions and taboos explainer for foreigners. “Why is it taboo?” + “What can I do instead?”

There is one thing that people preparing for a trip to Korea are surprisingly unaware of. In Korea, there are a few actions that foreigners do without malice, but the other person stiffens slightly. This can be done by writing a friend's name in red pen, giving a nice pair of shoes as a favor, or handing a business card to an adult with one hand. Koreans usually laugh it off, but if you know in advance and avoid it, it can give the impression that “this person knows a bit about Korean culture”. This article explains such superstitions and taboos not as “scary rules,” but in a practical way: Why they came into existence, how seriously modern Koreans take them, and what foreigners need to be careful of. Korean culture explainer series 8th edition.

First things first: A lot of what you see here is superstition. Many modern Koreans either take it lightly or keep it because “it’s just a custom.” It’s not because they truly believe in it, but it’s closer to the side of “I don’t need to cause trouble, so I don’t do it.” On the other hand, there are also things that are not superstitions but real manners that are still alive, such as the hands-on-hand etiquette (two hands) - they are marked separately below.

⚠️ 3 things foreigners often misunderstand

1. "All Koreans are superstitious" ❌ — Most treat it as half-joking, half-custom. I don't care about red pens or the 4th floor, but things like nail clipping and whistling at night are almost jokes these days. No need to be too nervous — just dodging a few is enough.

2. "Superstition and etiquette are the same thing" ❌ — Handing with both hands is not a superstition, but a real manner that is still used today (especially for superiors). On the other hand, the fan death theory is a scientifically incorrect superstition. You just have to differentiate between the two and accept them.

3. "If you make a mistake, you'll be in big trouble" ❌ — Koreans rarely get angry when foreigners make mistakes without knowing. Rather, I just say, “You might not know because you’re a foreigner” and move on. What is more important than apologizing is knowing what to do next time.

① Do not write your name in red — the most famous taboo

In Korea, writing people's names in red is the most widely known taboo. Even something as trivial as writing a friend's name in a notebook with a red pen can make the viewer cringe.

  • Why? In Korea, red has been traditionally associated with death. This is because of the custom of writing the name of the deceased on memorial tablets at ancestral rites, obituaries, and funeral wreaths in red. So a perception arose that “writing the name of a living person in red is unlucky.”
  • One theory of origin: There is also a story that during the Korean War, the names of the fallen were written in red to distinguish them from the survivors (one of several theories — not a confirmed orthodox theory).
  • Modern Sense: Few people truly believe they will “die,” but the sentiment “I don’t do it because it feels bad” is still strong. When writing someone's name on official documents, guest books, or congratulatory cards, use a black or blue pen.
  • Exception: The red ink on your seal (seal) is okay — this is a separate tradition as it is a substitute for a signature.

✅ Practical tip for foreigners: If you need to write someone's name, just use a black or blue pen. That alone is enough to avoid a major misstep.

② Gift taboos — shoes, knives, scissors, handkerchiefs

A gift given with good intentions can be read as sending the exact opposite message. That said, this one is often shrugged off too, depending on the relationship.

  • Shoes 👟 — from the association that 'putting on new shoes means walking away,' there's a superstition that gifting shoes to a romantic partner (or someone you're seeing) means they'll leave you. If you really want to give shoes anyway, there's a folk workaround: have the recipient symbolically pay a small amount (like a coin) to 'buy' them from you, which is said to ward off the bad luck.
  • Knives and scissors ✂️ — read as meaning 'cutting the relationship,' so they're avoided as gifts.
  • Handkerchiefs — sometimes avoided as gifts too, from the association with sadness and farewells (wiping away tears).
  • Sets of four — giving a gift in a set of four is avoided (4 = death, see ④ below).

✅ Practical tip for foreigners: safe gift choices are snacks, desserts, fruit gift sets, cosmetics, or nice tea/coffee. And when writing a name card (see ①), no red pen.

③ Giving and receiving with both hands — not a superstition, but 'real etiquette'

Unlike the items above, this one is not a superstition but a manner that's still very much alive today. It's also the point foreigners most often miss — and the one that earns the most points when done right.

  • The rule: with elders, people you're meeting for the first time, or in formal settings, use both hands when giving or receiving an object (a business card, gift, glass, or card).
  • How to actually do it: hold the object with one hand (usually the right), and lightly support that arm's wrist or forearm with the other hand. The overall feeling should be of offering something respectfully with both hands.
  • Especially at drinking gatherings: receive a glass poured by an elder with both hands, and pour for an elder with both hands too. (Turning your head slightly to the side while drinking is also considered polite.)
  • Why? In Confucian culture, this is a hand gesture that expresses respect for the other person. Handing something over carelessly with one hand can come across as 'insincere' or 'rude.'

✅ Practical tip for foreigners: handing over a card at a cafe, receiving a glass at a restaurant, receiving someone's business card — use both hands. It's the fastest way for a Korean person to register you as 'polite.'

④ The number 4 — 'neok sa' sounds like 'jugeul sa'

In Korea (and East Asia broadly), there's a tendency to avoid the number 4.

  • Why? In Hanja, the pronunciation of neok sa (넉 사, 四, 'four') is the same as jugeul sa (죽을 사, 死, 'to die'). It's a kind of homophone taboo.
  • Where you'll see it: hospitals, apartment buildings, and elevators sometimes label the 4th floor as 'F', or skip rooms numbered 4, 14, or 24. If you see an F instead of a 4 on an elevator button, this is why.
  • Modern sense: similar in spirit to the Western fear of the number 13. It's less a genuine fear and more a custom of 'there's no need to use 4, so we avoid it.' People get only mildly concerned when a 4 shows up in a room number or a gift count.

There's absolutely no issue if a foreigner is assigned a hotel room on the 4th floor — people who care about it simply choose the F floor or another room on their own.

⑤ The electric fan death theory — a 'modern superstition' taken seriously only in Korea

The superstition foreigners find most fascinating: the belief that 'sleeping in a closed room with an electric fan running can kill you.'

  • The claim: that a fan draws out oxygen and/or lowers body temperature, creating a risk of suffocation or hypothermia in an enclosed space.
  • The scientific fact: it's been debunked as false. There's no evidence that an electric fan can cause death in an ordinary home setting.
  • Why it spread: concern over electric fans dates back to newspaper articles from the 1920s warning about the dangers of new technology, and the media kept reproducing the claim for decades afterward. There's also a theory that the government promoted it during the 1970s energy crisis to cut electricity use (one theory among several — not confirmed).
  • Modern sense: even so, quite a few people still take it seriously. It's often cited as the reason Korean electric fans commonly come with a built-in timer. Foreigners don't need to insist 'that's not actually true' — it's fine just to know it as a piece of culture.

⑥ Exam luck superstitions — no seaweed soup, yeot and chapssaltteok are fine

Korea's exam culture comes with food superstitions firmly attached. They stand out even more during Suneung (the College Scholastic Ability Test) season.

  • No seaweed soup 🥣 — on the morning of an exam, people don't eat miyeokguk (미역국, seaweed soup). Seaweed is slippery, and that creates the association of 'slipping (and failing) on the exam.' The twist: the same seaweed soup that's a must-eat on birthdays becomes a forbidden food on exam day.
  • Yeot and chapssaltteok are fine 🍬 — by contrast, sticky, well-clinging yeot (엿) and chapssaltteok (찹쌀떡) are lucky foods. The Korean word butda (붙다) carries two meanings — to stick (cling tightly) and to pass (an exam) — so people gift them to test-takers with the wish 'stick to that exam and pass.'
  • Fork as a gift 🍴 — a fork is sometimes given as a pun-based cheer, meaning 'spear/pick out the right answer.'
  • Magpie vs. crow 🐦 — a kkachi (까치, magpie)'s call is considered a good omen meaning a welcome guest is coming, while the kkamagwi (까마귀, crow) has long been seen as a bad omen because of its black color and cry. (One explanation is that the Hanja for crow, 烏, sounds similar to 惡, meaning 'bad.') That said, the crow is also a symbol of the sun and the sacred — as with the three-legged crow (samjogo) of Goguryeo — so it isn't universally a bad bird.
  • A dwaejikkum (돼지꿈, pig dream) is the classic lucky dream 🐷 — dreaming of a pig is considered a sign of wealth luck. It's common for people who have a pig dream to buy a lottery ticket for fun.

✅ A note for foreigners: if a Korean friend has an exam coming up, try gifting yeot, chapssaltteok, or chocolate. It's the perfect 'stick and pass' cheer — just avoid seaweed soup that one day.

⑦ Small everyday taboos — nails, whistling, thresholds

Most of these are basically just jokes these days, but they're worth keeping lightly in mind around elders or in traditional spaces.

  • Cutting your fingernails/toenails at night ✂️ — An old superstition that cutting your fingernails at night will bring bad luck. (There is also an interpretation that the practical warning that was meant to prevent people from getting hurt while cutting in the dark in the days when there was no electricity has solidified into superstition.)
  • Whistling at night 🎵 — Superstition that whistling at night calls in ghosts (or snakes). Young people these days barely care.
  • Stepping on the threshold 🚪 — A taboo that says that stepping on the threshold will bring bad luck. It came from the view that the threshold was the border between this world and the underworld (there is also a theory that it originated during the Mongol invasion). In hanoks, old houses, and adult homes, it is considered polite to step over the threshold without stepping on it.
  • shakes leg 🦵 — Adults don't like it, saying, "It will lead to good fortune (wealth)." Half superstition, half manners.

Even if a foreigner makes a mistake because he or she does not know these items, it is hardly a problem. It is enough to be slightly conscious of them only when going to a traditional space with an adult.

⑧ Why did this superstition arise? — Three roots

Most Korean superstitions are not random but have their own logic. It is also the most interesting point for foreigners.

1. Chinese character homonyms (pun): The biggest axis. 4(四)=death, seaweed(slip)=slip on test, stick=pass — The overlapping pronunciation and meaning are the difference between good and bad. It is a way of thinking unique to Chinese culture where meaning is transferred through sound.

2. Confucian order and etiquette: Having both hands, respecting elders, being polite at the doorstep, etc. are not superstitions, but Confucian manners that distinguish between upper and lower, inside and outside are firmly established in daily life.

3. Traces of history and practicality: These are cases where real situations from the past were dressed up as ‘superstitions’ over time, such as Red names (funeral markings) and Cutting nails at night (a safety warning in the days when there were no lights).

In other words, Korean superstitions are close to cultural fossils that are a mixture of language play + Confucian etiquette + wisdom of old life. Rather than being “illogical,” it’s “understandable if you know why.”

❓ Foreigners Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ — When You Make a Mistake)

  • I wrote my friend’s name with a red pen without realizing it. Is it a big deal? — Not at all. "Oh, red wasn't allowed? I didn't know." One word is enough. Koreans hardly care about these mistakes made by foreigners.
  • I gave the object to the adult with one hand. — From now on, you can use two hands. One mistake doesn't make you a rude person. The very act of being conscious gives a good impression.
  • The hotel room is on the 4th floor/room 4. Do I need to change it? — No. Only those who care will avoid it, there is no problem with foreigners staying on the 4th floor.
  • Do Koreans really believe in these superstitions? — Each person is different. Many people follow the red name, number 4, and two-hand etiquette, but fans, fingernails, and whistles are taken lightly or viewed as jokes. There are more people who keep it “because it’s a custom” rather than “because they believe in it”.
  • You chose the wrong gift (shoes/knife). — If the recipient is the type who cares, you can solve it with a light trick joke, selling it for a coin. Most people just laugh it off.
