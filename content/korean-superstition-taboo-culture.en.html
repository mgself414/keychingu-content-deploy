Korean Superstitions & Taboos — Things That Quietly Make the Room Go Cold in Korea

> Number 4 (숫자 4) · Red Names (빨간 이름) · Fan Death (선풍기 사망설) · Seaweed Soup (미역국) for Exams · Gifting Shoes (신발 선물) · Two-Handed Etiquette (두 손 수수 예법) — A Korean superstition and taboo explainer for foreigners. Covers "why it's taboo" and "what to do instead."

There's one thing many people preparing for a trip to Korea surprisingly don't know: there are a few actions foreigners might do innocently that can make Koreans subtly stiffen up. These include writing a friend's name in red pen, kindly gifting a nice pair of shoes, or handing a business card to an elder with one hand. Koreans usually laugh it off, but knowing and avoiding these actions beforehand can give the impression that "this person knows a bit about Korean culture." This article explains such superstitions and taboos not as "scary rules," but practically, covering why they originated, how seriously modern Koreans take them, and what foreigners only need to be careful about. This is the 8th installment of the Korean Culture Explainer series.

> First, a note: A significant portion of what's presented here is superstition (미신). Most modern Koreans treat them lightly or observe them "just because it's customary." It's less about genuinely believing and more about "not wanting to create unnecessary friction." Conversely, some, like two-handed etiquette (두 손 수수 예법), are not superstitions but genuine manners (예법) that are still very much alive — these are marked below.

⚠️ 3 Common Misconceptions by Foreigners

1. "All Koreans believe in superstitions" ❌ — Most treat them as half-joke, half-custom. Things like red pens (빨간 펜) or the 4th floor (4층) are given some thought, but clipping nails (손톱 깎기) or whistling (휘파람) at night are mostly jokes now. No need to be overly tense — avoiding just a few is enough.

2. "Superstitions and etiquette are the same" ❌ — Giving things with two hands (두 손으로 건네기) is not a superstition, but a genuine manner (매너) that is still valid (especially towards elders). Conversely, the fan death (선풍기 사망설) theory is a scientifically incorrect superstition. It's good to distinguish between the two.

3. "Making a mistake is a big deal" ❌ — Koreans rarely get angry at mistakes made by foreigners out of ignorance. Instead, they usually say, "You're a foreigner, so you wouldn't know," and let it slide. What's important is to know for next time, rather than just apologizing.

① Don't Write Names in Red — The Most Famous Taboo

Writing a person's name in red (빨간색으로 쓰는 것) is the most widely known taboo in Korea. Even a small act like writing a friend's name in red pen (빨간 펜) in their notebook (노트) can make onlookers flinch.

  • Why? In Korea, red (빨간색) has traditionally been associated with death. This stems from the custom of writing the names of the deceased on ancestral tablets (위패) for ancestral rites (제사) or on death notices (부고) and funeral wreaths (장례 화환) in red. This created the perception that "it's ominous to write the name of a living person in red."
  • One theory of origin: There's a story that during the Korean War, the names of fallen soldiers were written in red to distinguish them from survivors (one of several theories — not a confirmed fact).
  • Modern sentiment: While almost no one genuinely believes someone will "die," the sentiment of "it's unpleasant, so I don't do it" is still strong. When writing someone's name on official documents, guestbooks, or congratulatory cards, use black or blue pens.
  • Exception: The red ink pad (붉은 인주) for your personal seal (인감) is fine — this is a traditional alternative to a signature and is separate.

> ✅ Practical Tip for Foreigners: If you need to write a name, just use a black or blue pen. Observing this one rule will help you avoid a major faux pas.

② Gift Taboos — Shoes (신발), Knives (칼), Scissors (가위), Handkerchiefs (손수건)

A gift given with good intentions can be interpreted with the opposite message. However, this also often gets overlooked depending on the recipient and relationship.

  • Shoes (신발) 👟 — Based on the association that "new shoes (새 신발) mean leaving far away," there's a superstition that gifting shoes (신발) to a lover or someone you're dating (썸) means they will leave you. There's also a belief that if you really want to give shoes, the recipient can symbolically pay a small amount of money (e.g., a coin) to 'buy' them, which acts as a charm to ward off bad luck (액땜).
  • Knives (칼) / Scissors (가위) ✂️ — These are avoided as gifts because they are interpreted as "cutting off relationships."
  • Handkerchiefs (손수건) — They are sometimes avoided as gifts due to their association with sadness and farewells (wiping away tears).
  • Sets of 4 (숫자 4) — Avoid giving gifts in sets of four (4개 묶음) (4 = 死, see ④ below).

> ✅ Practical Tip for Foreigners: Safe gifts include snacks (간식), desserts (디저트), fruit gift sets (과일 선물세트), cosmetics (화장품), or high-quality tea/coffee. When writing a name card (①), avoid red pens.

③ Giving and Receiving with Two Hands (두 손으로 주고받기) — This is Real Etiquette, Not Superstition

Unlike the items above, this is not a superstition but a strongly observed manner (매너) that is still alive today. It's also the point that foreigners most frequently miss and can most effectively earn them points.

  • Rule: When giving or receiving an item (business card (명함), gift (선물), cup (잔), card (카드)) to an elder, someone you've just met, or in a formal setting, do so with two hands (두 손으로).
  • Practical Action: Hold the item with one hand (usually the right hand), and lightly support the wrist or forearm of that hand with your other hand. The idea is to convey a respectful, two-handed gesture.
  • Especially at drinking parties (술자리): When an elder pours you a drink, receive the glass with two hands (두 손으로 받고), and when pouring for an elder, also use two hands. (Turning your head slightly to the side when drinking is also polite.)
  • Why? In Confucian culture (유교 문화), this expresses respect (존중) for the other person through hand gestures. Casually handing something with one hand can be interpreted as "lacking sincerity (성의 없다)" or "disrespectful (무례하다)."

> ✅ Practical Tip for Foreigners: When handing a card at a cafe, receiving a glass at a restaurant, or taking a business card from someone — use two hands (두 손). This is the action that Koreans most quickly perceive as "polite (예의 바르다)."

④ Number 4 (숫자 4) — 'Sa (四)' for Four Sounds Like 'Sa (死)' for Death

In Korea (and East Asia in general), there is a tendency to avoid the number 4 (숫자 4).

  • Why? The pronunciation of the Chinese character for four (넉 사, 四) is the same as the character for death (죽을 사, 死). It's a type of homophone taboo.
  • Where you'll see it: In hospitals (병원), apartments (아파트), and elevators (엘리베이터), the 4th floor (4층) might be labeled 'F', or rooms 4, 14, 24 might be avoided. If you see 'F' instead of '4' on an elevator button, this is why.
  • Modern sentiment: Similar to the Western 'fear of 13'. It's less about serious fear and more about the custom of "there's no reason to use 4, so we avoid it." People might subtly care if the number 4 is involved in room numbers (호실) or gift quantities (선물 개수).

> If a foreigner is assigned a 4th-floor hotel room, there's absolutely no problem — those who care simply choose an 'F' floor or a different room number.

⑤ Fan Death (선풍기 사망설) — A 'Modern Superstition' Taken Seriously Only in Korea

This is the superstition that foreigners find most peculiar. It's the belief that "you can die if you sleep with a fan (선풍기) on in a closed room (문 닫힌 방)."

  • Claim: The fan (선풍기) supposedly sucks up oxygen (산소) or lowers body temperature (체온), leading to suffocation (질식) or hypothermia (저체온) in a sealed space.
  • Scientific fact: It has been conclusively disproven. There is no evidence that a fan (선풍기) can cause death in a typical home environment.
  • Why it spread: Concerns about electric fans (전기 선풍기) date back to newspaper articles (신문 기사) in the 1920s (warning about new technology) and were subsequently reproduced by the media for a long time. There's also a theory that the government encouraged it during the 1970s energy crisis to reduce electricity consumption (a theory — not confirmed).
  • Modern sentiment: Nevertheless, a significant number of people still genuinely believe it. It's often cited as the reason why Korean fans (선풍기) commonly have timers (타이머). Foreigners don't need to argue, "That's not true" — just be aware of it as a cultural quirk.

⑥ Exam (시험) and Fortune (길흉) Superstitions — No Seaweed Soup (미역국), Yes Taffy (엿) & Rice Cakes (찹쌀떡)

Korea's exam culture (시험 문화) has strong food superstitions attached to it. This becomes even more pronounced during the Suneung (수능, College Scholastic Ability Test) season.

  • No Seaweed Soup (미역국 금지) 🥣 — On the morning of an exam (시험), people don't eat seaweed soup (미역국). This is due to the association that seaweed (미역) is slippery (미끌미끌해), which means "slipping (미끄러진다) on the exam (시험)" (failing). The key point is that seaweed soup (미역국), a must-have for birthdays (생일), becomes taboo on exam days.
  • Taffy (엿) & Glutinous Rice Cakes (찹쌀떡) OK 🍬 — Conversely, sticky (끈적하게 잘 붙는) taffy (엿) and glutinous rice cakes (찹쌀떡) are lucky foods. The Korean word "butda (붙다)" has two meanings: to stick (끈끈하게 달라붙다) and to pass (an exam) (시험에 합격하다). So, these are gifted to students (수험생) as encouragement, meaning "stick to the exam (시험에 착 붙어라)" (pass with flying colors).
  • Gifting Forks (포크 선물) 🍴 — Forks (포크) are sometimes given as gifts with the meaning of "pinpointing the correct answers" (a pun-based encouragement).
  • Magpie (까치) vs. Crow (까마귀) 🐦 — A magpie (까치) crying is considered a good omen (길조), signifying a welcome guest is coming. A crow (까마귀), due to its black color and caw, has been regarded as a bad omen (흉조). (The character for crow (까마귀 오, 烏) having a similar pronunciation to the character for bad (나쁠 오, 惡) is also cited as a reason.) However, crows (까마귀) were also symbols of the sun and divinity, like the Three-Legged Crow (삼족오) of Goguryeo (고구려), so they are not always considered bad birds.
  • Pig Dream (돼지꿈) = Lucky Dream (길몽) 🐷 — If a pig (돼지) appears in a dream, it's considered a representative lucky dream (길몽) for wealth (재물운). It's common to buy lottery tickets (복권) for fun after having a pig dream (돼지꿈).

> ✅ Foreigner Memo: If your Korean friend (한국인 친구) has an exam (시험) coming up, try gifting them taffy (엿), glutinous rice cakes (찹쌀떡), or chocolate (초콜릿). They're perfect for encouraging them to "stick (붙어라)" to it — just avoid seaweed soup (미역국) on that day.

⑦ Small Everyday Taboos — Nails (손톱), Whistling (휘파람), Threshold (문지방)

Most of these are almost at the level of jokes nowadays, but it's good to keep them in mind lightly when in front of elders (어른들) or in traditional spaces (전통 공간).

  • Clipping Nails (손톱·발톱 깎기) at Night ✂️ — An old superstition that bad luck comes if you clip your nails (손톱) at night. (Some interpret this as a practical warning to prevent injuries from clipping in the dark before electricity, which then solidified into a superstition.)
  • Whistling (휘파람 불기) at Night 🎵 — A superstition that whistling (휘파람) at night summons ghosts (귀신) (or snakes). Young people (젊은 층) nowadays barely pay attention to this.
  • Stepping on the Threshold (문지방 밟기) 🚪 — A taboo that stepping on the threshold (문지방) brings bad luck. This originated from viewing the threshold (문지방) as the boundary between this world and the afterlife (이승과 저승의 경계) (there's also a theory of its origin during the Mongol invasions). In a traditional Korean house (한옥), old traditional house (고택), or an elder's home (어른 댁), it's considered polite to step over the threshold (문지방) rather than on it.
  • Shaking Legs (다리 떨기) 🦵 — Elders (어른들) dislike this, saying "good fortune (복, wealth) will leave (나간다)." It's half superstition, half manner.

> These items are unlikely to cause any problems if a foreigner makes a mistake out of ignorance. Just be slightly mindful when visiting traditional spaces (전통 공간) with elders (어른).

⑧ Why These Superstitions Arose — Three Roots

Most Korean superstitions (한국 미신) are not random but have their own logic. This is also one of the most interesting points for foreigners.

1. Chinese Character Homophones (한자 동음이의어) (Wordplay): The biggest factor. 4 (四) = Death (死), seaweed (미역, slippery) = slipping on an exam (시험 미끄러짐), to stick (붙다) = to pass (합격) — good or bad fortune is determined by overlapping pronunciations and meanings. This is a unique way of thinking in Chinese character cultures where meaning is transferred through sound.

2. Confucian Order and Etiquette (유교적 질서·예법): Two-handed giving/receiving (두 손 수수), respect for elders (어른 공경), and threshold etiquette (문지방 예의) are less superstitions and more Confucian manners (유교 매너) that distinguish between superiors/inferiors and inside/outside, which have become ingrained in daily life.

3. Traces of History and Practicality (역사·실용의 흔적): Cases like red names (빨간 이름) (funeral markings) and clipping nails (손톱 깎기) at night (safety warning in times without lighting) are instances where real situations from the past have taken on the guise of 'superstition' over time.

In essence, Korean superstitions (한국 미신) are like cultural fossils, a mix of wordplay (언어유희) + Confucian etiquette (유교 예법) + old wisdom (옛 생활의 지혜). Rather than being "illogical," they are more "understandable once you know why."

❓ Frequently Asked Questions for Foreigners (FAQ — When You Make a Mistake)

  • I accidentally wrote my friend's name in red pen (빨간 펜). Is it a big deal? — Not at all. A simple "Oh, red (빨간색) isn't allowed, is it? I didn't know." is enough. Koreans (한국인) are rarely bothered by such mistakes from foreigners.
  • I gave an elder (어른) something with one hand. — Just use two hands (두 손) next time. One mistake won't make you a rude person. The act of being mindful itself leaves a good impression.
  • My hotel room is on the 4th floor (4층) / room 4 (4호). Should I change it? — No. Only those who care avoid it, and there's no problem at all for a foreigner to stay on the 4th floor (4층).
  • Do Koreans (한국인) really believe these superstitions? — It varies from person to person. Many observe red names (빨간 이름), number 4 (숫자 4), and two-handed etiquette (두 손 예법), while fan death (선풍기), nails (손톱), and whistling (휘파람) are taken lightly or as jokes. Many observe them more out of "custom (관습)" than "belief (믿어서)."
  • I chose the wrong gift (shoes (신발) / knife (칼)). — If the recipient is the type to care, you can lighten the mood with a joke about a small charm to ward off bad luck (액땜), like receiving a coin (동전 하나 받고) and 'selling' it. Most people will just laugh it off.
