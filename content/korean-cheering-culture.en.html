Korean Cheering & Singalong Culture — Why a Korean Crowd Roars as One

> KBO Cheer Captains, Player-Specific Cheering Songs, Thunder Sticks, Singalongs, Light Sticks, Chimaek, Heung (興) — A Korean cheering culture explainer for foreigners. Not just "noisy stadiums," but "why, what, and how."

Foreigners visiting a Korean baseball stadium for the first time are almost always shocked. Whether the score is 0:0 or their team is losing, the singing, chanting, and clapping in the stands never stop for all 9 innings. On a stage, one cheer captain (응원단장) blows a whistle, leading thousands of people, and whenever a new batter steps up, a new cheering song (응원가) specifically for that player rings out. It's the same at concert venues. The moment a K-pop singer extends the microphone to the audience, tens of thousands of people sing along (떼창) to the entire lyrics. Foreigners often dismiss this collective fervor as "Koreans are just really excited," but behind it lies a unique Korean 'culture of shouting together' that permeates baseball, soccer, and concerts. This article isn't about "which stadium is good" (→ separate topic), but rather where this collective passion comes from and how foreigners can join in. ⚠️ 3 Common Misconceptions Foreigners Have

1. "They only cheer when their team is winning, right?" ❌ — KBO (Korean Baseball Organization) cheering is unrelated to the score or outcome. Whether it's 0:0 or losing by 5 runs, the cheer captain (응원단장) leads the cheering for all 9 innings. While MLB (Major League Baseball) cheering often subsides during pitching changes, Korean baseball stadiums are structured so that cheering never stops throughout the game. Even the LA Dodgers manager called this culture "very refreshing."

2. "Korean-style cheering must be hard to follow." ❌ — On the contrary, it's arguably the easiest cheering in the world to join. The cheer captain (응원단장) on stage demonstrates the moves in real-time, and the cheering songs (응원가) have short, repetitive chorus structures, so foreigners can pick it up and bang their thunder sticks (막대풍선) within 1-2 innings. You don't even need to know the lyrics; just follow the hand movements.

3. "Isn't this just loud?" ❌ — It's not chaotic noise but a highly organized collective performance. Each team has an official cheering squad, unique colored thunder sticks (막대풍선), and player-specific cheering songs (응원가), with thousands moving in unison. At its root is a unique Korean sentiment of 'creating joyous energy together' (see ⑤ below).

① What is 'Korean-Style Cheering'? — The Audience as Players

The core of Korean cheering can be summarized in one word: 'participation'. While sports fans in other countries primarily 'watch and react,' Korean spectators are active participants who create the atmosphere from beginning to end.

  • There's a leader: At baseball stadiums, a cheer captain (응원단장) and cheerleaders, and at concerts, fan chant guides from the fandom, direct the cheering of thousands to tens of thousands of people in one direction.
  • There are rules: Player-specific cheering songs (응원가), team-specific light sticks (응원봉) and thunder sticks (막대풍선), and set chants ("Dae~hanminguk" with five claps, etc.) — it's not spontaneous but a shared 'script'.
  • It goes on until the end: It doesn't stop even if the game's outcome is clear. The cheering itself is the purpose.

Therefore, the impression foreigners get at a Korean stadium is not 'Team A vs. Team B game' but rather 'a feeling of entering a massive singalong (떼창) festival'. VisitKorea's official content also describes watching KBO (Korean Baseball Organization) as "feeling like participating in a huge festival, even if you're not interested in baseball."

② Baseball Cheering (KBO) — A 'Cheering Concert' Unlike Anywhere Else

The pinnacle of Korean cheering culture and the most recommended entry point for foreigners is professional baseball (KBO League, KBO 리그, launched in 1982). In 2024, the season saw approximately 10.88 million spectators, exceeding 10 million for the first time in KBO history — a testament to how deeply embedded this culture is in daily life.

Cheer Captain (응원단장) & Cheerleaders (치어리더) — On stage, a cheer captain (응원단장) leads the crowd for all 9 innings with a whistle and calls, while cheerleaders (치어리더) perform choreography in sync with these signals. Neither Major League Baseball nor Japanese baseball has this exact structure; it's unique to Korea. (In the early days after its launch in 1982, cheering was spontaneous clapping and singing, with organized official cheering squads establishing themselves after the 1990s.)

★ Player-Specific Cheering Songs (선수별 응원가) — The most distinctive scene in Korean baseball. Each individual batter has their own dedicated cheering song (응원가). The moment a player steps up to the plate, their song plays, and the entire crowd sings along (떼창), incorporating their name. Many songs are popular K-pop or Western songs with modified lyrics to be easy to sing along to. (The introduction of player-specific cheering songs at the team level is known to have become widespread in the late 2000s, notably with the Lotte Giants.) This culture has even reached overseas; when former KBO foreign batter Eric Thames moved to Major League Baseball in 2017, his KBO cheering song followed him, becoming a topic of discussion among American fans.

Thunder Sticks (막대풍선) — Long, inflatable cheering tools that make noise when hit together. The LG Twins (LG 트윈스) are recorded as the first in the world to introduce them during the 1994 Korean Series. They subsequently spread to all teams, with each team using its unique color (red, blue, white, yellow, etc.). This creates the spectacular sight of the stands waving in a single color.

Chimaek (치맥) — 'Chicken + Beer'. An inseparable combination from baseball stadiums. Spectators order fried chicken to their seats and enjoy it while cheering, and some stadiums even allow outside fried chicken to be brought in. This is why foreign guides often cite "chimaek (치맥) at a baseball stadium = essential Korean experience."

③ Soccer Street Cheering — The 'Plaza Singalong' Created by 2002

If baseball stadiums represent cheering 'inside the venue,' soccer symbolizes cheering that has 'spilled out into the streets'. The origin of this is the 2002 Korea-Japan World Cup.

  • In 2002, Korea reached the World Cup semifinals (4강). This was the first and, to date, only time an Asian team achieved this. They defeated Italy in the Round of 16 and Spain in the quarterfinals, then lost 0:1 to Germany in the semifinals. They finished the tournament in 4th place after losing to Turkey in the 3rd/4th place match. (The coach was Guus Hiddink from the Netherlands.)
  • Red Devils (붉은악마) — The official supporters' group for the Korean national soccer team. Organized in the late 1990s, it exploded into a nationwide phenomenon during the 2002 World Cup. Cheering fans dressed in matching red T-shirts painted the plazas crimson.
  • Plaza Singalong (광장 떼창) — Street cheering (길거리 응원) occurred simultaneously across the country in places like Seoul Plaza (서울시청 앞 광장), Gwanghwamun (광화문), Sinchon (신촌), and Busan's Gwangalli (광안리). The cumulative number of participants during the tournament was estimated at approximately 7 million people (about 20% of the adult population). It was not just one city, but the entire nation singing along (떼창) simultaneously.
  • "Dae~hanminguk" (대~한민국) + Clapping — The rhythm of the "Dae~hanminguk" chant followed by five claps (clap-clap-clap-clap-clap). After 2002, this became the basic form of cheering for all Korean sports.

This experience wasn't a one-off. During the 2022 Qatar World Cup, approximately 33,000 people gathered in the Gwanghwamun (광화문) fan zone in freezing temperatures and early morning hours to watch the Korea-Brazil Round of 16 match. The formula of 'big game = gather in the plaza and cheer together' is a cultural legacy created in 2002.

④ Concert Singalongs (떼창) · K-pop Fandom — The Audience as the 'Second Stage'

Foreigners attending a K-pop concert for the first time are often surprised not by the stage, but by the audience seating. The audience isn't just passive spectators but moves as an integral part of the performance.

  • Singalong (떼창) — When a singer turns the microphone to the audience, tens of thousands of people sing along (떼창) to the entire lyrics. Moments where the entire audience harmonizes a ballad verse often become the highlight of the performance.
  • Fanchant (팬챈트) — A 'pre-arranged cheer' where fans collectively shout members' names or specific slogans at designated lyrical timings. Official guides are distributed by each group, and fans memorize them in advance. This makes it easy for newcomers to join in by following those next to them (multi-language guides also lower the barrier for foreigners).
  • Light Stick (응원봉) — Each fandom has its own uniquely designed light stick (응원봉) (e.g., BTS's 'Army Bomb'). Modern models connect via Bluetooth, allowing all light sticks in the venue to change colors and create waves in sync with the music. The audience section itself becomes a massive lighting performance.

The core here is the same: the audience is an active participant, blurring the lines between the stage and the seats — exactly the same DNA as baseball stadium singalongs (떼창).

⑤ Why Do Koreans Gather and Shout Together? — Heung (興) · Sinmyeong (신명) · Unity

This is the core of this article. The singalongs (떼창) at baseball, soccer, and concerts are merely the surface; beneath them lies a unique Korean sentiment.

Heung (興, heung) — A positive emotion that wells up during joyful and pleasant moments. It's a specific emotion forged within Korean culture that isn't fully captured by English words like 'excitement' or 'joy'. When heung (흥) rises, one can't sit still and is compelled to sing and dance — the collective joyous energy at baseball stadiums and concert venues is a direct manifestation of this heung (흥).

Sinmyeong (神明) — In cultural psychology, sinmyeong (신명) is described as "a positive emotion that wells up when damaged self-worth is restored." Interestingly, this is the opposite of han (한), another pillar of Korean sentiment (a feeling of deep-seated resentment or unresolved grief). Thus, the act of shouting and singing together is a collective release that transforms pent-up emotions into sinmyeong (신명). Korea has a tradition of collective rituals since ancient times, where people would eat, drink, sing, and dance together during ancestral rites, and many interpret modern cheering, noraebang (노래방, private singing room), and hoesik (회식, company dinner) as their descendants.

Jeong (情) · Woori (우리) — Korea has a language and sentiment that prioritizes 'woori (우리)' (our team, our country, our family) over 'me' (→ [#1135 Jeong (정) Culture](1135_jeong_culture.md)). The experience of shouting and dancing together itself becomes a channel for building jeong (정). Just like dining, noraebang (노래방), and hoesik (회식), cheering is ultimately a social mechanism for 'becoming one through togetherness'.

> One-line summary: Korean cheering is about 'creating heung (흥) together to become woori (우리)' — it's not about shouting to win, but rather becoming one in the very moment of shouting.

⑥ Foreigner Participation in Baseball Cheering — The Easiest 'Korean Experience'

Among the many forms of Korean cheering, the baseball stadium is the stage where foreigners can most easily and safely join in. There's almost no language barrier, it's easy to follow, and the atmosphere is friendly.

> Prices and operations are estimated based on 2026 and vary by stadium and game — confirmation before visiting is recommended.

  • Where: In Seoul, Jamsil Baseball Stadium (잠실야구장) (home to LG Twins and Doosan Bears, Jamsil Station (잠실역) on the subway, outdoor) and Gocheok Sky Dome (고척 스카이돔) (home to Kiwoom Heroes, Korea's first domed stadium opened in 2015, regardless of weather) offer good accessibility. The outfield cheering section (외야 응원석) has the highest cheering density and is best for the experience.
  • Tickets: Foreigners can book tickets using international credit cards on English-supported sites like Ticketlink Global or Interpark Global. On-site ticket booths also sell tickets with a passport, and weekday games are generally available for same-day purchase. Prices range from approximately ₩8,000 to 53,000 (~$6-40 / ~40-280 RMB / ~800-5,300 JPY) depending on the seat.
  • Cheering Tools: Thunder sticks (막대풍선) are received upon entry or purchased at stadium surroundings or convenience stores (편의점). Just follow the cheer captain's (응원단장) movements and bang them.
  • Chimaek (치맥): Enjoy chimaek (치맥) by having it delivered to your seat or buying it outside the stadium. However, domed stadiums (like Gocheok Sky Dome) may have restrictions on bringing in cans or alcoholic beverages, so check beforehand. Outdoor stadiums like Jamsil (잠실) are generally more lenient.
  • Etiquette: Home and away cheering sections are separated, so pick a team and sit in their cheering section to naturally blend into the atmosphere. Not knowing the lyrics is OK — just match the hand movements and thunder sticks (막대풍선), and you'll become a 'Korean baseball fan' within 1-2 innings.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) for Foreigners

  • Is it fun even if I don't know the baseball rules well? — Absolutely. For Korean baseball stadiums, the festival atmosphere of cheering (응원), singalongs (떼창), and chimaek (치맥) accounts for half the fun, more than the game itself. VisitKorea also states, "Even if you don't know baseball, it feels like participating in a huge festival."
  • Can I join in if I don't know the cheering song (응원가) lyrics? — Yes. The cheer captain (응원단장) demonstrates the movements in real-time, and the choruses are short and repetitive, so you can naturally join in just by following the hand movements and thunder sticks (막대풍선).
  • Will it be awkward if I go alone? — Korean baseball stadiums have an atmosphere where even strangers cheer together if they're on the same team, so it's enjoyable even alone. This is especially true in the outfield cheering section (외야 응원석).
  • How can I prepare for concert singalongs (떼창) and fanchants (팬챈트) in advance? — Each group's official fanchant guide is distributed via videos and fan cafes. Many multi-language guides are available for foreigners, so you can just learn the choruses beforehand.
  • Can I still see street cheering (길거리 응원) like in 2002? — Not regularly, but during major international events like the World Cup (월드컵) or Olympics (올림픽), fan zones open in places like Gwanghwamun (광화문), recreating street cheering (it happened in 2022). You can experience it if you time your visit right.
