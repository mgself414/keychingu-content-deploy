Korean public transportation etiquette culture — How to avoid being a ‘villain’ on the Korean subway

Conscious seating, quiet car interior, backpack manners, getting on and off the car, escalator, T-money — Explainer on Korean public transportation etiquette for foreigners. Not a ‘list of rules’ but ‘why, what, and how’.

There are two things that foreigners first notice when riding the Seoul subway for the first time. One is "Why is it so quiet?" — Even though it is a crowded train commuting to and from work, everyone is putting their cell phones on silent or wearing earphones, and hardly anyone is talking on the phone loudly. The other one is "Why is that seat empty?" — The other pink/colored seats at both ends of the compartment are empty even though people are standing there. In Korean public transportation, there are far more 'mutually agreed upon manners' than what is stipulated by law. And if you don't know the manners, your thoughtless actions (sitting in a reserved seat, talking on the phone loudly, carrying a large backpack on your back) will be seen as 'a nuisance villain' by those around you. This article is not about “which station is better” (→ location guide), but explains the thinking style behind manners (consideration, tact, public morality) and practice (T-money, transfers, boarding and alighting) from a foreigner’s perspective. ⚠️ 3 misunderstandings foreigners often make

1. "It's empty so you can sit down for a while" ❌ — The Korean rule is to leave the accessible seats and the pink pregnant women's seats at both ends of the compartment empty even if it is crowded. Instead of "sitting down and giving up when someone who needs it comes" like in other countries, don't sit down from the beginning. In particular, there is a strong perception that pregnant women's seats are "empty even if no one needs them."

2. "One phone call would be okay" ❌ — Quiet is the default** in Korean subways. Loud conversations, laughter, and phone calls are frowned upon. Calling itself is not prohibited, but it is good manners to keep it short and small. Most problems are resolved through text messages and earphone music.

3. "You can just wear a backpack" ❌ — If you get on a crowded subway with a large backpack on your back, you hit the person next to you every time you turn. When it's crowded, basic manners are to carry it in front of you or put it down at your feet. The large backpacks of foreign tourists are especially noticeable.

① Korean public transportation manners are a place where there are more ‘promises’ than laws

There are written laws on Korean subways and buses (e.g., ban on free riding), but what really governs the atmosphere are unofficial manners that citizens promise to each other. Most of the things like leaving empty seats for consideration or going quietly are tacit agreements with no provisions for punishment**, so they are not visible to foreigners.

  • There are two roots — consideration (giving in to each other) and 'not causing trouble (public morality)'. It is a sense of prioritizing the tranquility of the common space over my own convenience.
  • So, in Korea, a ‘passenger with good manners’ is an inconspicuous passenger. Someone who is quiet, doesn't take up space, and doesn't block the flow. On the other hand, a 'villain' is someone who stands out alone** who is noisy, blocks the road, and takes up a lot of space.

If you understand this sense, the rules below are not a list to memorize, but are grouped into one principle.

② Seats for consideration — reserved seats (seats for the disabled and pregnant women)

This is the most important and the part where foreigners make the most mistakes. There are two types of special seats at both ends of the Korean subway car.

  • Priority seating (교통약자석): Introduced when the Seoul subway opened in 1980 as "elderly/frail seats," renamed "seating for the mobility-vulnerable" (교통약자석) in 2005. These seats are for people who have difficulty moving — the elderly, people with disabilities, pregnant women, caregivers with infants/toddlers, and the injured. Usually at both ends of the car, marked with a different color.
  • Pregnant women's seat (임산부 배려석, pink): A separate pink seat, introduced on the Seoul subway in 2013 to solve the problem that pregnancy isn't always visible, so pregnant riders weren't offered a seat. The norm that took hold is to always leave it empty. A pregnant rider can pick up a pink badge at a station customer center to signal to those nearby, "I may need this seat."

Practical tip for foreigners: In Korea there's no fine or penalty for sitting in a priority seat (unlike some other countries). But not sitting is the social rule. Even if the regular seats are full, leave the priority seats and pregnant women's seats empty. Koreans themselves treat the two slightly differently — "give up a priority seat when someone who needs it appears" versus "leave the pregnant women's seat empty no matter what" — but for foreigners, the safe move is to avoid sitting in either.

③ The quiet car — phone calls and loud noise (a nation of quiet cars)

The hush of the Korean subway is one of the traits that impresses foreigners most. It isn't total silence — you still hear the train and the announcements — but it's noticeably quieter than subways in similarly sized cities abroad.

  • Phone calls: Not banned, but keep them short and quiet. Long, loud calls draw dirty looks. Most riders handle things through texting or earphone music instead.
  • Conversation: Even talking with a companion should be at a low volume. Avoid loud laughter or raised voices.
  • Leaking earphone audio: Even with earphones in, if the volume is loud enough to leak out, that's a nuisance too. Keep the volume moderate.
  • Quiet Car signage: Some lines and cars post signs asking riders to refrain from calls and loud conversation.

This quiet is a direct expression of public morality (공중도덕) — 'not encroaching on each other's space.' For a foreigner, the easy way to remember it is: "turn the volume down a notch here."

④ Backpacks and boarding — manners for space and flow

The two things you most often bump into (literally) on a crowded train.

Backpack manners

  • Keep a large bag on your back and you'll hit the person next to you every time you turn. When it's crowded, carry it in front of you, hold it in your hand, or set it down at your feet.
  • "Backpack to the front when crowded" is such an established norm in Korea it shows up in subway manner campaigns. Foreign tourists with big travel backpacks stand out in particular, so once the car fills up, swing it forward naturally.

Boarding manners — those getting off go first

  • The most basic rule of all on the Korean subway: when the doors open, you board only after everyone getting off has gotten off, since boarding and alighting share the same door.
  • Before the doors open, step back to the side of the door to clear a path for people getting off. Then board one at a time, in line.
  • Many platforms have queuing lines painted on the floor. Standing along them prevents a 'human traffic jam' in front of the doors.

Follow these two rules and the flow stays smooth even on a packed train — the person who blocks that flow is the 'villain.'

⑤ Escalators — from 'stand in one line' to 'stand in two lines' (a shift underway)

This is the part most likely to confuse foreigners, so let's be precise. Korean escalator culture is in transition right now.

  • The old norm (2000s–): Starting as a civic campaign in the late 1990s, "stand in one line" spread nationwide around the 2002 World Cup. Stand on the right, leave the left open for people walking or running — a habit that's still second nature for many people today.
  • The current recommendation (safety): Standing in one line came with a real cost — falls from walking or running, plus equipment failures caused by weight leaning to one side. So the government and the Korea Elevator Safety Agency began officially recommending "don't walk or run, hold the handrail and stand", and in 2026 began pushing 'stand in two lines' again (the first such push in 11 years). Safety data shows most serious escalator accidents are logged as 'rider error from walking or running.'

To be clear (avoiding confusion): This isn't "standing in one line was wrong" — it's that habit is still one line, while the official recommendation is shifting to two lines (stand, don't walk). Foreigners just need to remember one simple rule: "don't walk or run on the escalator, hold the handrail and stand" — either way, that's safe and mannered. If you're in a hurry, take the stairs next to it instead.

⑥ Transit cards and transfers in practice — one T-money card covers it all

As important as manners is how payment actually works — the part foreigners ask about most.

  • T-money card: A rechargeable transit card that works on the subway, buses, and even taxis. Buy the card itself for about 3,000 won at CU, GS25, 7-Eleven, and other convenience stores. Accepted in most of Korea.
  • Topping up: At convenience stores or station top-up machines. Note — top-ups are usually cash only (many machines don't accept credit/debit cards). Bring cash beforehand.
  • ★ Tag both when boarding and getting off: The core rule of Korean transit. Tap the card once boarding, once getting off. Skip the tap on exit and you lose the transfer discount, and the distance-based fare calculation gets thrown off too. Same on buses — always tag the reader when getting off.
  • Transfer discount: Tag with the same card and within 30 minutes of getting off (60 minutes between 9 PM and 7 AM), bus ↔ subway transfers are discounted. Up to 4 transfers (5 rides total) per fare. This is one reason Korean public transit is so cheap.
  • Options for foreigners: Short-term visitors can also consider the Climate Card (기후동행카드) — unlimited Seoul buses, subway, and Ttareungyi bike-share for a set period — or a foreigner-specific card like Mpass. If you'll be moving around heavily for a few days, a flat-rate pass can pay off.
  • Apps: Check subway routes and transfers ahead of time with a subway app (or Naver Map, Kakao Map) and you won't get lost. English supported.

⑦ Why so quiet and considerate — noonchi (눈치) and public morality (공중도덕)

Lastly, the ‘why’** that ties all these manners together.

  • Notice: Koreans read the atmosphere and other people’s states even without speaking (→ #1145). On the subway, I notice myself constantly looking, "Am I noisy? Am I taking up too much space? Does that person need a seat?" Leaving seats empty and lowering the volume are ultimately expressions of discernment.
  • Public morality (not causing inconvenience): Korean society considers not causing inconvenience to others in public spaces as an important virtue. It is a sense of prioritizing the tranquility of the shared space over my own freedom. The subway is the stage where that feeling is revealed most concisely.
  • Consideration: Giving up your seat to the weak (elderly, pregnant women, disabled people) is a social expectation rather than an explicit rule. So it works even without punishment.

In other words, the quietness, order, and consideration of the Korean subway are not due to fear of rules, but are the embodiment of a culture of reading each other (being aware of each other) and not causing trouble (public morality). When you know this, “Why is it like this?” changes to “Oh, that’s why.”

❓ Foreigners Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • Can you really never sit in a courtesy seat? — There is no punishment, but it is a Korean principle to leave seats empty even if all the economy seats are occupied. In particular, there is a strong perception of not sitting on the pink pregnant women seats even if they are empty. It is safer for foreigners not to sit.
  • What if I get an urgent call? — You can answer it, but short and small. If it seems like it will be a long time, it is good manners to get off at the next station and make a phone call, or send a text saying "Subway, call me later."
  • Can I walk up the escalator? — There are still many people who habitually walk, but for safety reasons, the recommendation is to ‘stand while using it’ (moving to two lines). If you are in a hurry, use the stairs next to you. At least not run.
  • Do I have to swipe my card when getting off? — Yes, absolutely. If you do not swipe your card, the transfer discount will disappear and additional fees may apply. Tag when getting off the bus.
  • Can I eat food? — Avoid food that has a strong smell or may spill in the car. Water and light drinks are fine.
  • Do I always have to carry my backpack in the front? — It doesn’t matter when it’s quiet. If there are a lot of people, you can turn it forward or put it under your feet — the key is 'consideration of space when crowded'.
