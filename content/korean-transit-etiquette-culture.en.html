Korean Public Transit Etiquette — How Not to Be "That Person" on the Seoul Subway

> Priority seats (배려석) · A quiet car · Backpack manners · Boarding and alighting · Escalators · T-money — A Korean public transit etiquette explainer for foreigners. Not a "list of rules," but the "why, what, and how."

The first thing a foreigner notices on the Seoul (서울) subway is two things. One is, "Why is it so quiet?" — Even on a packed rush-hour train, almost everyone is looking at a silenced phone or wearing earphones, and hardly anyone talks loudly on the phone. The other is, "Why is that seat empty?" — Pink and differently-colored seats at both ends of the car sit empty even while people stand. Korean public transit has far more "manners agreed upon by everyone" (서로 약속한 매너) than things written into law. And if you don't know those manners, something you do without a second thought — sitting in a priority seat (배려석), a loud phone call, a big backpack on your back — can make you look like a "nuisance villain" (민폐 빌런) to the people around you. This article isn't "which station is good" (that's a place guide); instead, it unpacks the mindset behind those manners (consideration, nunchi (눈치), public decency) and the practical side (T-money, transfers, boarding) (실전) at a foreigner's eye level. This is the 37th installment of our Korean Culture Explainer Series.

> Core: Just keep the three red ones (priority seats (배려석), quiet (조용), boarding/alighting (승하차)) and you'll avoid being "that person." The rest are bonus points toward being "the foreigner with sense."

⚠️ 3 Common Misunderstandings Foreigners Have

1. "It's empty, so a quick sit is fine, right?" ❌ — The vulnerable-passenger seats (교통약자석) at both ends of the car and the pink pregnancy priority seats (임산부 배려석) are, by Korean principle, kept empty (비워두는 것) even when it's crowded. Unlike in other countries — "sit, then give it up when someone who needs it shows up" — here you don't sit in the first place (처음부터 안 앉는다). The perception is especially strong for pregnancy priority seats: "leave it empty even if no one who needs it is in sight."

2. "Just one phone call should be okay, right?" ❌ — On the Korean subway, quiet is the default (조용함이 디폴트). Loud conversation, laughter, and phone calls draw disapproving looks. Calls aren't outright banned, but short and soft (짧고 작게) is the manner. Most people handle things with texts or earphone music.

3. "I can just keep my backpack on, right?" ❌ — If you wear a big backpack on your back on a packed train, you hit the person beside you every time you turn. When it's crowded, the basic manner is to wear it in front or set it at your feet (앞으로 메거나 발밑에 내려두는). A foreign tourist's big rucksack especially stands out.

① What Korean Transit Manners Are — A Space Run by "Agreements" More Than Law

Korea's subways and buses do have written laws (e.g., no fare evasion), but what actually governs the atmosphere is the informal manners citizens have agreed upon among themselves (서로 약속한 비공식 매너). Leaving priority seats empty and traveling quietly are mostly "tacit agreements" with no penalty clause (처벌 규정 없는 암묵적 합의), so they're hard for a foreigner to spot.

  • The roots are two-fold — consideration (배려, mutual yielding) and "not being a burden" (폐 끼치지 않기, public decency) (공중도덕). It's a sense that prioritizes the calm of a shared space over my own convenience.
  • That's why a "well-mannered passenger" in Korea is a passenger you don't notice (눈에 안 띄는 승객). Someone quiet, who doesn't take up space, who doesn't block the flow. The opposite — the "villain" — is the person who stands out alone (혼자 튀는 사람), being loud, blocking the way, and hogging space.

Once you grasp this sense, the rules below stop being a list to memorize and bind together into one single principle (하나의 원리).

② Priority Seats — The Seats You Leave Empty (Vulnerable-Passenger and Pregnancy Seats)

This is the most important part, and where foreigners make the most mistakes. At both ends (양 끝) of a Korean subway car are two kinds of special seats.

  • Vulnerable-passenger seats (교통약자석): Introduced as "elderly and infirm seats" (노약자석) when the Seoul subway launched in 1980, the name was changed to "vulnerable-passenger seats" (교통약자석) from 2005. They are seats for people who have difficulty moving (이동이 어려운 사람) — the elderly, the disabled, pregnant women, those with infants, the injured, and so on. They are usually at both ends of the car and a different color.
  • Pregnancy priority seats (임산부 배려석, pink): A pink seat (분홍색 좌석) introduced separately on the Seoul subway in 2013. It was created to solve the problem that pregnancy is hard to tell at a glance, so expectant mothers weren't being offered seats. The principle has settled as always keeping it empty (항상 비워두는 것). Pregnant women can get a pink badge (분홍색 배지) from a station customer center and wear it to signal "I may need a seat."

> For foreigners, in practice: In Korea, sitting in a vulnerable-passenger seat carries no penalty like a fine (과태료 같은 처벌은 없다) (unlike some countries). But not sitting is the social principle (앉지 않는 것이 사회적 원칙). Even if every regular seat is taken, leave the vulnerable-passenger and pregnancy priority seats empty. Koreans themselves perceive "give up a vulnerable-passenger seat when someone who needs it comes" and "keep the pregnancy seat empty entirely" differently — but for foreigners, the safe move is not to sit in either (둘 다 안 앉는 쪽이 안전).

③ The Quiet Car — Calls and Loud Voices (The Land of the Quiet Car)

The quietness of the Korean subway is the trait foreigners find most striking. It isn't total silence (완전 무음은 아니지만) (you hear the train and announcements), but it's noticeably quieter (확연히 조용하다) than subways in comparable cities abroad.

  • Phone calls (통화): Not banned, but short and soft (짧고 작게). Long, loud calls draw stares. Most passengers handle it with texts or earphone music (문자·이어폰 음악).
  • Conversation (대화): Talk with your group in a low voice (낮은 목소리) too. Avoid big laughs and raised voices.
  • Leaky earphones (소리 새는 이어폰): Even with earphones in, if the volume is so high that sound leaks, that's a nuisance too. Keep the volume moderate.
  • Quiet Car signage (Quiet Car 표시): Some lines and cars have signage for a quiet car asking riders to refrain from calls and loud talk (통화·큰 대화 자제).

This quietness is a direct expression of the public decency (공중도덕) of "not intruding on each other's space." For a foreigner, it's easy to remember as "here, you turn the volume down a notch."

④ Backpacks and Boarding — Manners of Space and Flow

The two things you most often bump into (literally) on a packed train.

Backpack manners (백팩 매너)

  • If you keep a big bag on your back (등에 멘 채), you hit the person beside you every time you turn your body. When it's crowded, wear it in front, carry it in your hand, or set it at your feet (앞으로 메거나, 손에 들거나, 발밑에 내려두기).
  • In Korea, "backpack in front when crowded" is so established a norm that it even appears in subway-manners ads. A foreign tourist with a big travel rucksack especially stands out, so turn it to the front naturally as it gets crowded.

Boarding manners — let people off first (승하차 매너 — 내릴 사람 먼저)

  • The most basic of basics (가장 기본 중의 기본) on the Korean subway: when the doors open, you board after everyone getting off has gotten off (내리는 사람이 다 내린 뒤에). That's because you board and alight through the same door.
  • Before the doors open, step one pace aside next to the door (문 옆으로 한 발 물러나) and clear the path for those getting off. Then board one by one, in line.
  • Many platforms have queue-line markings (줄 서기 표시선) painted on the floor. Line up along them and you won't get a "human traffic jam in front of the door."

Keep these two, and the flow stays smooth even on a packed train — and the one who blocks the flow is the "villain."

⑤ Escalators — From "Stand on One Side" to "Stand on Both" (In Transition)

This is the part that can confuse foreigners most, so let's pin it down precisely. Korean escalator culture is in a transition period right now (지금 전환기).

  • The past (2000s~): Starting from a late-1990s civic campaign and popularized around the 2002 World Cup, "standing in one line" (한 줄 서기) took hold. You stand on the right and leave the left open for those walking or running — and it's still a habit ingrained in many people (몸에 밴 습관).
  • Current recommendation (safety) (현재 권장): Standing in one line came with big problems — accidents from walking or running and falling (걷다·뛰다 넘어지는 사고) and equipment failure from weight concentrated on one side. So the government and the elevator-safety authority began officially recommending "don't walk or run; hold the handrail and stand" (걷거나 뛰지 말고, 손잡이를 잡고 서서 이용), and in 2026 again began recommending "standing in two lines" (두 줄 서기) (revived after 11 years). That's because safety data tallied most serious escalator accidents as "fault of users who walk or run."

> In short (to avoid confusion): It's not that "standing in one line is wrong"; rather, the habit is still one-line, but the recommendation is shifting to two-line (standing) (두 줄 서기로 바뀌는 중). Foreigners can simply remember "on escalators, don't walk or run — hold the handrail and stand" (걷거나 뛰지 말고, 손잡이 잡고 서서 가기), and either way you're safe and well-mannered. If you're in a hurry, use the stairs beside it.

⑥ Transit Card and Transfers, in Practice — One T-money Card Does It All

As important as manners is the practical payment (실전 결제). The part foreigners ask about most.

  • T-money card: A rechargeable transit card used for the subway, buses, and even taxis. You buy one for about 3,000 won (won (원)) at convenience stores like CU, GS25, and 7-Eleven (편의점). It works in most regions of Korea.
  • Recharging (충전): At convenience stores or station machines. Note — recharging is usually cash only (충전은 보통 현금만 된다) (many places don't take credit/debit cards). Have cash ready in advance.
  • ★ Tag both on boarding and alighting (탈 때+내릴 때 모두 태그): The heart of Korean transit. Tag the card once when you get on, once when you get off (탈 때 한 번, 내릴 때 한 번). If you don't tag when getting off, the transfer discount disappears (내릴 때 안 찍으면 환승할인이 사라지고) and the distance fare gets tangled too. Same for buses — be sure to tag the reader when you get off.
  • Transfer discount (환승할인): Tag with the same card and a bus↔subway transfer is discounted within 30 minutes of alighting (하차 후 30분 이내) (60 minutes from 21:00 to 07:00). Up to 4 transfers (5 rides total). This is one reason Korean public transit is cheap.
  • Options for foreigners (외국인용 옵션): Short-term travelers can also choose the Climate Card (기후동행카드) (unlimited Seoul buses, subway, Ttareungi bikes, etc. for a set period) or foreigner-focused cards like Mpass. For a few days of intensive travel, a flat-rate pass may pay off.
  • Apps (앱): Check subway lines and transfers ahead with the subway app (지하철 앱) (or Naver Map / KakaoMap) and you won't get lost. English supported.

⑦ Why So Quiet and Considerate — Nunchi and Public Decency

Finally, the "why" that ties all these manners into one.

  • Nunchi (눈치): Koreans read the atmosphere and others' states without being told (분위기와 타인의 상태를 읽는다) (→ #1145). On the subway, constantly checking "Am I being loud right now? Am I taking up too much space? Does that person need a seat?" is nunchi (눈치). Leaving priority seats empty and turning the volume down are, in the end, expressions of nunchi (눈치의 표현).
  • Public decency (공중도덕, not being a burden): Korean society sees not being a burden (a nuisance) to others in shared spaces (공동 공간에서 남에게 폐 끼치지 않는 것) as an important virtue. It's a sense that prioritizes the calm of a shared space (함께 쓰는 공간의 평온) over my own freedom. The subway is the stage where that sense shows up most concentrated.
  • Consideration (배려): Yielding a seat to the vulnerable (the elderly, pregnant women, the disabled) is less an explicit rule than a social expectation (사회적 기대). That's why it works even without penalties.

In other words, the quiet, order, and consideration of the Korean subway aren't from fearing rules; they're a culture of reading each other (nunchi (눈치)) and not being a burden (public decency (공중도덕)) (서로를 읽고 폐 끼치지 않으려는 문화) made real as a space. Once you know this, "why go this far?" turns into "ah, that's why."

❓ Frequently Asked Questions from Foreigners (FAQ)

  • Can I really never sit in a priority seat (배려석)? — There's no penalty, but even when every regular seat is full, leaving it empty is the Korean principle (비워두는 게 한국 원칙). The perception is especially strong for the pink pregnancy priority seat (임산부 배려석): don't sit even when it's empty. For foreigners, not sitting is the safe move.
  • What if I get an urgent call? — You can take it, but short and soft (짧고 작게). If it'll run long, get off at the next station to talk, or text "On the subway, call you later" — that's the manner.
  • Can I walk up the escalator? — Many people still walk out of habit, but the safety recommendation is "stand" (안전상 권장은 서서 이용) (shifting to two-line). If you're in a hurry, use the stairs beside it. At the very least, don't run (뛰지는 않기).
  • Do I really have to tag the card when getting off too? — Yes, absolutely (네, 반드시). If you don't, the transfer discount disappears and an extra fare may apply. Tag on the bus when you get off, too.
  • Can I eat? — Refrain from strong-smelling or spillable food in the car. Water or a simple drink is fine.
  • Do I always have to wear the backpack in front? — When it's empty, it doesn't matter. When it gets crowded (사람이 많아지면), turn it to the front or set it at your feet — the key is "space consideration when crowded."
