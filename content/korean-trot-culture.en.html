Korean Trot Culture — From Grandma's Music to an All-Generations Craze

> Trot (트로트) · Ppongjjak (뽕짝) · Kkeokgi vocal style (꺾기) · Miss Trot (미스트롯) / Mr. Trot (미스터트롯) — a Korean popular-music explainer for foreigners. Not "old non-K-pop songs," but "why, what, how, and ★how it came roaring back."

Travel around Korea and it isn't only K-pop you hear. From speakers in market alleys, inside tour buses, on restaurant TVs — somewhere a song drifts out that is soulful and aching yet upbeat, with a distinctive warble all its own. Foreigners usually brush it off as "old Korean pop," but to Koreans this is trot (트로트), a genre of popular music nearly a century old. Once pushed aside as "grandma and grandpa music," trot came roaring back through the 2019–2020 audition-show phenomenon into a culture that thrills every generation, from teenagers to people in their 90s. This article isn't about "which noraebang (노래방) to sing it in" (→ cross-link), but explains, at a foreigner's eye level, what trot is, why it sounds the way it does, what history it passed through, and how it rose again. This is installment #45 of the Korean Culture Explainer Series.

⚠️ 3 Things Foreigners Often Get Wrong

1. "Trot = just an old version of K-pop" ❌ — Trot is a separate, independent genre from K-pop. Where K-pop has aimed at the global market on a dance/hip-hop/R&B base since the 1990s, trot is a native Korean popular music with its own 5-note scale, duple beat, and kkeokgi (꺾기) vocal style carried down since the 1930s. Both are "Korean popular music," but their roots and sounds differ.

2. "Trot = music only old people listen to" ❌ — Before 2019 that perception was indeed strong. But after the Miss Trot (미스트롯) / Mr. Trot (미스터트롯) audition phenomenon, trot became an all-generation culture enjoyed from teen idol fandoms to seniors in their 90s. In Korea today, trot is closer to a "trendy revived genre."

3. "Trot = a knockoff of Japanese enka (演歌)" ❌ (don't assert this) — This is a sensitive subject debated by Korean scholars and critics for decades. There are views that acknowledge enka's influence, and views that stress contemporaneous mutual influence and independent post-liberation development — both exist (see ③ below). A foreigner who flatly calls it "an enka knockoff" can give offense — understanding it in a balanced way is the point.

① What Trot Is — the Korean Soul Called 'Ppongjjak'

Trot (트로트) is a genre of Korean popular song (gayo, 가요). Koreans also call it ppongjjak (뽕짝), a nickname that comes from an onomatopoeia mimicking the "kung-jjak, kung-jjak" duple beat.

  • Trot's sentiment is often regarded as the vessel that most directly holds Koreans' joys and sorrows. Parting, hometown, mother, life's regrets — it sings, richly, the universal emotions anyone can relate to.
  • So trot is taken as more than a mere music genre: it is a pop-culture code deeply tied to Korean sentiment (jeong, 정 / han, 한).
  • Many songs have a single lyric line that circulates like a maxim for life, such as "Life Is Unfinished" (Insaengeun Mi-wanseong, 인생은 미완성) and "I Love My Hometown" (Gohyangi Joa, 고향이 좋아).

If K-pop is "polish and global reach," trot is "soulfulness, jeong (情, 정), and our own sentiment." The two aren't rivals but the two pillars of Korean popular music.

② The Signature Sound — Kkeokgi, the 5-Note Scale, the Duple Beat

Hear trot once and it's hard to forget. That distinctiveness comes from a few musical traits.

  • Kkeokgi (꺾기, the trembling style): Trot's biggest signature. Rather than pulling a note straight, the singer finely trembles it, bends it upward, and shakes it. It's seen as influenced by the trembling style of Namdo folk songs (Namdo minyo, 남도 민요, the folk songs of the Jeolla region). This 'kkeokgi (꺾기)' produces trot's signature ache and exuberance at once.
  • Pentatonic (5-note scale): Instead of a 7-note scale (do-re-mi-fa-sol-la-ti), it mainly uses a five-note scale — a minor pentatonic ("la-ti-do-mi-fa" type) or a major pentatonic that emphasizes certain notes. This scale creates the "unmistakably trot" melody.
  • Duple beat: The repetitive "kung-jjak, kung-jjak" duple meter is the base. The very nickname 'ppongjjak (뽕짝)' came from this rhythm.

> Note: the name 'trot' comes from the American ballroom dance tune foxtrot, but actual Korean trot and the foxtrot have almost no musical connection beyond both being in duple meter. Only the name was borrowed.

③ History and Origin — Starting from 1930s 'Popular Songs' (★the Enka Debate)

Trot's history reaches nearly 100 years.

  • In the early 1930s, modern popular songs began appearing in colonial-era Korea, and these are trot's roots. There was no genre name "trot" then; they were called "popular songs" (yuhaengga, 유행가) and "popular ditties" (yuhaeng sogok, 유행소곡).
  • The nickname 'ppongjjak (뽕짝)' was attached in the mid-1960s (at first with a somewhat derogatory nuance, later gradually replaced by the neutral 'trot').

★ The Enka (演歌) Origin Debate — Don't Jump to Conclusions

There is a sensitive point about trot's origin that foreigners should know. It concerns the relationship with Japanese popular music, enka (演歌). In Korea this is a real academic and social debate that has run for decades.

  • One view (enka-influence thesis): a critical perspective holding that trot is a form shaped under the influence of Japanese enka during the colonial era, a product of the colonial period. Two major "ppongjjak debates" (뽕짝 논쟁) erupted in the 1960s and the 1980s, and in 1984 a provocative claim along the lines of "Who calls ppongjjak (뽕짝) our own?" lit the controversy.
  • The other view (mutual-influence / independent-development thesis): a perspective holding that trot was shaped through mutual influence with Korean traditional folk songs and Western blues-derived music, not just Japanese enka, and that after liberation in 1945 it walked its own path distinct from enka. In particular, trot's shift toward major keys and the 7-note scale from the 1940s on is seen as a development unique to trot and absent in the enka of the time. It stresses that the lyrics, too, were written by Koreans in Korean, holding Korean sentiment. There are also rebuttals that frame the enka-origin thesis as "colonial-victim consciousness."

> The point: whether enka influenced trot is "a debate Korean scholars argue in a balanced way," not a matter for a foreigner to settle by declaring "it's just copied enka." Within the larger flow of modern East Asian popular music influencing one another, the balanced view is to understand trot as a genre that took root and developed independently in Korea.

Their songs are still sung endlessly in noraebang (노래방), on trot variety shows, and at senior gatherings. A foreigner who wants to bond with older Koreans will find that simply knowing these names opens the door to conversation.

⑤ ★★ The Modern Revival — the Miss Trot / Mr. Trot Phenomenon

Until 2019, trot was boxed into the perception of "old songs the older generation listens to" and pushed to the margins. The event that completely flipped this was two audition variety shows on TV Chosun (TV조선).

  • "Tomorrow Is Miss Trot" (Naeireun Miss Trot, 내일은 미스트롯) (2019, TV Chosun, TV조선): an audition to discover female trot singers. It lifted the once-marginal trot into a nationwide phenomenon. The winner of this season was Song Ga-in (송가인) — who became a singer representing the 2020s trot revival.
  • "Tomorrow Is Mr. Trot" (Naeireun Mister Trot, 내일은 미스터트롯) (2020, TV Chosun, TV조선): the male version. Airing in January 2020, it recorded explosive ratings and buzz. In this season Lim Young-woong (임영웅) won (took the "Jin" (진) title) and rose to the rank of a "national singer."

These two programs lifted trot at a single stroke to the peak of mainstream culture. Countless trot auditions and variety shows poured out afterward, and stars including Lim Young-woong (임영웅) and Song Ga-in (송가인) came to command huge fandoms rivaling those of K-pop idols.

> An important point from the foreigner's perspective: in Korea, "Mr. Trot" (미스터트롯) and "Lim Young-woong" (임영웅) are not just the names of a TV show and a singer, but a core phenomenon of 2020s Korean pop culture. The firepower of middle-aged-and-older fandoms in particular is no less than that of K-pop fandoms.

⑥ Why Every Generation Is Crazy About It

The most striking thing about trot's revival is that it knows no generational bounds. Why is that?

  • Middle-aged and older — nostalgia and pride: Watching "the music of my generation" come roaring back from childhood, they rallied powerfully. They are the core firepower of the Lim Young-woong (임영웅) and Song Ga-in (송가인) fandoms.
  • The MZ generation — 'fresh' retro: To a young generation that never lived through trot's heyday, trot instead arrived as fresh material. A flood of skilled newcomers, plus fusions of trot with other genres like dance and ballad, captured young ears too.
  • The whole family together: Trot variety became one of the rare kinds of content a grandmother and grandchild enjoy together in front of the same TV. Creating a shared topic across generations was a major engine of the revival.
  • The power of 'story': The audition narrative of rising from obscurity to stardom, and each singer's personal life story layered onto the songs, create deep immersion.

⑦ How a Foreigner Can Enjoy Trot

> A beginner's guide for foreigners who want to taste Korean pop culture a little more deeply.

  • Starter songs: On YouTube, start with the talked-about Mr./Miss Trot stages of Lim Young-woong (임영웅) and Song Ga-in (송가인), or with classics like Lee Mi-ja's "Camellia Lady" (Dongbaek Agassi, 동백아가씨) and Na Hoon-a's "Hometown Station" (Gohyangyeok, 고향역) — they make trot's texture easy to feel. (Verify singer/song names and spellings.)
  • Listen for the kkeokgi (꺾기): Focus on how the singer trembles and bends notes upward, and trot's unique charm finally comes through. It's the opposite of K-pop's sleek vocals.
  • Take on the noraebang (노래방, coin karaoke): Korean noraebang are packed with trot classics. Even without knowing the lyrics' meaning, anyone can enjoy the duple-beat groove. With older Koreans, it's the ultimate icebreaker.
  • Watch trot variety: Watch Mr./Miss Trot stages in subtitled clips and you'll instantly understand "why Korea is wild about this genre."
  • One point of manners: Don't put trot down as a "tacky old song." To Koreans, trot is a genre that holds sentiment, history, and pride. Approach it with curiosity and respect, and you'll be welcomed.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • What's the difference between trot and K-pop? — Both are Korean popular music, but K-pop is a globally oriented genre on a dance/hip-hop base since the 1990s, while trot is a native Korean genre with a 5-note scale, duple beat, and kkeokgi (꺾기) vocal style carried down since the 1930s. The sound and the sentiment differ.
  • Did trot really come from Japanese enka? — It's a subject Korean academia has debated for decades. There are views acknowledging enka's influence, and views stressing mutual influence with Korean traditional and Western music and independent post-liberation development. Flatly calling it "an enka knockoff" is neither accurate nor polite.
  • Is 'ppongjjak (뽕짝)' a derogatory word? — In the 1960s it started with a somewhat derogatory nuance, but today it is also widely used as an affectionate nickname for trot. Still, in formal or polite settings, 'trot' is the safe choice.
  • Is Mr. Trot really that popular? — Yes. After airing in 2020, it lifted trot to the peak of Korean mainstream culture — a social-phenomenon-level variety show. Its winner, Lim Young-woong (임영웅), is currently one of the singers with the biggest fandom in Korea.
  • Do young people listen to trot too? — Yes. Since the revival, trot has become an all-generation culture. With fusions across genres and the rise of skilled newcomers, there are many MZ-generation fans.
