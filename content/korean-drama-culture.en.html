Korean drama (K-drama) culture — Why do Korean dramas make the world cry?

16 episodes, short-term completion, writer-centered, emotional line, OST, webtoon IP — K-drama explainer for foreigners. Not ‘what kind of work do you see?’ but ‘why·how·★ behind it’.

When you turn on Netflix, Korean dramas are at the top of charts around the world. Foreigners who cried after watching “Squid Game,” viewers who fell in love with the warmth of “Weird Lawyer Woo Young-woo,” fans who binge-watched the revenge drama of “The Glory” — now K-drama has gone beyond Korea and has become the emotional language of people around the world. However, there is something that foreigners do not know. Why Do Korean dramas especially make people cry? While American dramas go on to season 10, why do Korean dramas end with one 16-episode season and then move on to the next work? This article is not about “what to watch” (recommended works), but rather explains the unique structure, aesthetics, and history of K-drama, as well as the light and dark side of the production site behind the splendor, from a foreigner’s perspective. ⚠️ 3 things foreigners often misunderstand

1. "K-drama = All the same Cinderella romance" ❌ — In the early days of the Korean Wave, the image of romance was strong, but now K-drama has a wide range of genres, including survival (Squid Game), revenge drama (The Glory), courtroom (Woo Young-woo), historical drama, medical, and fantasy. The fact that it does not follow a uniform happy ending or hero narrative is actually considered a strength.

2. "Why is the season so short? Isn't it unfinished?" ❌ — Most K-dramas complete the story in one season of 12 to 16 episodes. Rather than filling up the story by extending the season in the American style, the intended design is a short-term conclusion that ends the story within one work. So the “satisfaction of regular driving” is great.

3. "Because it's flashy, the production environment must be good" ❌ — Aside from its global success, Korean drama working conditions at the production site have long been pointed out as a problem (⑦ light and shade below). As the light is stronger, there are also shadows — knowing this will help you understand K-dramas in a more three-dimensional way.

① What is K-drama — The core axis of the Korean Wave

K-drama (Korean drama) refers to TV/OTT dramas produced in Korea. Along with K-pop and K-food, it is one of the three main axes of the Korean Wave** and is a major contributor to the globalization of Korean popular culture.

It is clear that K-dramas differ from dramas from other countries. Rather than quick action or shocking twists, it builds up the character's inner world, relationships, and emotions closely. Universal themes such as work, social pressure, family responsibility, and unspoken sacrifices are expressed with Korean emotions, eliciting empathy by saying "I know that feeling, too", even if the culture is different. A representative example is that Latin American viewers fell in love with K-dramas because of the emotional immersion similar to their country's telenovelas.

② Short-term completion of 16 episodes — What is the difference from the American season system?

The biggest structural characteristic of K-drama is self-contained.

  • K-drama: Usually 16 episodes (6, 8, and 12 episodes are also common in recent OTTs), the story is completely completed within one season. There are few filler episodes, and the beginning, development, and ending are compressed, so immersion is high.
  • American drama: A season system in which the next season is produced** while about 20 episodes are aired in one season. If it is popular, the season continues, and a long-term contract of 5 years can be signed with one actor.
  • Why the difference: The US and Japan negotiate contracts and schedules on the premise of a season system and pre-production from the planning stage. Korea, by contrast, has traditionally had a structure where season 2 is only planned after a work succeeds, which created heavy burdens like coordinating actor schedules, recasting, and soaring production costs.

Thanks to this short-completion structure, K-drama became "content that's perfect for one big binge," giving it strong competitiveness in the global OTT era. Behind it, though, lies the shadow of production-schedule pressure too (⑦).

③ Emotional Lines, OST, Writer-Centered — The Aesthetics That Make K-Drama "Make You Cry"

The secret behind K-drama making the world cry can be summed up in three things.

  • Deep emotional lines: Writers calculate precisely which narrative and character arcs will move viewers, placing an emotional high point in every episode. An unspoken feeling, a single glance, a moment of silence — these act as an "emotional shortcut." Packing maximum emotion into a small number of scenes is the grammar of Korean-style direction.
  • The power of the OST: In K-drama, the OST (theme song and insert songs) is inseparable from the work itself. A ballad playing over a decisive scene amplifies the emotion, and the OST itself climbs the charts, connecting back to K-pop (→ idols sometimes sing the OST or even appear on-screen, linked to #1161).
  • Writer-centered system: Where the US centers on showrunners and studios, Korea leans heavily on "star writers." The names of writers like Kim Eun-sook (김은숙) and Park Ji-eun (박지은) are practically a guarantee of success — the script determines the work's identity.

⑤ From the Origins of Hallyu to Global OTT — The History of K-Drama's Spread

K-drama's globalization progressed in stages. (Titles and years update quickly, so checking the latest information is recommended.)

  • 1st generation — Asian Hallyu (2000s): "Winter Sonata" (Gyeoul Yeonga, 겨울연가) caused a sensation in Japan, and "Dae Jang Geum" (Daejanggeum, 대장금) caused one across Asia, together coining the term "Hallyu." At this stage, it was mostly centered on Asia.
  • 2nd generation — OTT entry (late 2010s): Netflix began investing in Korean dramas, and Netflix originals such as "Kingdom" announced K-drama's new possibilities to the world.
  • 3rd generation — world peak (2020s): "Squid Game" (2021) swept Netflix's global No. 1 spot, putting K-drama at the center of world content. Afterward, "Extraordinary Attorney Woo" and "The Glory" continued the momentum with consecutive global hits.

The key is the expansion from Asia to the world, and TV to OTT. As OTT platforms tore down language and border barriers, dramas from the small country of Korea went straight into living rooms around the world.

⑥ Webtoon IP — K-Drama's Hidden Source

Nowadays, a good many hit K-dramas start out as webtoons (source material) (→ #1175 webtoon culture).

  • "Itaewon Class" is based on the identically-titled webtoon that drew a cumulative 10 million readers, and the original author took part directly in writing the drama's script. Numerous hits such as "Misaeng" and "Why Her?" are likewise based on webtoons and web novels.
  • Why webtoons: Webtoons already offer stories, characters, and worldbuilding validated by reader response. From a drama production standpoint, this lowers the risk of failure and lets a solid IP be expanded into video.
  • This shows that the Korean content industry has built an IP ecosystem flowing from webtoon → drama → global OTT. For foreigners, it's also a new way to enjoy content: "finding and reading the original webtoon of your favorite K-drama."

⑦ ★★ Light and Shadow — The Production Site Behind the Glamour

To understand K-drama in three dimensions, you also need to know the shadow side of its global success. This is the most important balancing point in this article.

  • Jjokdaebon and live-broadcast-style filming: Traditionally, Korean dramas rarely used pre-production, and were often filmed while airing at the same time. "Jjokdaebon" (쪽대본) — where the script arrives just minutes before filming — and "live-broadcast-style filming" (saengbangsong-sik chwaryeong, 생방송식 촬영) — where episodes are shot and handed over right up until broadcast — became entrenched practices.
  • Long working hours: As a result, the labor intensity for production staff was extreme. One survey found that staff averaged about 19 hours of work a day, with less than 3 hours of rest. Cases such as filming for over 18 hours in a heatwave, or working 28 hours straight, were reported in the media and became a social issue.
  • Contract-practice problems: It was also pointed out that even though staff were effectively employees, they worked under "freelance" or "turnkey contract" arrangements that exposed them to long hours and low pay.
  • Improving: Since then, a 52-hour workweek limit was applied through a revision of the Labor Standards Act, and as pre-production (filming everything in advance before airing) has become more common, conditions have been on an improving trend. Discussions on introducing a standard labor contract are also ongoing. Still, many assessments call it a "work in progress," so it is not a fully resolved problem.

The light (global success, polished quality) and shadow (production labor) of K-drama are two sides of the same coin. Enjoy the work, but remembering that countless people's labor lies behind it lets you appreciate it more deeply.

⑧ For foreigners to enjoy K-dramas more

  • Choose an introductory genre: If it’s your first time, Romance, Fantasy, Thriller is okay. If you want to learn deeply about Korean culture, watch historical drama, and if you want to watch society, watch survival/satire.
  • Subtitles vs. dubbing: Subtitles (original language) recommended to enhance the emotions and taste of words. In K-drama, the nuances and politeness of the lines are important, so the original language audio increases immersion.

❓ Foreigners Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • Why are the seasons of K-dramas so short? — This is because most of them have a short-term completion structure where 16 episodes are completed in one season. It is not an incomplete work, but an intentional design to end the story within one work. The reason for the great satisfaction of regular driving.
  • Why does it make people cry like this? — This is because the writer exquisitely arranges emotional high points in each episode and closely builds up the unsaid feelings, OST, and character growth. K-drama grammar focuses on emotion rather than action.
  • Why is season 2 not coming out? — Traditionally in Korea, seasons are planned only after the success of a work, so there is a huge burden on actor schedules, recasting, and production costs. Recently, the number of season systems focusing on global OTT hits has been increasing.
  • Should I watch it with subtitles or dubbed? — To capture the nuances of emotions and polite language, subtitles (original language) are recommended. If you want to enjoy it lightly, dubbing is okay.
  • Is it true that the K-drama production environment is difficult? — In the past, short scripts and long working hours were serious problems, and this is now improving with the expansion of pre-production and 52 hours a week. However, it has not been completely resolved (⑦).
