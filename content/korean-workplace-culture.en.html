Korean work culture — Overtime, lunch, coffee, reading the texture of Korean company life

Hierarchy, rank, overtime, 52-hour workweek, lunch, after-dinner coffee, company dinner, notice, MZ work-life balance — Explainer of Korean work culture for foreigners. Not ‘How to get a job at a Korean company’ but ‘Why is life at a Korean company like this?’

When watching K-dramas, Korean corporate scenes often appear. If the manager doesn't get off work, he can't leave the office, and during lunch time, people in the department rush out to eat together, and after the meal, everyone comes back with a glass of Americano. Newcomers call their superiors "Manager" and seniors "Assistant Manager Kim", and even the order in which drinks are poured at company dinners is set. These scenes, which seem unfamiliar and sometimes frustrating to foreigners, actually encapsulate the framework of Korean society: hierarchy, group, and diligence. Although tourists rarely work in Korean workplaces, they are the background for dramas, movies, and entertainment shows, and half of the daily lives of Koreans. This article is not about “how to get a job at a Korean company,” but explains why office life is like this — and how it is changing so quickly from a foreigner’s perspective. Deep individual topics such as company dinners, recognition, and nicknames are linked in separate sections (→ cross-link). Korean culture explainer series 56.

Key line: All customs in the Korean workplace were built on the tradition of “organization over the individual, above below, and diligence before efficiency” — and now that premise is rapidly shaking (⑥ below).

⚠️ 3 things foreigners often misunderstand

1. "Korean companies = unconditional overtime and abuse of power" ❌ — Long working hours were true, but it was closer to an old image. The landscape is changing rapidly with the introduction of the 52-hour workweek in 2018 and the MZ generation's demand for work-life balance. The number of companies proud of “leave work on time” has increased. Dramas also tend to depict old scenes more vividly for dramatic effect.

2. "Calling someone by rank is authoritarian" ❌ — Calling someone by their name "Manager" or "Assistant Manager Kim" is not rude, but Korean etiquette of respecting others. Rather, it is very rude to call a superior by his/her name (→ #1150). However, an increasing number of companies are changing even this to horizontal titles such as "○○"·"Pro"·"Manager".

3. "Lunch or company dinner together = invasion of privacy" ❌ — In Korea, eating together is not just a meal, but socializing to build relationships and information (→ 'Jung' culture #1135). Although coercion has decreased, the sense of becoming colleagues through ‘eating together’ is still at the core of Korean workplace sentiment.

① What is a Korean workplace — in one sentence

Korean work culture can be summarized as a combination of Confucian hierarchy + collectivism + hard work ethics.

  • Hierarchy: Hierarchy based on age, rank, and years of employment permeates every aspect of daily life. Who speaks first at a meeting, who pours drinks at a company dinner, who leaves last at the office — these are all scenes that reveal ranking.
  • Collectivism: Prioritizes team/department harmony over individual performance. The attitude of ‘going along’ rather than standing out has been considered a virtue.
  • Diligence: Through the era of compressed growth, 'working long and hard' has become a value in itself. Working overtime was once a sign of dedication.

These three axes appear in specific customs such as lunch, nicknames, company dinners, and overtime. And these three axes are currently being renegotiated due to the MZ generation and system changes.

② Hierarchy, rank, title — ‘Manager’ instead of name

The first thing that stands out at a Korean company is that people almost never use first names. Instead, they address each other by rank (직급, jikgeup).

A typical general-track rank order (varies by company):

Sawon (사원, staff) → Juim (senior staff) → Daeri (assistant manager) → Gwajang (manager) → Chajang (deputy general manager) → Bujang (general manager) → (executives: director, managing director, executive director, vice president, president…)

  • Superiors and seniors are addressed as "surname + rank + nim" — for example, "Kim Daeri-nim" or "Park Gwajang-nim." The honorific "nim" is almost always attached to the rank.
  • Using someone's plain name is only acceptable between peers and juniors; calling a superior by their first name is a breach of etiquette.
  • This system of address even determines who uses honorific speech toward whom, who greets first, and who speaks up first with an opinion. Korean honorific grammar and workplace hierarchy are effectively locked together.

To go deeper into the texture of address terms → #1150 Korean Forms of Address (unni, imo, sajangnim). This article focuses on how that hierarchy operates across the entire "workplace" system.

★ Change point: A fast-growing number of companies are dropping rank titles in favor of horizontal communication. IT firms and startups that use neutral titles like "○○-nim," "Pro," "Manager," "Lead," or "in charge" instead of rank are the leading example (see ⑥ below).

③ Overtime and the 52-Hour Workweek — Korean Workplaces' Biggest Turning Point

Korea was long famous for its long working hours. Overtime (yagun, 야근) was never just extra work — it once stood as "proof of diligence and dedication." There was an unspoken atmosphere that "you must not leave before your boss does," and it was common for managers to plan workloads assuming overtime as a given.

The turning point that changed this by law is the 52-hour workweek system.

  • What: A system capping weekly working hours at a statutory 40 hours + 12 hours of overtime = a maximum of 52 hours.
  • When: Took effect in July 2018 at large companies and public institutions with 300+ employees → then expanded in stages to workplaces with 5 or more employees.
  • Meaning: As symbolized by the phrase "a life with evenings," it was an institutional signal shifting the center of gravity from work to personal life.

★ Working hours — verification recommended: In international comparisons, Korea's annual working hours are reported to be longer than the OECD average (one source puts Korea at roughly 1,901 hours in 2022 vs. an OECD average of roughly 1,752 hours). Statistics vary by source and year, though, so checking the latest OECD/Statistics Korea figures is recommended. The direction is clear either way — still long, but falling fast.

As overtime has declined, more companies have reached a point where saying "I'm heading home" no longer requires reading the room — this is the most tangibly felt part of the change in Korean workplaces.

④ Lunch, Coffee, and Communication — The Social Rhythm of Korean Workplaces

One Korean office scene that foreigners often find surprising is lunch.

  • Eating lunch together: When lunchtime comes, the department or team heads out to a restaurant all together, in a group. Eating with someone by arrangement, rather than alone, has long been closer to the default. Lunch isn't just a meal — it's social time for building relationships and sharing information.
  • ★ Post-lunch Americano: After eating, almost ritually, everyone moves to a café together and each gets a cup of coffee — especially an Americano. "Eat, then a coffee" is the Korean office worker's lunch routine in itself (this also connects to why Korea is a "café republic" → #1158).
  • The texture of communication: Rather than stating opinions directly in meetings, it's common to speak indirectly or wait for the right moment, reading the boss and the room. This sense of "reading without being told" is exactly nunchi (눈치) (→ #1145).

★ Change point: "Honbap" (eating lunch alone) is no longer seen as strange (→ #1159), and mandatory lunches or forced seating together have declined. Even so, the feeling of becoming a team through "eating together" is still very much alive.

⑤ Hoesik and Nunchi — Relationships That Continue After Work Ends

In Korean workplace culture, hoesik (회식, company dinners) and nunchi cannot be left out. Both are deep topics covered in their own separate articles, but here's where they sit within the "workplace system":

  • Hoesik: A gathering after work where the department eats and drinks together to bond. Etiquette like the order of pouring drinks, toasts, and moving to a "second" or "third" round is intertwined with hierarchy. It once served to build dedication and belonging, but these days pressure to attend has dropped sharply, and the trend is toward lighter formats like "lunch hoesik" or "culture hoesik." For more → #1140 Korean Dinner and Drinking Culture.
  • Nunchi: The sense of acting on reading your boss's and team's mood even without explicit instruction. In Korean workplaces, being told "you have no nunchi" counts as a fairly serious flaw. Push back too bluntly or fail to read the room, and you become "someone with no nunchi." For more → #1145 Nunchi Culture.

Both are cases of hierarchy and collectivism extending beyond working hours (hoesik) and beyond words (nunchi). That's why Korean workplaces can feel to foreigners like "something more than just work."

⑥ ★★ Change — The MZ Generation, Work-Life Balance, and Horizontalization

This is the most important part of this article. The "rigid Korean workplace" described above is rapidly becoming a thing of the past. Korean workplace culture is right now in the middle of a major shift being pushed by generational change and institutional reform together.

  • Work-Life Balance (워라밸): The term began circulating in job-seeker communities around 2015 and became mainstream discourse through the 52-hour workweek debate (2017–2018). Multiple surveys report that "work-life balance" ranks as the MZ generation's top priority in choosing a job, at times even ahead of salary or job security (figures vary by survey — verification recommended).
  • Horizontalization: As companies set horizontal communication as an organizational-culture goal, a trend has spread of dropping rank titles for terms like "○○-nim" or "Pro," and simplifying reporting and approval processes.
  • Work from home/flexible work: Work from home has increased rapidly during COVID-19 (one government survey reported a significant increase compared to before the pandemic). Since then, in line with the trend of returning to the office (office return), the number of companies adopting flexible work arrangements such as staggered commuting and optional work has increased.
  • Quiet Quitting: An attitude of not leaving the company but working ‘just as much as you are’ and showing excessive dedication. It is a value that is completely opposite to the past, when working overtime and dedication were seen as virtues, and is often mentioned among Korean office workers these days.
  • Job change is becoming more common: The term 'lifelong job' has faded, and changing jobs** in search of better conditions has become a natural career strategy.

Balanced picture: Korean workplaces still remain hierarchical and group-oriented — there are large differences depending on the company, industry, and generation. The landscape of conservative large corporations and manufacturing industries is quite different from that of horizontal and flexible IT and startups. The key point is that 'it is changing rapidly in one direction': Up → Horizontal, working overtime → work-life balance, lifelong job → job change.

⑦ Reasons why it is good for foreigners to know this culture

Tourists rarely enter Korean companies directly. Still, if you know the work culture, you can see Korea much more clearly.

  • I read the drama/movie: I understand why the tension and humor in office-set K-dramas (boss, new recruit, company dinner, overtime). If you know the meaning of hierarchy, discernment, and leaving work on time, the scene looks different.
  • The ‘key’ of Korean society: Workplace culture is a condensation of the codes of the entire Korean society: hierarchy, group, and diligence. Even the names of restaurants and stores, waiting in line, and the meaning of quick (→ #1143) come from the same root.
  • If you are a business visitor: If you have a meeting or meal with a Korean partner, it will be of great help to know Exchanging business cards (two hands), Priority to seniors and positions, and Meaning of the meal.
  • Looking at ‘Korea Now’: The changing work culture is a window showing the Korean youth generation’s values ​​(work-life balance, individuality, fairness).

❓ Foreigners Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • Do Korean companies really have that many overtime hours? — This was the case in the past, and there are still large differences by industry and company, but the overall trend is decreasing since the 52-hour workweek in 2018. The number of companies that pride themselves on leaving work on time has increased. The appearances in the drama are somewhat exaggerated.
  • Why do you call your co-workers by their rank instead of their name? — It is Korean etiquette of respect. It is rude to call a superior by their first name. However, an increasing number of companies are changing their titles to horizontal titles (“Mr. ○○”, “Pro”) (→ #1150).
  • Do I have to participate in lunch or company dinners? — The pressure has decreased significantly. However, if you know that 'eating together' is a Korean social practice that builds relationships, you can see that participation is not just an obligation but a process of becoming colleagues.
  • Are Korean workplaces very different from other countries? — There was a big difference from the Western individual and horizontal culture in that there was a strong sense of hierarchy, group, and diligence. However, due to the MZ generation and system changes, the gap is rapidly narrowing.
  • What is ‘notice’? Why is it important at work? — It is the sense of reading the mood of your boss and team without saying anything. As a core technology that smoothens relationships in a hierarchical society, being "not tactful" is a pretty harsh evaluation (→ #1145).
