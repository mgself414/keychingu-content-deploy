Korean Film Culture — From Chungmuro to Bong Joon-ho, the Land of 10-Million-Ticket Movies

> Chungmuro (충무로) · Bong Joon-ho (봉준호) · Parasite (기생충) · cheonman yeonghwa (천만 영화, 10-million-viewer films) · BIFF — a Korean film culture explainer for foreigners. Not "which movie to watch," but "why Korean cinema works on the world stage."

In February 2020, at the U.S. Academy Awards, one Korean film was called out for Best Picture: director Bong Joon-ho's (봉준호) Parasite (기생충). It was the first time in the Oscars' 92-year history that a non-English-language film won Best Picture. To foreigners, Korea is now not only the land of K-pop and K-drama but a nation of cinema that Cannes and the Oscars have honored. But it didn't happen overnight. Behind it lies a 30-year arc — enduring an era of censorship, exploding back to life in the late 1990s, and seeing auteur directors sweep the world's festivals. Koreans created a signature box-office phenomenon called the cheonman gwangaek (천만 관객, 10-million-audience) film, and they refer to the entire industry with a single word meaning "the street of cinema": Chungmuro (충무로, 忠武路). This article isn't "where to watch" (→ place guide); it explains, at a foreigner's eye level, why Korean cinema is strong — its history and meaning. This is episode 52 of the Korean culture explainer series.

> Key Takeaway: The strength of Korean cinema isn't "because it has lots of money." It's the result of three things locking together — freed expression + the individuality of auteur directors + a thick, deep audience base.

⚠️ 3 Things Foreigners Often Misunderstand

1. "Korean film = the movie version of K-drama" ❌ — Korean cinema and K-drama are different ecosystems. Where dramas center on romance and warmth, Korean film is especially strong in heavy, dark genres — thriller, social critique, black comedy. Just remember that Parasite (기생충) and Oldboy (올드보이) are not melodramas.

2. "Parasite was a sudden stroke of luck" ❌ — Parasite (기생충) was no accident but the result of two accumulated decades. Directors like Park Chan-wook (박찬욱) and Lee Chang-dong (이창동) had already been winning at Cannes since the 2000s, and Bong Joon-ho (봉준호) was a proven director through Memories of Murder (살인의 추억) and The Host (괴물).

3. "Chungmuro is the neighborhood where they shoot films now" ❌ — Chungmuro (충무로, 忠武路) is no longer a filming district. It comes from the era when film studios gathered there, and it is now a nickname for "the Korean film industry" itself — just as Hollywood is both a place name and a byword for an industry.

① What Korean Cinema Is — Starting from the Word 'Chungmuro'

A word foreigners inevitably meet when reading about Korean film is Chungmuro (충무로, 忠武路, Chungmuro). Originally the name of a street in Jung-gu, Seoul, it became, as film production companies and actors' offices clustered there, a byword for "the Korean film world" as a whole. Phrases like "Chungmuro's next-generation directors" all mean Korean cinema as an industry. It's exactly the same usage as Hollywood being a neighborhood in LA while standing for the entire American film industry.

The identity of Korean cinema, in a phrase, is the fusion of "genre entertainment + social message." It carries Hollywood-grade entertainment value while facing head-on the inner flesh of Korean society — class inequality, state violence, family. Parasite (기생충) skewering the class problem while moving across comedy, thriller, and drama is the prime example. This "coexistence of entertainment and critique" is the core reason foreign critics are wild about Korean film.

② History — From an Era of Censorship to a Renaissance

Korean cinema was not always free. If anything, it was the opposite.

  • The era of censorship (~1980s): Under military regimes, Korean film was bound by heavy gyeomyeol (검열, censorship). Political and social critique — let alone freedom of expression — was restricted, and only a handful of government-licensed production companies existed.
  • The turning point toward liberalization (1986~1987): The 1986 revision of the Film Act (영화법 개정) let anyone establish a film company, and after democratization in 1987 (민주화), films that seriously tackled once-taboo social issues began to appear.
  • The renaissance's opening shot, Shiri (쉬리, 1999): Director Kang Je-gyu's (강제규) Shiri (쉬리, 1999) was Korea's first full-scale blockbuster, drawing 6 million admissions nationwide and breaking the formula that "only Hollywood tentpoles are hits." Afterward, large-scale capital flowed into Chungmuro (충무로), the number of productions and budgets surged, and Korean cinema entered a renaissance where quality and quantity exploded together. People often say "Korean film is divided into before and after Shiri (쉬리)."

On this current, in the 2000s directors like Park Chan-wook (박찬욱), Bong Joon-ho (봉준호), and Lee Chang-dong (이창동) broke into the world's festivals. In other words, a three-step leap — freedom (expression) → capital (renaissance) → auteur (individuality) — built today's Korean cinema.

③ Bong Joon-ho and Parasite (기생충) — The Peak of Globalization

The symbol of Korean cinema's globalization is, without question, director Bong Joon-ho's (봉준호) Parasite (기생충, 2019). Its achievement was precisely this.

  • 2019, the 72nd Cannes Film Festival — Palme d'Or (top prize). The jury was unanimous, and it was the first-ever win for a Korean film.
  • 2020, the 92nd Academy Awards — a 4-win sweep. It took Best Picture, Best Director, Best Original Screenplay, and Best International Feature. It was the first time in history a non-English-language film won the Oscar for Best Picture, and it was also the first time a Korean film won at the Academy Awards.

Parasite (기생충) drew the universal theme of class through the clash of a poor family in a semi-basement and a rich household on a hill, rendered in Korean detail. The verdict that followed: "the most Korean story turned out to be the most universal." Bong Joon-ho (봉준호) had already been proven as "a director who twists genre" with The Host (괴물, 2006), which folded social critique into a monster movie, and Memories of Murder (살인의 추억, 2003), about an unsolved case. Parasite (기생충) was the crowning piece of that accumulation.

If Parasite (기생충) is the peak, that mountain range has many summits. The real strength of Korean cinema lies in having multiple masters of strong authorship.

In particular, Park Chan-wook (박찬욱) raised Oldboy (올드보이) to the ranks of a global cult with the aesthetics of intense visuals, revenge, and desire, while Lee Chang-dong (이창동) commands the deep respect of critics with poetic, weighty human dramas. That several masters of utterly distinct styles exist at the same time is the depth of a Korean cinema that doesn't rely on any single star.

⑤ The 'cheonman yeonghwa (천만 영화)' Phenomenon — A Film Seen by 1 in 5 Koreans

What foreigners find most curious is the cheonman yeonghwa (천만 영화, 10-million-ticket movie). It's the term for a film that has surpassed 10 million (1,000만) admissions — and since Korea's population is about 51 million (5,100만), it means one in five citizens saw the same film in a theater. It's a Korea-specific "the whole nation watches together" box-office phenomenon, rarely seen in the U.S. or Europe.

  • Representative 10-million films (admissions, verification recommended): Roaring Currents (명량) about 17.61 million (1,761만, 2014) · Extreme Job (극한직업) about 16.26 million (1,626만, 2019) · Along with the Gods: The Two Worlds (신과함께-죄와 벌) about 14.42 million (1,442만, 2017) · Ode to My Father (국제시장) about 14.26 million (1,426만, 2014), and others.
  • In all-time box-office history, 10-million films already number in the dozens, and only a tiny few have crossed 15 million. (Rankings and figures update over time — checking the official KOFIC (영화진흥위원회) tally is recommended.)
  • Why it's possible: it's the result of a thick audience base + a dense multiplex network + a "you have to see the talked-about one" word-of-mouth culture locking together. Going to the cinema with family and friends during holidays and vacations is also a long-standing rhythm of life.

> Tip for foreigners: when a Korean says "this film passed 10 million," take it not as mere box-office success but with the nuance of "a film of social-event scale that the entire nation watched."

⑥ Cinema Culture — Multiplexes, Booking Apps, BIFF

The "way of watching" film is distinctive in Korea, too.

  • The big 3 multiplexes: Korean cinemas are split among three large chains — CGV · Lotte Cinema (롯데시네마) · Megabox (메가박스) (by screen count CGV is the largest, with Lotte and Megabox following — market share and merger trends should be verified). Multiplexes are common inside downtowns and shopping malls, so shopping, dining, and a movie flow together in one building.
  • Booking-app culture: choosing seats in advance via online and app reservation is the standard. Seats for popular titles and weekends fill up fast, so booking ahead is the safe move.
  • Popcorn and premium screens: popcorn and drinks are a basic course, and premium-screen technologies like 4DX and ScreenX are advanced enough that Korean companies export them worldwide.
  • Busan International Film Festival (BIFF, 부산국제영화제): launched in 1996, it is Asia's flagship international film festival, recognized for its standing alongside Cannes, Venice, and Toronto. It is held every autumn in Busan.

In short, Korea has a rare film ecosystem in which both everyday viewing (multiplexes) and the stage for art and international cinema (BIFF) are solidly in place.

⑦ How Foreigners Can Enjoy Korean Cinema

> Prices and screening info vary by time and branch, so checking before visiting or booking is recommended.

  • Entry titles: watching in the order Parasite (기생충, class · black comedy) → Oldboy (올드보이, thriller · cult) → Memories of Murder (살인의 추억, crime drama) → Burning (버닝, art · mystery) gives you the spectrum of Korean cinema.
  • Watching in theaters: Korean films have limited English-subtitle (ENG SUB) showtimes depending on the branch and title, so confirm when booking. English-language foreign films get Korean subtitles, so you can enjoy them even without reading. Book through the CGV · Lotte Cinema (롯데시네마) · Megabox (메가박스) apps or on-site kiosks (assigned seating — book popular titles early).
  • Timing tip: if you're in Korea in autumn, a visit to Busan during the Busan International Film Festival (BIFF, 부산국제영화제) period makes for a special experience (check official channels for the lineup and tickets).
  • Etiquette: phones and chatter during a screening are bad manners. Some Korean films have a post-credits "cookie" scene, so many viewers keep their seats to the very end.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • Do Korean films have English subtitles? — Some showtimes provide English subtitles (ENG SUB), but not all, so confirming when you book is essential. On OTT (Netflix, etc.), English-subtitled Korean films are far more widely available.
  • Besides Parasite, what should I watch? — Oldboy (올드보이, Park Chan-wook / 박찬욱) · Memories of Murder (살인의 추억) and The Host (괴물, Bong Joon-ho / 봉준호) · Secret Sunshine (밀양) and Burning (버닝, Lee Chang-dong / 이창동) are master-class entry titles. For a lighter hit, the 10-million film Extreme Job (극한직업, comedy).
  • What exactly is a 'cheonman yeonghwa (천만 영화)'? — A film that has passed 10 million admissions. At one-fifth the scale of Korea's population, it's regarded as a social phenomenon on the level of "a film the whole nation has seen."
  • Is Chungmuro the place where films are shot? — No. It's a nickname meaning "the Korean film industry" (the same usage as Hollywood), not an actual filming location.
  • Why is it all so dark and intense? — It's the fusion of a tradition of facing social critique head-on after being freed from censorship + genre boldness. This "entertainment + critique" is why the world's critics rate Korean cinema so highly.
