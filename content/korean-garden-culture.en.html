Korean garden culture — Chagyeong, a garden borrowing from nature (Changdeokgung Palace Garden, Soswaewon)

Borrowing of scenery · Natural · Artificial minimum · Asymmetry — Explainer of Korean garden culture for foreigners. Not “How pretty is it?” but “Why do Korean gardens look empty, and why is that on purpose?”

Foreigners who first enter Changdeokgung Palace's back garden or Soswaewon often look sideways. "You said it's a garden... It's just a forest and a pond, right?" The gravel is not combed like a knife like the garden in Kyoto, Japan, nor is it dense with strangely shaped rocks and pavilions like the garden in Suzhou, China. However, ‘emptiness’ is the core of Korean gardens. Korean gardens do not conquer nature and build new ones, but choose to leave the existing mountains, water, and rocks as they are and allow people to dwell in them.

This article is not about “which garden is pretty” (→ location guide), but explains what Korean gardens pursued and why they look like that, and how they differ from neighboring Japanese and Chinese gardens. There is one key word — borrowing scenery, which is the idea of ​​‘borrowing scenery’. Korean culture explainer series 73.

⚠️ 3 things foreigners often misunderstand

1. "Because it's empty, it must have been under-cultivated / It must be unfinished." ❌ — The emptiness of a Korean garden is not laziness or incompleteness, but intention. The goal was to minimize artifacts and bring nature to the fore. A pavilion and a pond were only placed to “enable people to enjoy nature better” and do not show off.

2. "Aren't Korean, Chinese, and Japanese gardens all similar oriental gardens?" ❌ — The three have distinctly different philosophies and appearances. China recreates it in a grand way, Japan reduces and abstracts nature and elaborates it, and Korea stays in the nature that exists with as little effort as possible. Although it has the same East Asian roots, the conclusion is different.

3. "Changdeokgung Palace Garden = Secret Garden (Flower Garden)" ❌ — It is easy to imagine a gorgeous flower garden due to the English nickname 'Secret Garden', but the garden garden is a natural garden consisting of a forest, hill, pond, and pavilion. ‘Secret garden’ and ‘Geumwon (禁苑)’ mean ‘a garden that no one can enter (for the royal family only)’ and not a flower garden.

① Korean gardens are about ‘introducing’ nature, not ‘building’ it.

If Western gardens rearrange nature geometrically (symmetrical flower beds in Versailles) and Chinese gardens recreate and compress nature in one place, the starting point of Korean gardens is the exact opposite. Do not disturb existing nature as much as possible.

Rather than leveling flat land to create a new view, a garden-builder looking for a good site sought out a spot where water flowed, rocks stood, and mountains wrapped around, and set a house and a pavilion gently down inside it. If the site allowed for a pond, they dug one, planted lotuses, and built a pavilion beside it to look at; if a stream ran through, they built a pavilion beside it to listen to the sound of the water. The key verb isn't 'to build' but 'to watch, to enjoy.' That's why three things always show up in a Korean garden.

  • A pond: a round island inside a square pond (cheonwonjibang, 천원지방, 天圓地方, 'heaven is round, earth is square') — a miniature of the East Asian cosmology.
  • A nujeong (누정, pavilion): an open building with no walls, just pillars and a roof. Open on every side, it lets the outside scenery straight in. Usually square, hexagonal, or octagonal in shape.
  • Flowing water, a mountain stream: not an artificial fountain, but natural stream water drawn in or left running beside the garden as it is.

This emptiness and restraint might look 'boring' to a foreign eye, but for Koreans it's an expression of the attitude that nature is the protagonist and people are the guest.

② ★ Chagyeong (借景) — The Core Philosophy of a Garden That Borrows Scenery

If you had to explain the Korean garden with a single concept, it would be chagyeong (차경). Literally, it means 'to borrow a view' — instead of building everything inside the garden wall, the idea is to pull the mountains, sky, river, and distant peaks beyond the wall into the garden as part of it.

The principle is simple yet deep. Sit in a pavilion and look at the mountain outside through the 'frame' formed by pillars, eaves, windows, and doors — and that mountain becomes your garden's scenery in that moment, even though it isn't your land. There's no need to cut the mountain down and move it into your yard — you simply 'borrow' it when you want to see it. This is how Korean scholars appreciated outside nature through the artificial frame of a window or door. There's a clear worldview behind this idea.

  • People are part of nature — Taoism's 'muwi-jayeon (무위자연),' effortless naturalness, and Confucianism's 'cheonin-hapil (천인합일),' the unity of heaven and humanity. Humans don't own or conquer nature — they dissolve into it.
  • So there's no reason to copy a beautiful view and claim it as your own. Nature is already perfect; all a person needs to do is prepare a good spot to look at it from — a pavilion, a window.
  • A garden wasn't something to be owned — it was a stage for contemplation, poetry, and study. A scholar would sit in the pavilion, look at nature, compose poems, and cultivate the mind.

Once you know about chagyeong, the 'emptiness' of a Korean garden looks different. That empty space is left empty on purpose, so the outside view can come in. Where Japanese and Chinese gardens fill everything inside the wall to create a 'complete world,' the Korean garden lowered its walls and opened its sightlines toward an open world connected to the outside. (Japan has the same concept, called 'shakkei' — the same characters, 借景 — but where Korea barely touches the mountain terrain, Japan tends to precisely groom even the borrowed view and fit it into the composition.)

③ Changdeokgung Palace's Huwon (Biwon) — a King's Garden, Designed to Follow Nature

The place that best shows the aesthetic of chagyeong and following nature is Changdeokgung Palace's Huwon (후원, 後苑, 'rear garden'). It's the garden behind the palace once used by the Joseon royal family, made up of several zones including Buyongji, Aeryeonji, Gwallamji, and Okryucheon. In 1997, all of Changdeokgung Palace was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and its core value is precisely a garden design that preserved the natural terrain as it was.

  • What the names mean: 'Huwon' means the garden to the north/rear of the palace; 'geumwon' (금원, 禁苑) means a royal-only garden that no one else may enter. The name 'Biwon' (비원, 秘苑, 'Secret Garden') came into use from the early 1900s — that's where the English nickname 'Secret Garden' comes from.
  • A layout that doesn't fight nature: the real virtue of Huwon lies in leaving the hills, valleys, forest, and waterways uncut and unmoved, and setting buildings among them. Spring water welling up from the ground was left to fill Aeryeonji and Buyongji and overflow naturally — even the waterways were made to follow nature's own flow. This is regarded as an embodiment of cheonin-hapil, 'heaven and humanity becoming one.'
  • Buyongji and Buyongjeong: a cheonwonjibang composition with a round island at the center of a square pond (Buyongji). Sit in the pavilion beside it, Buyongjeong, and the pond, forest, and sky all fall into a single view — a textbook scene of chagyeong.
  • Okryucheon: deep within Huwon, a spot where a small channel was cut into the rock, and kings and officials composed poetry beside the running water. It reveals a distinctly Korean sensibility — enjoying a small, natural stream rather than a grand artificial waterfall.

Huwon defies the expectation that "it's a king's garden, so it must be extravagant." The fact that the royal family chose a garden that settled into nature with the greatest humility, instead of a grand structure flaunting power, is the essence of the Korean view of gardens.

④ Soswaewon and the Byeolseo Garden — Where a Scholar Retreated Into Nature

If palace gardens belonged to the royal family, the other pillar of Korean garden culture is the scholar's byeolseo-jeongwon (별서정원, 別墅庭園). 'Byeolseo' refers to a villa-like retreat set up in nature after leaving government office. The best-known example is Soswaewon (소쇄원, 瀟灑園) in Damyang, South Jeolla Province.

  • How it came to be: the mid-Joseon scholar Yang San-bo (양산보, 梁山甫, 1503–1557) built this garden after returning to his hometown and giving up on an official career, following the persecution of his teacher Jo Gwang-jo in the Gimyo Purge. Soswaewon is, in short, a space shaped by a scholar's heart turning away from the world to settle into nature.
  • What the name means: 'soswae' (소쇄, 瀟灑) means 'clear and pure.' It carries a vow to leave behind the murk of politics and live a clean life.
  • A design centered on the valley: Soswaewon isn't a garden carved from flat land — it kept the natural valley stream right at its center and arranged its buildings to follow that flow. The key was keeping the waterway alive rather than damming or redirecting it.
  • Gwangpunggak (光風閣) and Jewoldang (齊月堂): Gwangpunggak, the pavilion beside the valley where guests were received, means "the clear breeze that blows after the rain clears"; Jewoldang, where the owner himself stayed, means "the clear moon rising in a sky cleared of rain." Both names come from a Song-dynasty scholar's praise of a person of pure character as 'gwangpung-jewol' (光風霽月) — the building names themselves are a vow toward a pure character.

Soswaewon is especially interesting to foreigners. It has no flashy sights, but a scholar's philosophy of 'how to live within nature' is etched into every corner — a very Korean idea, that a garden is a direct reflection of a person's heart.

⑤ Nujeong and Ponds — The Small Devices That Build a Korean Garden

Once you know the 'grammar' of a Korean garden, any garden becomes fun to read. There are two recurring core devices.

Nujeong (누정, 樓亭) — a pavilion without walls

A nujeong is the heart of a Korean garden. With almost no walls — a building left open on every side by just its pillars and roof — the outside view flows in unobstructed once you sit inside. It's the device that puts chagyeong into practice. In a pavilion, a person doesn't 'own' nature; they 'welcome' it. Across East Asia — Korea, China, Vietnam, and elsewhere — these were favorite structures to build in mountains and gardens, most often square, hexagonal, or octagonal in shape.

A pond — a vessel holding heaven and earth

A pond isn't just a simple puddle of water either. In the cheonwonjibang (天圓地方) composition — a square pond with a round island — it embodies the East Asian cosmology that 'heaven is round and earth is square.' Lotuses were commonly planted in the pond, with a pavilion set beside it, so people could watch the sky, the moon, and the lotus flowers reflected in the water (Changdeokgung's Buyongji is the classic example).

Put the two together and the archetypal Korean garden is complete — a spot where you sit in an open pavilion beside the water and look at nature through the window and eaves. There, a person becomes not a conqueror of nature but a guest appreciating it.

⑥ ★★ Korean, Chinese, and Japanese Gardens — Same Roots, Different Conclusions

Even within the same East Asian garden tradition, Korea, China, and Japan take distinctly different attitudes toward nature. To put it in a metaphor garden scholars like to use: if Korea transplants nature at 1 to 1, China handles it at 1 to 10 (bigger), and Japan at 1 to 1/10 (smaller).

Category 🇨🇳 Chinese Garden 🇯🇵 Japanese Garden 🇰🇷 Korean Garden
Attitude Nature recreated and compressed Nature miniaturized and abstracted Nature brought in as-is
Sense of scale Grand (too much to take in at a glance) Precise and small True to nature's own scale
Artificial beauty Dense with dramatic rocks and pavilions, flaunting artifice Gravel, moss, and stone arranged with precision Minimal artifice, no trace of a human hand
Representative style Suzhou gardens (dramatic rocks, covered corridors) Karesansui (枯山水, dry landscape) — abstracting scenery with sand and stone, no water Huwon, byeolseo gardens, nujeong
Feeling Overwhelming, grand Meditative, precise (a beautifully groomed miniature) Empty, unadorned, open
  • The Chinese garden recreates mountains, caves, waterfalls, and dramatic rocks inside the garden as if they were real. The virtue is to overwhelm with scale, so much that you can't take it all in at a glance. The idea is to 'bring all of nature inside myself.'
  • The Japanese garden miniaturizes and abstracts nature, then refines it with precision. The representative style is karesansui (고산수, 枯山水, 'dry landscape') — a Zen garden that uses not a single drop of water, representing waves with raked patterns in sand and islands or mountains with stones. The gravel is raked daily to maintain a perfect pattern. It's a highly controlled, 'beautifully groomed condensation of nature.'
  • The Korean garden stands at the opposite extreme. It barely touches the nature that's already there. No mountains get moved, no gravel gets raked. All a person does is gently place one pavilion, one pond, to create a better spot to watch nature from. So the Korean garden isn't as precise as Japan's, nor as grand as China's — but the line between nature and artifice is the blurriest of the three.

In one sentence: China brings nature in, Japan grooms nature, Korea settles into nature. It's fascinating that all three countries started from the same Taoist, Buddhist, and Confucian roots, yet arrived at opposite conclusions on how much to control nature.

⑦ A Practical Guide to Appreciating the Garden as a Foreigner — See It This Way and Korean Gardens Come Into Focus

A Korean garden is boring if you're looking for a 'pretty photo spot.' Change how you look, and it becomes something else entirely.

  • Sit inside a pavilion and look for the 'frame': the essence of a Korean garden opens up once you sit on a pavilion's wooden floor. The mountain, pond, and sky seen through the frame made by its pillars, eaves, and windows — that's chagyeong, right there. Don't just pass through standing up — sit down and look outward.
  • Read the 'emptiness' as intention, not a flaw: don't be disappointed that there's no packed flowerbed. That emptiness is space left open on purpose — for the outside view to come in, and for the mind to rest.
  • Take in the sound of water, the wind, and the season together: a Korean garden isn't a purely visual art. The sound of the stream, the wind passing through the pavilion, and seasonal change — fresh spring green, autumn foliage — are all part of the garden. (→ see #1194 for how the seasons transform the scenery.)
  • Visiting tip: Changdeokgung's Huwon can only be entered on a timed guided tour (reservation recommended; garden admission is a separate fee) to protect the site — check Changdeokgung's official information before you go. Soswaewon is in Damyang, far from Seoul, but worth the trip if you want to see a 'scholar's garden.'
  • The best seasons: chagyeong shines brightest in spring (April–May), with fresh green leaves, and autumn (October–November), with colored foliage — because that's when the borrowed outside view itself is at its most beautiful.

One practical line: at a Korean garden, don't ask "what has been built here?" — ask "what do I see if I sit here?" That's how you enjoy chagyeong.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) for Foreigners

  • Why do Korean gardens look 'less manicured' than Japanese gardens? — On purpose. Where the Japanese garden miniaturizes and abstracts nature into something precisely groomed, the Korean garden treats touching nature as little as possible as a virtue. It's not 'less manicured' — it's the result of aiming for 'nature as it is.'
  • What exactly is borrowed scenery? — It means ‘borrowing scenery.’ Instead of building everything inside the garden wall, the idea is to bring in the mountains and sky beyond the wall and enjoy them as part of the garden. When I sit in the pavilion and look at the mountain outside through the window or eaves, the mountain becomes the scenery of my garden.
  • Can I just enter the back garden of Changdeokgung Palace? — For preservation purposes, admission is usually limited to guided tours and limited number of times (admission to the garden is separate from palace entrance and a separate fee and reservation is recommended). Before visiting, be sure to check the official Changdeokgung Palace information for times and reservation methods.
  • Where is Soswaewon and why is it famous? — It is Byeolseo Garden from the Joseon Dynasty in Damyang, Jeollanam-do. It was built by Yang Sanbo, a scholar, in seclusion in nature after his death, and it represents the ‘Garden of Scholars’ with design that takes advantage of the valley and pavilions such as Gwangpungak and Jewoldang.
  • Korean, Chinese, and Japanese gardens, how can we distinguish them in one line? — China brings nature (grandeur, reproduction), Japan refines nature (miniaturization, elaborateness, and high landscapes), and Korea dwells in nature (as is, emptiness, borrowed landscape).
