Korean Garden Culture — Chagyeong, the Art of "Borrowing" Nature (Changdeokgung Secret Garden & Soswaewon)

> chagyeong (차경, borrowed scenery) · nature as-is · minimal artifice · asymmetry — a Korean garden culture explainer for foreigners. Not "which one is pretty," but "why do Korean gardens look empty, and why is that on purpose?"

A foreigner stepping into Changdeokgung Secret Garden or Soswaewon (소쇄원) for the first time often tilts their head. "You called this a garden... isn't it just a forest and a pond?" It has neither the razor-combed gravel of a Kyoto garden in Japan nor the dense crags and pavilions of a Suzhou garden in China. Yet that very "emptiness" is the heart of the Korean garden. A Korean garden was not built by conquering nature and constructing something new; it chose to leave the existing mountains, water, and rocks as they are and let people gently settle inside them.

This article is not about "which garden is pretty" (→ that would be a location guide). It unpacks what the Korean garden pursued and why it looks the way it does, and how it differs from its neighbors, the Japanese and Chinese gardens. There is one word that holds the key — chagyeong (차경, borrowed scenery), the idea of "borrowing the scenery." Korean culture explainer series, Part 73.

⚠️ 3 things foreigners often misunderstand

1. "It's empty, so it must be poorly kept / unfinished" ❌ — the emptiness of a Korean garden is intention, not laziness or incompleteness. Minimizing artificial objects and placing nature front and center was the goal. Even a single pavilion or a single pond was set there only "to let you enjoy nature better," never to show itself off.

2. "Aren't Korean, Chinese, and Japanese gardens all just similar Asian gardens?" ❌ — the three differ clearly in both philosophy and appearance. China reproduces nature grandly, Japan miniaturizes and abstracts nature into refined precision, and Korea touches as little as possible to settle into the nature that is already there. Same East Asian roots, different conclusions.

3. "Changdeokgung huwon = a Secret Garden (flower garden)" ❌ — the English nickname "Secret Garden" makes it easy to imagine a showy flower bed, but the huwon (후원, rear garden) is a natural-style garden of forest, hills, ponds, and pavilions. Biwon (비원, secret garden) and geumwon (금원, forbidden garden) mean "a garden no one may enter (reserved for royalty)," not "a flower garden."

① What a Korean garden is — not "building" nature but "inviting it in"

If a Western garden rearranges nature into geometry (the symmetrical flower beds of Versailles) and a Chinese garden reproduces and compresses nature into one place, the Korean garden's starting point is the opposite. Touch the nature that already exists as little as possible.

When choosing a good site, the garden-maker looked less for flat ground to bulldoze into a new view than for a spot where water flows, rocks sit, and mountains wrap around — then gently set a house and a pavilion there. If they could afford to dig a pond, they dug one, planted lotus, built a pavilion beside it, and gazed; if a stream flowed, they built a pavilion beside it and listened to the sound of water. The key verb was not "to make" but "to behold, to enjoy." That is why three things always appear in a Korean garden.

  • A pond: a round island inside a square pond (cheonwon-jibang 천원지방, "round heaven, square earth") — a miniature of the East Asian cosmology.
  • A nujeong (누정, pavilion): an open building of only pillars and a roof, no walls. Open on all sides, it lets the outside scenery flow straight in. Usually square, hexagonal, or octagonal in shape.
  • Flowing water / a mountain stream: not an artificial fountain but natural valley water drawn in as-is or kept close by.

This emptiness and restraint may look "dull" to a foreigner's eye, but to Koreans it expresses the attitude that nature is the protagonist and the human is the guest.

② ★ Chagyeong (차경, borrowed scenery) — the core philosophy of "borrowing" the view

If you had to explain a Korean garden with a single concept, it would be chagyeong (차경, borrowed scenery). Literally "to borrow the scenery" — instead of building everything inside the garden walls, it is the idea of drawing the mountains, sky, river, and distant peaks beyond the wall into the garden as part of it.

The principle is simple yet deep. Sit in a pavilion and look past the "frame" made by pillars, eaves, windows, and doors at the mountain outside, and that mountain — though it is not your land — becomes the scenery of your garden in that moment. There is no need to carve up a mountain and move it into the yard; you can just "borrow" the view when you want it. Korean scholars thus appreciated the outside nature through the artificial frame of windows and doors. Behind this idea lies a clear worldview.

  • Humans are a part of nature (Daoism's muwi-jayeon 무위자연, "nature without contrivance" · Confucianism's cheonin-habil 천인합일, oneness of heaven and human). Humans do not own or conquer nature but dissolve into it.
  • So there is no reason to copy a beautiful scene and rebuild it as one's own. Nature is already perfect, so a person need only prepare a good seat from which to behold it (a pavilion, a window).
  • The garden was not an object of ownership but a stage for contemplation, poetry, and study. A scholar sat in a pavilion, watched nature, composed poems, and cultivated the mind.

Once you know chagyeong, the "emptiness" of a Korean garden looks different. That empty space is a spot left open for the outside scenery to enter. Where Japanese and Chinese gardens fill their walls to make a "completed world," the Korean garden lowered its walls and opened the sightline to aim at an open world connected to the outside. (Japan, too, has the same concept — chagyeong, called shakkei — but where Korea barely touches the mountain forms, Japan tends to refine even the borrowed view precisely and fit it into the composition.)

③ Changdeokgung huwon (Biwon) — a king's garden designed to follow nature

The place that best shows the aesthetic of chagyeong and yielding to nature is the Changdeokgung huwon (후원, rear garden). The garden behind the palace used by the Joseon royal family, it consists of several zones such as Buyongji (부용지) · Aeryeonji (애련지) · Gwallamji (관람지) · Ongnyucheon (옥류천). In 1997 all of Changdeokgung was inscribed as a UNESCO World Cultural Heritage site, and its core value is precisely a garden design that preserves the natural terrain as-is.

  • Meaning of the name: huwon (후원) means the garden to the north/rear of the palace; geumwon (금원, forbidden garden) means a garden reserved for royalty that no one may enter. The name biwon (비원, Secret Garden) came into use from the early 1900s. The English nickname "Secret Garden" comes from this.
  • A layout that does not defy nature: the true virtue of the huwon lies in leaving the hills, valleys, forests, and waterways uncarved and unmoved and placing buildings among them. Spring water welling up from underground fills Aeryeonji and Buyongji and overflows, so that even the waterways follow nature's flow. This is praised as an embodiment of cheonin-habil (천인합일, the oneness of heaven and human).
  • Buyongji and Buyongjeong: a cheonwon-jibang (천원지방) composition with a round island in the middle of a square pond (Buyongji 부용지). Sit in the pavilion beside it, Buyongjeong (부용정), and pond, forest, and sky enter a single frame — a textbook scene of chagyeong.
  • Ongnyucheon (옥류천): deep in the huwon, a spot where a small channel was cut into the rock and the king and his courtiers composed poems beside the flowing water. Instead of a huge artificial waterfall, the Korean taste for a small, natural mountain stream shows itself here.

The huwon betrays the expectation that "a king's garden must be lavish." That the royal family chose a garden settling most humbly into nature rather than colossal structures flaunting power is the essence of the Korean view of gardens.

④ Soswaewon and the byeolseo-jeongwon — a garden where a scholar hid in nature

If the palace garden belonged to royalty, the other axis of Korean garden culture is the scholars' byeolseo-jeongwon (별서정원, scholar's retreat garden). Byeolseo (별서) means a villa-like dwelling set up in nature after leaving office. Its representative is Soswaewon (소쇄원) in Damyang, South Jeolla Province.

  • Background of its creation: the mid-Joseon scholar Yang San-bo (양산보, 1503–1557), after his teacher Jo Gwang-jo fell victim to the Gimyo Literati Purge (gimyo-sahwa 기묘사화), gave up his ambition for office, returned to his hometown, and built this garden. So Soswaewon is a space shaped by a scholar's wish to turn his back on the world and settle into nature.
  • Meaning of the name: soswae (소쇄) means "clear and clean." It holds the resolve to leave the murk of politics and live purely.
  • A design centered on the valley: Soswaewon is not a garden made by bulldozing flat land; it placed the natural valley stream right at its center and arranged the buildings along its flow. Keeping the watercourse alive without blocking or diverting it is the heart of it.
  • Gwangpunggak (광풍각) and Jewoldang (제월당): the pavilion beside the valley, Gwangpunggak (광풍각), was the guest hall, meaning "the clear breeze that blows after the rain clears," and Jewoldang (제월당), where the owner stayed, means "the clear moon in a rain-cleared sky." Both names come from a Song-dynasty scholar's praise of a person of pure character as gwangpung-jewol (광풍제월, "clear breeze and bright moon") — the building names themselves are a vow toward a clear character.

Soswaewon is especially interesting to foreigners. It has no flashy spectacle, but a scholar's view of life — "how should one live within nature?" — is carved into every corner. The very Korean idea that the garden is the person's own mind.

⑤ Nujeong and the pond — the small devices that make up a Korean garden

Once you know the "grammar" of a Korean garden, reading any garden becomes a pleasure. Two core devices recur.

Nujeong (누정, an open pavilion) — a wall-less pavilion

The nujeong is the heart of a Korean garden. With almost no walls, it is a building that opens on all four sides with only pillars and a roof, so when you sit inside, the outside scenery enters unobstructed — it is the very device that realizes chagyeong. In a pavilion a person does not "own" nature but "receives" it. Across East Asia — Korea, China, Vietnam — people loved to raise them in mountains and gardens, and their shapes are often square, hexagonal, or octagonal.

The pond — a vessel holding heaven and earth

A pond, too, is not a simple puddle. With its square pond + round island cheonwon-jibang (천원지방) composition, it holds the East Asian cosmology that "heaven is round and earth is square." Lotus was often planted in the pond and a pavilion set beside it, to behold the sky, moon, and lotus reflected on the water (Changdeokgung's Buyongji 부용지 is the prime example).

When these two combine, the archetype of the Korean garden is complete — a seat in an open pavilion beside the water, beholding nature past the window and eaves. There, a person becomes not a conqueror who has subdued nature but a guest who appreciates it.

⑥ ★★ Korean, Chinese, and Japanese gardens — same roots, different conclusions

Even within the same East Asian garden tradition, Korea, China, and Japan differ clearly in their attitude toward nature. A metaphor garden scholars love sums it up like this — if Korea moves nature at 1:1, China handles it at 1:10 (larger), and Japan at 1:1/10 (smaller).

Category 🇨🇳 Chinese garden 🇯🇵 Japanese garden 🇰🇷 Korean garden
Attitude reproduce and compress nature miniaturize and abstract nature bring nature in as-is
Sense of scale grand (can't take it in at a glance) precise, small natural size as-is
Artifice dense crags and pavilions, flaunting artifice gravel, moss, and stone arranged precisely minimal artifice, no trace of a human hand
Signature style Suzhou garden (crags, corridors) karesansui (枯山水) — abstracting scenery with sand and stone, without water huwon, byeolseo-jeongwon, nujeong
Feeling overwhelming, grand meditative, precise (a well-trimmed miniature) empty, plain, open
  • The Chinese garden reproduces mountains, caves, waterfalls, and crags inside the garden as if they were real. Its virtue is to overwhelm by scale, so much that you can't see it all at a glance. The idea is "to bring all of nature inside me."
  • The Japanese garden miniaturizes and abstracts nature and refines it precisely. Its representative is karesansui (枯山水, karesansui) — a Zen garden that uses not a single drop of water and expresses waves with combed patterns in sand and islands and mountains with stones. The gravel is combed daily to keep a perfect pattern. It is a highly controlled "well-trimmed compression of nature."
  • The Korean garden stands at the opposite end. It barely touches the nature that is there. It neither moves a mountain nor combs the gravel. All a person does is set a single pavilion or a single pond to make a seat from which to behold nature better. So a Korean garden is neither as precise as Japan's nor as grand as China's, but the boundary between nature and artifice is blurriest of all.

In one sentence: China brings nature in, Japan refines nature, and Korea settles into nature. It is fascinating that the three nations started from the same Daoist, Buddhist, and Confucian roots yet reached opposite conclusions on how much to control nature.

⑦ Garden-viewing in practice for foreigners — see it this way and the Korean garden appears

Seen with eyes hunting for a "pretty photo spot," a Korean garden is dull. Change the way you look and it comes across completely differently.

  • Sit inside the pavilion and find the "frame": the essence of a Korean garden opens when you sit on a pavilion floor. The mountain, pond, and sky seen past the frame made by pillars, eaves, and windows — that is chagyeong. Don't pass by standing; sit and behold the outside.
  • Read the "emptiness" as intention, not defect: don't be disappointed that there's no packed flower bed. That emptiness is a spot left open for the outside scenery to enter, and for the mind to rest.
  • Appreciate the water sound, wind, and seasons together: a Korean garden is not an art of sight alone. The sound of the valley stream, the wind passing through the pavilion, and seasonal change like spring's fresh greenery and autumn's foliage are part of the garden. (→ For how the seasons change the scenery, see #1194.)
  • Visiting tip: Changdeokgung huwon can be entered only by timed guided tour (reservation recommended; separate garden admission fee) for preservation — check the official Changdeokgung guidance before visiting. Soswaewon is in Damyang, far from Seoul, but is worth a trip if you want to see a "scholar's garden."
  • Best season: a Korean garden's chagyeong shines most in the fresh greenery of spring (April–May) and the foliage of autumn (October–November), because the borrowed outside scenery itself is at its most beautiful then.

> One line for practice: at a Korean garden, ask not "what has been built" but "what can I see if I sit here." That is how to enjoy chagyeong.

❓ Frequently asked questions from foreigners (FAQ)

  • Why does a Korean garden look "less tidy" than a Japanese garden? — on purpose. Where the Japanese garden miniaturizes and abstracts nature into precise refinement, the Korean garden makes a virtue of touching nature as little as possible. It is not "less tidy" but the result of aiming for "nature as-is."
  • What exactly is chagyeong (차경, borrowed scenery)? — it means "to borrow the scenery." Instead of building everything inside the garden walls, it is the idea of drawing the mountains and sky beyond the wall into the garden as part of it and appreciating them. Sit in a pavilion and look past the window and eaves at the mountain outside, and that mountain becomes your garden's scenery.
  • Can you just walk into Changdeokgung huwon? — for preservation, you usually enter on a guided tour that limits numbers and time (garden admission is a separate fee from palace admission; reservation recommended). Be sure to check the times and reservation method in the official Changdeokgung guidance before visiting.
  • Where is Soswaewon and why is it famous? — it is a Joseon-era byeolseo-jeongwon (별서정원, scholar's retreat garden) in Damyang, South Jeolla. The scholar Yang San-bo (양산보) built it while withdrawing from the world into nature, and it represents the "scholar's garden" with its design that keeps the valley as-is and pavilions like Gwangpunggak and Jewoldang.
  • Korean, Chinese, and Japanese gardens in one line? — China brings nature in (grand, reproduced), Japan refines nature (miniaturized, precise, karesansui), and Korea settles into nature (as-is, empty, chagyeong).
