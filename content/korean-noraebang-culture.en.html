Korean Karaoke Culture — Why do Koreans go to ‘karaoke’ when they get together?

Karaoke·Coin karaoke·2nd company dinner·tambourine·heung — Explainer of Korean karaoke culture for foreigners. Not ‘karaoke bar’ but ‘why, what, and how.’

After dinner, someone always says this. "Shall we go to a karaoke room for the second time?" Whether in a K-drama, at a company dinner, or after having drinks with friends — when Korean people get together, they head to karaoke. Foreigners usually translate it as “karaoke,” but Korean karaoke bars are crucially different from Western karaoke bars. Instead of singing alone on stage in front of strangers, we sing for each other in a small private room that only our group enters. The score pops up, the tambourine shakes, and applause breaks out even if you can’t sing. This article is not about “Which karaoke is good?” (→ location), but explains why Koreans gather and express themselves through singing, and how foreigners enjoy it. ⚠️ 3 things foreigners often misunderstand

1. "Karaoke = like a Western karaoke bar" ❌ — Western karaoke is usually sung on the stage of a bar in front of unfamiliar guests. Korean karaoke rooms are private rooms that only our group can enter. The audience is only friends — so even tone-deaf people can sing with ease. In Korean, sing + room = literally ‘singing room’.

2. "Karaoke = an entertainment establishment where people drink alcohol" ❌ — A regular karaoke is not a bar but a clean family/friend space where people only sing along with drinks and snacks (coin karaoke usually does not have alcohol itself). Although it is a second party for late-night company dinners, karaoke itself is a universal entertainment culture enjoyed by people of all ages and even minors. (Places with ‘Danran Bar/Room Salon’ in their names are completely different businesses — be careful of confusion.)

3. "You have to be good at singing to get there" ❌ — The unwritten rule of Korean karaoke is enthusiasm, not skill. Even if you can't sing, if you can brighten the mood, that's the answer. The person who doesn't sing takes the tambourine — it's rather awkward to stay still.

① What is a karaoke room — not an ‘open stage’ but a ‘private room’

A karaoke room can be defined in one word: "A place where you rent a small singing room for yourself by the hour."

  • The room is equipped with a large screen (lyric video), accompaniment player, two microphones, sofa, lighting (disco light), and tambourine.
  • Tens of thousands of songs are organized by number, and you can select and sing a song using the remote control (or a thick book).
  • The key point is that it is a private space. Because it is performed in a closed room with close people, rather than on a stage where one is evaluated in front of strangers like in Western karaoke, the barrier to shame is significantly lower.

This 'private room' structure is the decisive difference that made karaoke a daily social space for Koreans. Now that stage fear is gone, anyone can grab the mic even if they can't sing.

② A regular course for the second part of a company dinner — a place with karaoke

In Korean workplace and gathering culture, the most frequent place where karaoke appears is at a company dinner. A typical company dinner course goes like this.

  • 1st = Restaurant (meat, alcohol and meal) → 2nd = Karaoke (or bar) → 3rd = Cafe, convenience store, chicken, etc.
  • In one survey, 'non-drinking type' (including singing and dancing) ranked first with about 47% as the type of company dinner most preferred by office workers, Karaoke is a representative course for the second round of company dinners.
  • The reason karaoke fits into a company dinner is clear — the awkward up-and-down hierarchy at the dinner table loosens up once everyone is singing and clapping together. When a boss and a new hire sing a duet, the distance between them shrinks for the rest of that day.

Changing: Lately, the MZ generation (MZ세대) often finds a late-night second round with coworkers burdensome, and a strong "first round only" trend has taken hold. So karaoke's center of gravity is gradually shifting from a company-dinner obligation to voluntary fun (with friends, at coin karaoke). → The company-dinner culture itself is covered in depth in #1140.

③ Coin Karaoke — the MZ Generation's Solo Karaoke

A new branch that emerged and took off after the 2010s is coin karaoke (coin noraebang).

  • How it works: You step into a small booth and feed coins or bills in yourself, singing however many songs or minutes you paid for. At around ₩500 per song, or ₩3,000~5,000 for 30 minutes, it's perfect for a quick solo session.
  • Solo culture (honkono): If traditional karaoke is 'for groups,' coin karaoke created a culture of going alone and singing to your heart's content. It's shortened to 'honkono (혼코노)' — solo coin karaoke. With no group to gather and no drinking-table pressure, you can blow off steam on the spur of the moment.
  • Everyday life for the MZ generation: It first targeted teenagers with small 1-2 person booths, but as that generation grew into their 20s, it became everyday fun around college towns and downtown areas. As chains grew, sofas and sound systems improved to near general-karaoke level, so the line between karaoke and coin karaoke is now blurring.

Coin karaoke is also the lowest-barrier experience for foreign tourists — alone or with one friend, for just a few thousand won, in a short burst.

④ Manners and Props — the Unwritten Rules of Tambourine, Score, and Mic

Karaoke rooms have unwritten rules every Korean knows. Learn them and you'll blend right in.

  • The tambourine isn't optional. When it's not your turn to sing, it's polite to keep the mood up with the tambourine and applause. Sitting still and staring at your phone kills the vibe.
  • The score (score feature) — when a song ends, a score from 0 to 100 appears on screen. It's less a real measure of skill than a device for laughing and teasing each other. A 100 draws cheers, and a low score is just as good for a laugh.
  • The mic gets passed around. No one hogs it — taking turns is the default. Queuing up a song you think someone else will enjoy is a nice touch too.
  • Keep one 'mood-lifting song' in your pocket — queue up an upbeat track everyone can sing along to (the so-called 'atmosphere song') and you become the hero of the room.
  • Heung (흥) comes first — singing with energy is more welcome than hitting the right pitch or rhythm. It's not a serious concert, it's a place to play together.

⑤ Why Release It 'Through Song' — the Social Psychology of Jeong, Heung, and Stress Relief

This is what foreigners are most curious about. Why karaoke, of all things? There are reasons that go beyond simple entertainment.

  • A safe valve for releasing emotion — Korean society has a strong culture of restraining emotion. Karaoke is almost the only public outlet where you can shout, cry, and laugh without being judged. Singing is even said to raise endorphins and lower cortisol (the stress hormone) — so when Koreans say "let's go blow off steam at karaoke," there's actually some science behind it.
  • Heung — one of the core emotions in Korean culture is sinmyeong, an exhilarating excitement. Singing and dancing together to build up that heung is, in itself, the Korean way of having fun.
  • Building jeong (정) — eating, drinking, singing, and dancing together layers shared emotion and builds jeong, a bond of affection. Psychology researchers at Seoul National University note that "Koreans share emotion through song and dance, and build bonds by acting together as a group." People who have sung along together at karaoke end up closer than before. → 'Jeong' itself is covered in depth in #1135.
  • A leveling device that breaks down hierarchy — the moment both boss and new hire turn out to be equally tone-deaf, the wall of rank briefly disappears. Karaoke is a space of horizontal equality.

In short, karaoke is a device where stress relief + heung + jeong + relationship leveling all happen for Koreans at once. You don't go there "to sing well" — you go "to release it together" — and that one line is the heart of karaoke culture.

⑥ History — the 'One-and-a-Half-Pyeong Stage' that Began in Busan in 1991

Karaoke is a younger culture than you'd think.

  • In 1991, Korean-style karaoke equipment was first introduced in Busan (in arcade booths in front of Dong-A University), and in May of that same year, 'Hawaii Beach Norae Yeonseupjang (하와이비치 노래연습장)' in Haeundae was recorded as the official first location. In its early days it ran on coins — a booth of about 1.5-1.6 pyeong charging ₩300 per song — essentially the prototype of today's coin karaoke.
  • Backing-track machine makers like TJ Media (TJ미디어) — known then as Taejin Media — and Geumyoung (금영) (brand code KY) emerged in late 1991, and the industry exploded. These two companies still make almost every karaoke machine today — you'll spot the 'TJ' or 'KY' logo in the corner of the screen.

In the 2000s, dedicated internet lines made real-time new-song updates possible, and Korean karaoke became the fastest karaoke system in the world for adding the latest hits. In just 30 years, karaoke has become a national pastime found everywhere across the country.

⑦ Practical Guide for Foreigners — Where, How Much, and How to Sing

Prices and hours are estimates as of 2026 and vary by store — checking ahead of your visit is recommended.

  • Where: Karaoke rooms and coin karaoke cluster in busy districts like Hongdae, Gangnam, Itaewon, and Myeongdong. If you see a lit sign reading noraebang (노래방), you're good to go. Stores in tourist areas like Hongdae are used to serving foreign visitors.
  • Cost: A general karaoke room runs ₩15,000~25,000 per hour (not per person — the more people split it, the cheaper per head). Coin karaoke runs ₩500 per song or ₩3,000~5,000 for 30 minutes. Many places also throw in extra service time (서비스 타임) for free.
  • Find English/Pop Songs: There are thousands of English songs (classics from the 1950s to the latest Billboard) available on almost every machine. Switch to English mode with the 'Korean/English' button on the remote control, then search by song title/singer — English keyboard supported. Of course, K-pop is also abundant.
  • How ​​to sing: If you know the song number, enter number on the remote control → Start. If you don't know it, search 'Title'/'Singer' to find it directly on the screen. If you make a reservation, it will be played in order.
  • Materials/Tips: No special preparation required. It's enough to just think of 2~3 pop/K-pop titles you want to sing in advance. If you are alone, use coin karaoke, or if you are with friends, use regular karaoke room.
  • Manner: When not called, respond with tambourine and applause, pass the microphone around, and look at the score laughing. When you remember that excitement, rather than skill, is welcome, you become part of the room.

❓ Foreigners Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • I really can't sing, can I go? — Rather, it's a welcome. The rule of karaoke is excitement, not skill. Even if you can't sing, if you sing excitedly and shake the tambourine, that's the answer. Because it is a closed private room, there are no strangers.
  • Are there any English songs (pop songs)? — Yes. There are thousands of English songs for almost every machine. Switch to English mode with the ‘Korean/English’ button and search by title/singer. The latest Billboard songs also arrive quickly.
  • Isn’t it weird to go alone? — Not at all. Coin karaoke (Honkono) is a basic culture of going alone. 30 minutes, no burden, for just a few thousand won.
  • Do I need to drink alcohol? — No. A regular karaoke bar only has drinks and snacks, Coin karaoke rooms usually do not have alcohol. A karaoke room is not a bar, but a clean space for singing.
  • What do I do if I run out of time? — Regular karaoke bars often provide 'happy time' (free additional time) when the time is up. For coin karaoke, you just need to put in more coins.
  • Do I need a reservation? — Most coin karaoke rooms are walk-in (you can enter right away). Regular karaoke bars usually have immediate access, but there may be a wait at popular stores on weekend nights.
